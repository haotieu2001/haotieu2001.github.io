Underneath, a data pipeline on AWS is almost always the same shape. Raw files land in S3. A compute engine, Glue for most jobs or EMR when you need a full Spark cluster, reads them, cleans and joins them, and writes the results back to S3 in a query-friendly layout. MWAA (managed Airflow) decides the order and reruns anything that fails; Lambda covers the small event-driven bits. When data arrives continuously instead of in files, Kinesis or Kafka (MSK) carry it, Firehose or Flink land and process it, and the same S3 lake still sits at the end. Then Athena queries the S3 data with SQL, or you load it into Redshift for a fast warehouse. Under all of it, IAM is the permission fabric: every service assumes a role, and that role is the only thing standing between "the job runs" and "Access Denied".

The guide moves from framework to ingestion (batch, CDC and streaming), storage, transformation and serving, then goes deep on each undercurrent, adds the open-source ecosystem, and puts it together in worked designs and interview questions. Streaming and messaging (Kinesis, Firehose, MSK, Flink, SQS, SNS, EventBridge), database replication (DMS, Debezium) and open-source tooling each have their own sections. 01. The Data Engineering Lifecycle & the Six Undercurrents

The spine for any AWS system-design answer: five stages the data moves through, and six concerns that cut across all of them.

A system-design interview for a data engineer is rarely "which AWS service does X?" It is "design a platform that does Y" and then a probe of whatever you forgot. The most reliable way to forget nothing is to use the framework from Fundamentals of Data Engineering (Joe Reis and Matt Housley). Data has a lifecycle: it is generated by source systems, ingested, stored, transformed, and served. And six undercurrents run underneath every stage: security, data management, DataOps, data architecture, orchestration and software engineering. Walk the stages to prove the data flows; sweep the undercurrents to prove it is safe, governed, observable, maintainable and changeable. Most candidates only do the first half.

Using the framework in the interview

  1. Clarify requirements first: who consumes the data, how fresh it must be, how big it is and how fast it grows, how long it is kept, and what compliance applies. Every later choice is justified against these.
  2. Walk the stages left to right with one concrete AWS component per stage and a reason ("bronze on S3 because it is cheap and immutable; Glue for the nightly Spark job because there is no cluster to run").
  3. Sweep the six undercurrents as a closing checklist: how it is secured, governed, monitored and deployed, how it is orchestrated, how the code is tested and versioned, and how the design can change later.
  4. Pre-empt the follow-ups: what if the source sends duplicates, the job fails at 3 a.m., the schema changes, or volume grows tenfold?

Interview angle. "Design a data platform for X on AWS." Start with the lifecycle as your outline, finish by touching all six undercurrents explicitly. Candidates who describe only the pipeline (generation to serving) sound like implementers; candidates who add security, governance, DataOps, architecture trade-offs, orchestration and engineering practice sound like owners.

02. Data Architecture: Principles, Patterns & Trade-offs on AWS

Good architecture is the set of decisions that fit the business need, cost what they are worth, and can be reversed when they turn out wrong.

This is the undercurrent behind every "why did you choose that?" question. A system-design answer is judged less on the services you name than on whether you can state the requirement, the options you weighed, and the trigger that would make you change your mind. This section gives you the principles, the patterns, the AWS-specific structure (accounts, regions, resilience) and the estimation habits that interviewers probe. The concrete walk-throughs are in §27.

AWS packages a similar checklist as the Well-Architected Framework (six pillars: operational excellence, security, reliability, performance efficiency, cost optimization, sustainability) with a dedicated Data Analytics Lens that applies each pillar to lakes, warehouses and streaming. Naming it, and using its pillars as a review checklist, is a strong signal.

Concepts every architecture answer uses

  • Loose vs. tight coupling. One giant job fails and changes as a unit. Independent stages that hand off through S3 or a queue can be scaled, tested and replaced separately, at the price of more moving parts to observe.
  • Event-driven design. Producers publish events (S3 notifications, EventBridge, Kinesis); consumers react. It decouples teams and adds replay, at the cost of eventual consistency and harder debugging.
  • Distributed-systems realities. Networks fail, instances vanish, messages arrive twice or out of order. This is why idempotency and replay are design requirements, not polish.
  • Single- vs. multi-tenant. Shared infrastructure with logical isolation (Lake Formation row filters, per-tenant prefixes) is cheaper; separate accounts or clusters are safer and simpler to reason about. Decide by compliance and noisy-neighbor risk.
  • Brownfield vs. greenfield. Most work is brownfield. Migrate with the strangler pattern, moving one domain at a time, rather than a big-bang rewrite.
  • OLTP vs. OLAP. Row-oriented transactional stores (Aurora, DynamoDB) serve applications; columnar analytical stores (S3 Parquet/Iceberg, Redshift) serve aggregation. Never run heavy analytics on the production OLTP database.

Multi-account structure and a data mesh on AWS

Why accounts, not just IAM policies? An AWS account is the strongest isolation boundary AWS offers: it limits the blast radius of a mistake, gives each domain its own bill and service quotas, and lets Service Control Policies (SCPs) set organization-wide guardrails such as "no public S3 buckets" or "only these regions." A typical data organization has separate accounts per environment (dev, staging, prod) and per domain, plus security, log-archive and tooling accounts.

Planning for failure: availability, durability, RTO and RPO

  • Durability vs. availability. S3 is designed for 99.999999999% (eleven nines) durability and 99.99% availability for Standard, spread across at least three Availability Zones. Losing an object to hardware is not the risk; deleting or overwriting it is, which is what versioning, Object Lock and replication address.
  • RTO (how long you can be down) and RPO (how much data you can lose) are business inputs. Ask for them before choosing a strategy.

Back-of-the-envelope sizing

Requirement: 50M users, 20 events/user/day, 1 KB/event, keep 13 months.

Events/day      = 50e6 * 20               = 1e9

Peak (x5)                                  = ~58,000 events/s     -> ~58 MB/s

Raw volume      = 1e9 * 1 KB              = 1 TB/day

Interview angle. "Why this architecture and not another?" 03. Source Systems & Ingestion on AWS

Generation and ingestion: you do not own the sources, but you inherit every quirk. Evaluate the source first, then choose the pattern, then the service.

Duplicates, late events, mutable rows, silent schema changes and fragile APIs are properties of the source, and ingestion is where you absorb them. Tool choice comes last. The order of thinking is: what does the source do (evaluation checklist), what pattern fits (batch or stream, push or pull, full or incremental, CDC method), and only then which AWS service. This section gives the framework and the AWS toolbox; database replication (§04), streams (§05, §06, §07) and queues and events (§08) each have a deep dive.

CDC from a database to the lake

-- Athena: apply a DMS change batch to an Iceberg table (latest change per key wins, deletes honored)
MERGE INTO curated.orders t
USING (
  SELECT * FROM (
    SELECT *, row_number() OVER (PARTITION BY order_id ORDER BY change_seq DESC) AS rn
    FROM raw.orders_cdc WHERE ingest_date = DATE '2026-09-23'
  ) WHERE rn = 1
) s
ON t.order_id = s.order_id
WHEN MATCHED AND s.Op = 'D' THEN DELETE
WHEN MATCHED THEN UPDATE SET status = s.status, amount = s.amount, updated_at = s.updated_at
WHEN NOT MATCHED AND s.Op != 'D' THEN INSERT (order_id, status, amount, updated_at)

Event-driven file ingestion

# Lambda: an S3 "object created" event starts the pipeline. S3 delivers at least once, so be idempotent.
import boto3, json, urllib.parse
sfn = boto3.client("stepfunctions")

def handler(event, context):
    for rec in event["Records"]:
        key = urllib.parse.unquote_plus(rec["s3"]["object"]["key"])
        if key.endswith(".tmp") or not key.endswith((".csv", ".json")):
            continue                                           # ignore partial or irrelevant files
        try:
            sfn.start_execution(
                stateMachineArn=STATE_MACHINE_ARN,
                name=f"ingest-{rec['s3']['object']['eTag']}",   # same object = same execution name
                input=json.dumps({"bucket": rec["s3"]["bucket"]["name"], "key": key}))
        except sfn.exceptions.ExecutionAlreadyExists:
            pass                                               # duplicate notification: already started or finished

Notice the deliberate execution name derived from the object's ETag. Starting a Standard workflow twice with the same name either returns the running execution or raises ExecutionAlreadyExists, so S3's at-least-once notifications cannot start the pipeline twice for the same object. That is idempotency by construction rather than by hope.

Landing conventions that save you later

  • Never mutate the raw zone. It is your replay source. Add metadata (ingest time, source file, batch id) as columns or in object metadata, but keep the payload as received.
  • Partition by ingestion date in raw (ingest_date=) even if the business date is different, so a bad day can be found and reloaded precisely.
  • Write files atomically: upload to a temporary prefix or name and rename, or write a _SUCCESS marker last, so readers never see half a file.
  • Use a dead-letter queue for events or files that fail repeatedly, and alarm on its depth.
  • Avoid tiny files: buffer (Firehose hints) or compact early (§09).

Interview angle. "Ingest an orders table from a production Aurora database into the lake." Ask the source questions first (replica available? deletes? reliable timestamp?). Choose DMS log-based CDC into S3 raw (or zero-ETL if the warehouse is the target), merge to a curated Iceberg table on key and sequence, keep raw immutable for replay, and say how you would detect and recover a missed change (periodic row-count and checksum reconciliation, replay from raw). Mention that DMS and S3 events are at-least-once, so every downstream step must be idempotent.

04. Database Replication & CDC: DMS, Debezium and Zero-ETL

Most analytics data starts life as rows in someone else's production database. Change data capture (CDC) is how you copy every insert, update and delete out of it without hurting it.

§03 introduced CDC as one of the ingestion patterns. This section is the deep dive, because "replicate our orders database into the lake" is the single most common data-engineering interview question and the one with the most hidden traps. The short version: read the database's transaction log (not the tables), keep every change as an immutable event, and derive current state downstream. There are three practical ways to do it on AWS: AWS DMS (managed), Debezium (open source, on Kafka) and zero-ETL integrations (managed, no pipeline at all).

Why the log, and what it gives you

A database already writes every change to a log before it touches the tables (MySQL binlog, Postgres WAL, Oracle redo log, SQL Server transaction log) so it can recover from a crash and feed its own replicas. Log-based CDC reads that same log as if it were a replica. Compared with polling SELECT ... WHERE updated_at > ? it sees deletes (a deleted row is simply absent from a query), sees every intermediate change (a row updated three times between polls shows up three times), needs no reliable timestamp column, and adds almost no read load to the primary. The price is that the source must be configured to keep a readable log, and someone must watch the log's disk usage.

AWS DMS: how it is built

AWS Database Migration Service has four parts. A source endpoint and a target endpoint describe where to read and where to write. A replication instance (or DMS Serverless, which sizes capacity for you) is the compute that does the work. A replication task ties them together with table mappings (which schemas and tables, optional filters and column transformations) and a migration type: full load, CDC only, or full load + CDC, which copies a consistent snapshot and then continues from exactly where the snapshot ended.

  • Targets a data engineer uses: S3 (Parquet or CSV), Kinesis Data Streams, MSK or another Kafka, Redshift, and other databases. For a lake, land in S3 first; for several consumers or low latency, land in a stream.
  • S3 output shape: full-load files first, then CDC files. With IncludeOpForFullLoad every row carries an Op column (I, U, D), and TimestampColumnName adds the commit timestamp. Those two columns are what your merge logic keys on.
  • Primary keys matter: updates and deletes are applied by key. A table with no primary key gives you a change stream you cannot merge safely. Push back on the source team, or add a surrogate key.
  • Large objects (LOBs) are slow. Use limited LOB mode with a sensible size cap when values are small; full LOB mode is far slower.
  • Validation can compare source and target rows after load. It costs source and target reads, so enable it deliberately, not by default on the biggest tables.
  • Schema changes: DDL support is partial and depends on the engine and target. Treat raw as schema-on-read, publish a data contract (§19) and alarm when a new column appears.
  • Health: watch CDCLatencySource (log to DMS) and CDCLatencyTarget (DMS to target). Rising source latency means the reader is behind the log; rising target latency means the sink is slow. Run the replication instance Multi-AZ for anything important.
  • Heterogeneous moves (Oracle to Postgres) also need the AWS Schema Conversion Tool for the schema and code. That is a migration project, not an ingestion pipeline.

Debezium: open-source CDC on Kafka

Debezium is an open-source set of Kafka Connect source connectors (MySQL, PostgreSQL, SQL Server, Oracle, MongoDB and more). Each connector reads the database log and writes one Kafka topic per table. On AWS you run it on MSK Connect (managed Kafka Connect) against MSK (§06), so you never manage a Connect cluster. Its events are richer than DMS's flat rows: every message carries the row before and after the change, the operation, and the source position.

{ "before": { "order_id": 42, "status": "NEW",  "amount": 90.0 },
  "after":  { "order_id": 42, "status": "PAID", "amount": 90.0 },
  "source": { "connector": "postgresql", "table": "orders", "lsn": 123456789, "ts_ms": 1758700000000 },
  "op": "u",                      // c = create, u = update, d = delete, r = snapshot read

A delete produces an event with op = "d" and then a tombstone (a message with the key and a null value), which lets Kafka's log compaction eventually drop the key. Debezium first takes a consistent snapshot (op = "r") and then streams changes, and it stores its own schema history so it can decode old log entries after a table changes.

Zero-ETL: no pipeline to build

For the most common case, zero-ETL integrations replicate an operational store into an analytics store as a managed feature: Aurora and some RDS engines into Redshift, DynamoDB into Redshift, OpenSearch and lakehouse targets, and several SaaS sources. There is nothing to schedule and the lag is typically seconds to minutes. The limits are the point of the interview answer: fixed source and target pairs, limited transformation in flight, you cannot change how it lands, and the target sees the source's schema. Use it when the warehouse is the only consumer and current state is enough; use DMS or Debezium when you need raw history in S3, multiple consumers, or control over the landing format.

Turning change events into a table

Whatever tool captured the changes, the downstream logic is the same and lives in §13: keep the raw change log immutable in raw/, pick the latest event per key by the change sequence (row_number() over (partition by key order by seq desc)), and MERGE into an Iceberg table, deleting where the operation is D. The SQL is shown in §03. If you also need history, write an SCD Type 2 table from the same events (§13).

The traps, as a checklist

  • Snapshot to CDC handoff. The snapshot and the first change must line up on a log position, or you lose or duplicate rows around the boundary. Full load + CDC in DMS and Debezium's snapshot handle this; a hand-rolled export plus a separate CDC start does not.
  • Ordering. Order by the log position or commit sequence, never by arrival time or an application-set updated_at.
  • Big transactions and bulk updates arrive as bursts. Size for the burst, not the average, and expect lag after a backfill on the source.
  • Hard deletes and GDPR. Deletes flow through as events; the erasure job in §27 must also purge raw copies.
  • Silent gaps. Any CDC pipeline can lose changes (log purged, task restarted wrongly). Reconcile counts and checksums per table on a schedule, and alarm on latency.
  • Cutover. For a migration, run source and target in parallel, compare, switch consumers by domain, and keep the ability to roll back.

Interview angle. "Replicate our production Postgres into the lake, deletes included, without slowing it down." Say log-based CDC from a read replica or with a logical slot, DMS full load + CDC into S3 (or Debezium on MSK if there is a Kafka platform), Parquet with an Op flag, merge to Iceberg on key and sequence, raw kept immutable for replay. Then volunteer the two operational risks that show experience: the replication slot filling the source disk if the consumer stops, and silent gaps caught by reconciliation.

05. Streaming Fundamentals: Kinesis Data Streams & Data Firehose

A stream is a durable, ordered, replayable log of events. Learn the vocabulary once and it applies to Kinesis, Kafka and every engine that reads them.

Everything before this section assumed data sitting still in files. Clickstreams, IoT telemetry, application logs and change events do not: records arrive continuously, and one or more consumers process them within seconds or minutes. The tool for that is a stream, not a queue and not a bigger batch job. This section covers the streaming vocabulary and AWS's native pair, Kinesis Data Streams (the log) and Amazon Data Firehose (managed delivery to storage). Kafka is in §06, computation over streams in §07, and queues and event buses in §08. For the Kafka plus Spark view of the same ideas see Real-Time Data & Stream Processing.

Kinesis Data Streams

A Kinesis stream is a set of shards. A record's partition key is hashed to choose its shard, so records with the same key are always in the same shard and in order. Capacity is measured in shards: each shard accepts about 1 MB/s or 1,000 records/s of writes and serves about 2 MB/s of reads shared among ordinary consumers. Exceed a shard's limit and the producer gets ProvisionedThroughputExceededException.

Sizing: the arithmetic to do out loud

1 billion events a day is about 11,600 events/s on average. With a 5x peak that is 58,000 events/s; at 1 KB per event about 58 MB/s. A shard takes 1 MB/s, so you need about 60 shards in provisioned mode, or you choose on-demand mode, which scales for you and removes the capacity planning. Provisioned is cheaper for steady, well-understood load; on-demand for spiky or unknown load. Also check the records-per-second limit: 1 KB events hit 1,000 records/s at only 1 MB/s, so tiny records are the constraint, not bytes.

Producing safely

  • Batch: PutRecords takes up to 500 records per call. The Kinesis Producer Library also aggregates many small user records into one Kinesis record, which cuts request count and cost.
  • Cost shape: provisioned streams bill per shard-hour plus per 25 KB PUT payload unit, and every record is rounded up to a unit. Millions of 200-byte records each cost as 25 KB unless aggregated.
  • Partition key: high cardinality and evenly spread, but related events share a key when order matters (a user id, not a country). A skewed key makes one hot shard throttle while the others idle.
  • Partial failure is normal: PutRecords can succeed overall while individual records fail. You must check FailedRecordCount and retry only the failed ones with backoff.
  • Large payloads: records have a size cap. Put big blobs in S3 and send a pointer (the claim-check pattern).
def put_batch(kinesis, stream, events, attempts=5):
    entries = [{"Data": json.dumps(e).encode(), "PartitionKey": str(e["user_id"])} for e in events]
    for attempt in range(attempts):
        resp = kinesis.put_records(StreamName=stream, Records=entries)
        if resp["FailedRecordCount"] == 0:
            return
        # keep only the records that failed (they carry an ErrorCode) and retry with jittered backoff
        entries = [en for en, r in zip(entries, resp["Records"]) if "ErrorCode" in r]
        time.sleep(min(0.1 * 2 ** attempt, 2) + random.random() * 0.1)
    raise RuntimeError(f"{len(entries)} records still failing after {attempts} attempts")

Scaling and operating

  • Resharding (provisioned): split a hot shard or merge cold ones. Consume the parent shard's remaining records before its children to preserve order; the KCL and Lambda do this for you.
  • Retention costs more beyond 24 hours. Keep only as long as replay needs; raw S3 is the long-term replay source.
  • Exactly-once: Kinesis is at-least-once. Producers can retry duplicates and consumers can reprocess a batch, so downstream writes must be idempotent (dedupe on an event id, MERGE on a key).
  • Security: KMS encryption at rest, IAM per producer and consumer role, and a VPC interface endpoint for private traffic (§17).

Amazon Data Firehose: managed delivery

Firehose (formerly Kinesis Data Firehose) takes records from direct API calls, a Kinesis stream, MSK, CloudWatch Logs and other AWS sources, buffers them, optionally transforms them, and writes them to a destination: S3, Iceberg tables, Redshift (through S3 and COPY), OpenSearch, and HTTP endpoints such as Splunk or Datadog. It is the answer to "I need this stream in S3 and I do not want to write and run a consumer." It is delivery, not processing, and it is near real time, not real time, because it buffers by size or time before writing.

  • Buffering: it flushes at a size (up to 128 MB) or a time interval (up to 900 seconds; the S3 default is 300, and some destinations allow near-zero), whichever comes first. Bigger buffers mean fewer, larger files (good for Athena), but more latency. This is your small-file control.
  • Format conversion: JSON in, Parquet or ORC out, using a schema from a Glue Data Catalog table. Big columnar files with no code.
  • Dynamic partitioning: extract a field (for example the event date) and use it in the S3 prefix, so files land in dt=2026-09-23/ folders your query engine can prune.
  • Lambda transform: light per-record changes (mask a field, drop noise) in flight. Heavy transformation still belongs downstream.
  • Iceberg destination: writes straight into Iceberg tables (including inserts, updates and deletes), so the stream lands as a queryable, compacted-friendly table.
  • Failures: records that cannot be delivered or converted go to an error prefix in S3 instead of being lost. Alarm on delivery failures and data freshness.
  • No replay, no ordering guarantee: Firehose is a pipe. Replay comes from the source stream or the S3 copy.
  • Cost shape: per GB ingested (records are rounded up to 5 KB), plus extra for format conversion and dynamic partitioning. Many tiny records are expensive; aggregate first.

The hot shard and the poison record. These two problems cause most Kinesis incidents. 06. Apache Kafka & Amazon MSK

Kafka is the industry-standard event log. MSK is real Kafka with AWS operating the brokers, so what you learn here transfers to every Kafka in every company.

Apache Kafka is an open-source distributed log. Producers append records to topics; each topic is split into partitions, each an ordered, replicated log on the brokers; consumers read at their own pace by tracking an offset per partition. Its ecosystem is the reason it dominates: Kafka Connect (source and sink connectors, including Debezium for CDC), Kafka Streams and ksqlDB (processing), schema registries, and first-class support in Flink, Spark and nearly every data tool. Amazon MSK runs the brokers for you on AWS; the wire protocol, topic model and consumer-group behavior are standard Kafka, so existing clients and connectors work unchanged.

Sizing a topic and a cluster: a worked example

An order platform averages 20 MB/s with a 60 MB/s peak, keeps 7 days, and uses replication factor 3.

  • Storage: 20 MB/s x 86,400 s is about 1.7 TB per day; times 7 days is about 12 TB; times 3 replicas is about 36 TB of broker storage (tiered storage moves older data to cheaper storage). Retention and replication factor are the two cost dials.
  • Network: a write of 60 MB/s becomes 180 MB/s of cluster traffic once followers copy it, plus every consumer group's reads. Cross-AZ traffic is billed, so consumers reading from the closest replica (rack awareness) is a real saving.
  • Partitions: choose from the consumer side. If one consumer instance handles about 10 MB/s and you need 60 MB/s at peak, you need at least 6 consumers, so at least 6 partitions; take 12 to leave room to grow. Then verify the brokers cope with the write rate per partition.
  • Lag alarm: alarm on consumer-group lag (MSK publishes MaxOffsetLag and SumOffsetLag), not only on broker health.

07. Stream Processing: Flink, Spark Structured Streaming & Friends

A stream moves records. Stream processing computes over them: running counts, windows, joins, alerts. Time and state are what make it hard.

Moving records is Kinesis and Kafka's job (§05, §06). The moment a question needs "the number of failed payments in the last five minutes per merchant", something must remember earlier events, decide when a window is finished, and survive its own crashes without double counting. That is a stream processor. This section gives the concepts every engine shares, then the AWS options and how to choose. The mental model is portable: the same ideas apply in Flink, Spark, Kafka Streams and Databricks (see Real-Time Data & Stream Processing for the Kafka plus Spark walkthrough).

Managed Service for Apache Flink

Apache Flink is the leading open-source stateful stream processor. Managed Service for Apache Flink (formerly Kinesis Data Analytics) runs your Flink application without a cluster to size or patch. You package a Java, Scala or Python application (or write Flink SQL in a Studio notebook), give it a source and sink (Kinesis, MSK, S3, Iceberg, OpenSearch, DynamoDB, JDBC and more), an IAM role and optionally a VPC, and AWS runs it. Capacity is measured in KPUs (each about 1 vCPU, 4 GB of memory and 50 GB of working storage), parallelism is set per application, and autoscaling adjusts it to load.

  • Checkpoints and snapshots: automatic checkpoints (about every minute by default) let a failed task resume from its last consistent state. Snapshots (Flink savepoints) are taken on purpose before upgrades so you can change code and continue without losing state.
  • Exactly-once: Flink's checkpoints coordinate source offsets and state; sinks that support two-phase commit or idempotence (Kafka transactional sink, Iceberg commits per checkpoint, file sinks) complete the guarantee.
  • Health signals: source lag (millisBehindLatest for Kinesis, consumer lag for Kafka), checkpoint duration and failures, backpressure, and CPU by task. A growing checkpoint time is usually growing state.
  • Why not Lambda for this? A Lambda invocation is short-lived and stateless. A rolling five-minute average would mean reading and writing state to a database on every invocation, which is slow and easy to get subtly wrong on retries. Flink keeps that state as a first-class, checkpointed part of the program.
-- Flink SQL: per-minute page views on event time, tolerating 30 seconds of lateness

  url        STRING,

        'scan.stream.initpos' = 'LATEST', 'format' = 'json');

Reprocessing and backfill

Streaming code has bugs like any code, and the fix is to replay. Keep events long enough on the stream (or in raw S3) to reprocess: start a new job from an earlier offset or timestamp, write to a new table or a new version of the output, compare, then switch consumers. Running the same logic over bounded history (Flink reading S3, Spark using the same DataFrame code in batch) is the practical answer to the Lambda versus Kappa question: one code path that can run in streaming or batch mode is easier to keep correct than two.

State is not free or automatically bounded. A window or a join keyed on a high-cardinality field (a raw user_id with tens of millions of values, kept indefinitely) makes checkpointed state, checkpoint time, recovery time and cost grow with it. Bound window lengths, expire idle keys with a time-to-live, and choose keys deliberately. Growing checkpoint duration is the early warning sign.

Interview angle. "Alert within seconds when a card is used in two countries within ten minutes." Cover time (event time, watermark, late events to a side output), state (checkpoints to S3, bounded window and key TTL), delivery (at-least-once source, idempotent alerting) and operations (lag, checkpoint failures, replay to fix a bug). 08. Queues, Topics & Event Buses: SQS, SNS and EventBridge

Not every asynchronous problem needs a stream. Queues absorb work, topics fan events out, buses route them by content. Know which primitive you are holding.

Data pipelines are full of small asynchronous hand-offs: a file landed, a job finished, a quality check failed, a partner needs to be told. The AWS messaging services are the glue between them, and choosing the wrong primitive is a classic mistake (using a stream where a queue was enough, or a Lambda calling a Lambda where an event should have been published). Three services cover almost everything a data engineer needs; §05 and §06 cover the fourth primitive, the replayable log.

Amazon SQS: the queue

SQS stores messages until a consumer processes them. A consumer receives a message, which makes it invisible to others for the visibility timeout; if the consumer finishes it deletes the message, and if it crashes or is too slow the message becomes visible again and is retried. That single mechanism gives you durable work distribution and automatic retry, and it also explains why processing must be idempotent: a slow success looks exactly like a failure.

  • Dead-letter queue (DLQ): after maxReceiveCount failed receives a message moves to a DLQ so one poison message cannot loop forever. Alarm on the DLQ's depth; a DLQ nobody watches is a silent data-loss mechanism. Redrive lets you send messages back after the bug is fixed.
  • Long polling (WaitTimeSeconds up to 20) cuts empty responses and cost.
  • Retention from one minute to 14 days (default four days), so a queue also buffers an outage of the consumer.
  • Message size is limited (up to 1 MiB now; older docs and libraries say 256 KB). For files and big payloads send a pointer to S3 (the claim-check pattern).
  • With Lambda: set the visibility timeout to at least six times the function timeout, enable ReportBatchItemFailures so only failed messages return, and use maximum concurrency on the event source mapping to protect a downstream that cannot take unlimited parallel calls.

Amazon SNS: the topic

SNS is publish/subscribe. A producer publishes once; every subscription receives a copy. Subscribers can be SQS queues, Lambda functions, Firehose delivery streams, HTTP endpoints, email and SMS. Filter policies let a subscriber receive only matching messages, by attributes or by fields in the message body, so one topic can serve many consumers with different interests. SNS pushes and does not store: a subscriber that is down loses the message unless the subscription is an SQS queue, which is why the standard pattern below exists.

Amazon EventBridge: the bus and the clock

EventBridge does two related jobs. As an event bus it receives events (from AWS services, your applications and SaaS partners) and routes each one to targets using rules that match on the event's content. As a scheduler it starts targets on a cron, rate or one-time schedule, with time zones and retry policies. Together they are how most AWS pipelines actually start and how failures are noticed.

  • Rules and targets: a rule holds an event pattern and up to several targets (Lambda, Step Functions, SQS, SNS, Kinesis, ECS tasks, API destinations for third-party HTTP). Targets have retry policies and their own DLQ.
  • Pipes: a point-to-point connection from a source (SQS, Kinesis, DynamoDB Streams, MSK) through optional filtering and enrichment to a target, with no glue code.
  • Archive and replay: store events and replay them into a bus later, which helps when a consumer was broken or you add a new one.
  • Schema registry discovers and versions event shapes, and generates code bindings.
  • Delivery is at-least-once and ordering is not guaranteed. Consumers must be idempotent, which is the same rule as everywhere else in this guide.

09. Amazon S3 - the storage layer

Every AWS data platform starts here. Get the layout and permissions right and everything downstream is easy; get them wrong and every query pays for it forever.

S3 (Simple Storage Service) stores objects - a file plus its metadata - inside a bucket. Each object is addressed by a key, which is the whole path string after the bucket name. S3 has no real folders: data/sales/2026/01/file.parquet is one flat key; the slashes are just characters, and tools draw a folder tree from them. A leading chunk of the key shared by many objects is a prefix (data/sales/2026/) - that word comes up constantly in permissions and performance.

Versioning, lifecycle, encryption

  • Versioning keeps every past version of an object instead of overwriting. A "delete" just adds a delete marker; the old bytes are still there. It protects against a bad job or a fat-fingered aws s3 rm, but old versions keep costing money until a lifecycle rule removes them.
  • Lifecycle policies are per-prefix rules that run daily: "move raw/ objects to Glacier after 30 days", "delete noncurrent versions after 7 days", "delete incomplete multipart uploads after 3 days". This is your main cost lever.
  • Encryption at rest is on by default (SSE-S3). Use SSE-KMS when you need an audit trail of who decrypted what, or key-level access control - the trade-off is a small per-request cost and a KMS permission every reader also needs.

Multipart upload

Any upload over ~100 MB should be split into parts (5 MB–5 GB each), uploaded in parallel, and reassembled by S3. The SDKs and aws s3 cp do this automatically. Two things follow: a failed big upload leaves orphan parts that still cost storage until a lifecycle rule cleans them up, and Spark/Glue writing many files uses multipart under the hood - which is why thousands of tiny output files are slow and expensive to produce, not just to read.

Layout: partitions, file format, and the small-file problem

A partition is a column encoded into the key path, almost always date first:

s3://lake/silver/orders/order_date=2026-04-01/part-0000.parquet
s3://lake/silver/orders/order_date=2026-04-02/part-0000.parquet

Athena, Redshift Spectrum, Glue and Spark all read the partition value straight from the path and skip folders a query's WHERE clause can't match. Rules that hold everywhere:

  • Partition by what you filter on - usually date, occasionally one more low-cardinality column (region, event type). Never by something high-cardinality like user_id: you get millions of one-row folders and zero speed-up.
  • Use a columnar format - Parquet (or ORC). It compresses ~5× better than CSV/JSON and lets engines read only the columns a query needs. Keep CSV/JSON for raw landing only, and convert to Parquet in the first transform.
  • Aim for 128 MB–1 GB files. Thousands of tiny files ("the small-file problem") make every listing and every job slow because each file is a separate open, read, and close. Compact them on write (coalesce / repartition) or with a scheduled compaction job.

Consistency, performance, and two "conceptual" features

  • Consistency: S3 is now strongly consistent - a PUT is immediately visible to the next GET or LIST. The old "eventual consistency" workarounds in tutorials are obsolete.
  • Performance: S3 scales per prefix - thousands of requests per second per prefix, effectively unlimited if the load spreads across many prefixes. A date-partitioned layout spreads it naturally. There is no benefit anymore to hashing random characters into the key.
  • Cross-Region Replication (CRR): a rule that copies new objects to a bucket in another Region, for disaster recovery or to put data near a second team. Asynchronous; adds transfer and storage cost.
  • Event notifications: S3 can fire an event on s3:ObjectCreated:* to Lambda, SQS, or EventBridge. This is how "a file landed → start processing" pipelines begin (see §06).

Securing a bucket, the short checklist: Block Public Access on (account-wide and per-bucket); default encryption on; a bucket policy that Denys any request where aws:SecureTransport is false; access granted only through IAM roles with a specific prefix (arn:aws:s3:::lake/silver/*), never s3:* on *; versioning on for anything you can't rebuild; CloudTrail data events on if you need an audit log of object access.

How a query engine actually reads S3

Spark, Athena and Trino do not download whole files. For a Parquet file they issue byte-range GETs: first read the footer (schema + row-group offsets + column min/max), decide which row groups and columns the query needs, then range-GET only those byte spans. This is why Parquet + partitioning + column selection cut cost so sharply - the engine skips folders (partition pruning), skips files (row-group stats), and skips columns (columnar layout) before reading a byte of data. It is also why a 1 GB Parquet file is fine but 10,000×100 KB files are slow: each tiny file still costs a footer read, a GET, and an open/close.

A few features worth recognising

  • Access Points - a named endpoint with its own policy on top of one bucket, so each team or app gets a scoped door instead of everyone sharing one giant bucket policy.
  • Object Lock (WORM) - write-once-read-many retention a compliance team asks for; an object literally cannot be deleted or overwritten until its retention date.
  • Presigned URLs - a time-limited signed link that lets someone without AWS credentials PUT or GET one specific object; how upload forms and "download your export" links work.
  • Requester Pays - flips the data-transfer bill to whoever reads the bucket; used for shared public datasets.
  • Directory markers - some tools write a zero-byte prefix/ object to fake an empty folder. Harmless, but it can confuse a crawler or a naive list; know it when you see _$folder$ or a stray 0-byte key.

Lakehouse table formats on S3: Iceberg, Delta, Hudi

Plain Parquet files on S3 are immutable and unmanaged: you cannot update one row, two writers can corrupt each other's results, and "what did this table look like yesterday?" has no answer. An open table format adds a metadata layer on top of the same Parquet files that provides ACID transactions, row-level MERGE/UPDATE/DELETE, schema and partition evolution, snapshot isolation and time travel. This is what turns a data lake into a lakehouse, and it is the answer to most "how do you do upserts on S3?" questions.

How Iceberg works, in one paragraph: a table is a pointer to a metadata file, which lists snapshots; each snapshot points to manifest lists, which point to manifests, which list the actual data files with their column statistics. A write creates new data files and a new metadata file, then atomically swaps the pointer (the catalog does the atomic swap; on AWS that is the Glue Data Catalog). Readers always see a consistent snapshot; old snapshots stay readable until you expire them. The statistics let engines skip files, and because partitioning is recorded in metadata, you can change the partition scheme without rewriting old data.

-- Athena: create an Iceberg table, upsert into it, and travel back in time
CREATE TABLE curated.orders (order_id string, status string, amount decimal(12,2), updated_at timestamp, order_date date)
PARTITIONED BY (day(order_date))
LOCATION 's3://lake/curated/orders/'
TBLPROPERTIES ('table_type' = 'ICEBERG', 'format' = 'parquet');

MERGE INTO curated.orders t USING staging.orders_delta s ON t.order_id = s.order_id
WHEN MATCHED THEN UPDATE SET status = s.status, amount = s.amount, updated_at = s.updated_at
WHEN NOT MATCHED THEN INSERT VALUES (s.order_id, s.status, s.amount, s.updated_at, s.order_date);

SELECT * FROM curated.orders FOR TIMESTAMP AS OF (current_timestamp - interval '1' day);   -- yesterday's state

OPTIMIZE curated.orders REWRITE DATA USING BIN_PACK;      -- compact small files
VACUUM curated.orders;                                    -- expire old snapshots, delete orphaned files

Maintenance is part of the deal. Every write adds files and a snapshot. Without periodic compaction, snapshot expiry and orphan-file cleanup, an Iceberg table becomes slow and costly. Schedule the maintenance, or use S3 Tables, an S3 bucket type that stores Iceberg tables and performs compaction and snapshot management automatically. Snapshot expiry also determines how long deleted data remains recoverable, which matters for GDPR erasure (§19).

❯ Short version

S3 = objects in a bucket, addressed by a flat key; prefixes are the unit of both permissions and scaling. Storage classes and lifecycle rules control cost. Lay data out as Bronze/Silver/Gold prefixes, Parquet, partitioned by date, in 128 MB–1 GB files. Grant access with narrow IAM policies on specific prefixes plus a bucket policy that enforces TLS; block public access always.

~/aws ❯ the default ETL engine

10. AWS Glue - catalog, crawlers & Spark ETL

Two separate things share the name "Glue": a metadata catalog that makes S3 files queryable as tables, and a serverless Spark service that runs your ETL jobs.

The Glue Data Catalog

The Data Catalog is a metastore: it holds databases (namespaces), tables (a schema plus the S3 location and file format), and each table's partitions (the list of date=2026-04-01 folders that exist and where they are). It stores no data - only "there is a table called orders, it's Parquet, it lives at s3://lake/silver/orders/, and here are its 400 date partitions." Athena, Redshift Spectrum, EMR and Glue jobs all read this same catalog, so one table definition works everywhere. It is API-compatible with the Hive metastore.

Crawlers

A crawler points at an S3 prefix (or a JDBC source), samples the files, infers a schema and the partition columns, and writes or updates the table in the catalog. Run it on a schedule or after each load so newly added date partitions become visible. Caveats: a crawler can guess a type wrong (a ZIP code as bigint), it will happily create a hundred tables if your prefix layout is inconsistent, and for a table whose schema you control it is often cleaner to CREATE TABLE once and just call MSCK REPAIR TABLE / ALTER TABLE ADD PARTITION (or enable partition projection, see §07) instead of crawling repeatedly.

Glue Jobs - serverless Spark

A Glue job is a PySpark (or Scala) script that AWS runs on a Spark cluster it provisions for the run and tears down after. You choose a worker type (G.1X, G.2X, ...) and a number of workers; you pay per second of DPU (data processing unit) time. There is nothing to keep running between jobs.

PieceWhat it is
GlueContextA wrapper around Spark's SparkContext that adds catalog-aware readers/writers and bookmark tracking. You still have full SparkSession access underneath.
DynamicFrameGlue's own distributed table type. Like a Spark DataFrame but with a flexible schema - it can hold a column that is a string in some rows and an int in others (a "choice" type) and resolve it later. Handy for messy JSON; convert to a normal DataFrame with .toDF() the moment the data is clean, because DataFrames are faster and every Spark tutorial assumes them.
Job bookmarkState Glue persists between runs so a job processes only new data. It remembers which S3 files (by path + timestamp) or which JDBC key range it already read. Turn it on for incremental jobs; it is the built-in answer to "don't reprocess everything every night."
ConnectionA stored, reusable set of connection details + credentials (via Secrets Manager) for a JDBC database, Kafka, or a VPC. A job that reads Postgres or writes Redshift references a Connection so it lands in the right network and gets the right secret.
Glue StudioA visual editor that generates the PySpark script from drag-and-drop boxes (source → transform → target). Fine for simple jobs and for learning the API; teams usually switch to hand-written scripts in Git once logic gets real.
Triggers & WorkflowsA trigger starts a job on a schedule, on demand, or when another job succeeds. A workflow chains crawlers and jobs into one monitored DAG. Both exist, but for anything non-trivial teams orchestrate Glue from MWAA or Step Functions instead - more visibility, retries, and backfill.

A minimal S3 → Glue → S3 job

# read Silver, aggregate, write Gold - partitioned Parquet, bookmark on

IAM, monitoring, and tuning a slow job

  • The Glue IAM role (a service role Glue assumes) needs: s3:GetObject/PutObject on the job's input and output prefixes, glue:*Table/*Partition on the catalog, logs:* for CloudWatch, secretsmanager:GetSecretValue if it uses a Connection, and (for Redshift) sts:AssumeRole or a pass-role on the Redshift COPY role. See §04.
  • Monitoring: the job run page shows DPU-hours, duration, and a link to CloudWatch logs; enable the Spark UI and job metrics options to get the same stage/shuffle/skew view as any Spark job.
  • A slow Glue Spark job is a slow Spark job - the fixes are the same: partition pruning and Parquet on the input, broadcast() the small side of a join, raise workers if it's genuinely CPU/shuffle-bound (not if it's waiting on a skewed key), fix skew with salting or AQE, and stop writing thousands of tiny files. Job bookmarks fix the "reprocessing everything" flavour of slow.
  • Handling failures: a Glue job that fails part-way has usually written some output. Design writes to be idempotent - mode("overwrite") on a single date partition, or write to a temp prefix and atomically swap - so a retry is safe. Set --max-retries, alert on the failed-run CloudWatch metric, and let MWAA rerun the task.

Glue vs EMR vs Lambda. Lambda: tiny, short (<15 min), event-driven - reformat one file, kick off a job, call an API. Glue: the default for scheduled Spark ETL - no cluster to manage, scales to hundreds of GB, per-second billing. EMR: reach for it when you need a long-lived cluster, non-default Spark/Hadoop config, other frameworks (Hive, HBase, Presto, Flink), custom bootstrap software, or the lowest cost on very large sustained workloads with Spot.

Glue versions, workers & the DPU bill

A Glue job pins a Glue version, which fixes the Spark and Python versions (Glue 5.0 → Spark 3.5, Python 3.11; Glue 4.0 → Spark 3.3). Pin it explicitly and upgrade deliberately - a bump can change Spark behaviour under you.

SettingWhat it controls
Worker type (G.1X, G.2X, G.4X, G.8X)Memory/CPU per worker. G.1X = 4 vCPU / 16 GB / 1 DPU; each step up doubles it. Use bigger workers for wide shuffles and joins that spill.
Number of workersParallelism. More workers = more tasks in flight = shorter wall-clock, up to the point the job stops being parallel (skew, one huge file).
Auto scaling (--enable-auto-scaling)Glue adds/removes workers during the run based on pending Spark tasks; you set only a max. Good default - you stop guessing the worker count.

The bill = DPU-hours × the DPU rate, per second, with a 1-minute minimum. A G.2X job with 10 workers is 20 DPU; run it 6 minutes and you pay 2 DPU-hours. The lever is total DPU-seconds - a right-sized job that finishes in 6 minutes beats an over-provisioned one that finishes in 5.

Push the filter into the read

Don't read a whole table and then filter. Pass a pushdown predicate so Glue only lists and reads the partitions you need - the same idea as a WHERE on a partition column, applied at the catalog level:

dyf = gc.create_dynamic_frame.from_catalog(
    database="silver", table_name="orders",
    push_down_predicate="year='2026' and month='04'",   # partition columns only
    additional_options={"boundedFiles": "5000"})          # cap files per run for incremental control

For DataFrame reads, the equivalent is a normal .where() on partition columns before any wide operation - Spark's Catalyst prunes it. Job bookmarks and pushdown predicates solve different halves of "read less": bookmarks skip files already processed, predicates skip partitions the query doesn't want.

Register new partitions as you write

A common bug: a job writes s3://lake/silver/orders/order_date=2026-04-02/ but Athena still can't see it because the catalog wasn't told. Fix it in the write itself:

sink = gc.getSink(path="s3://lake/silver/orders/", connection_type="s3",
                  updateBehavior="UPDATE_IN_DATABASE",
                  partitionKeys=["order_date"], enableUpdateCatalog=True)
sink.setCatalogInfo(catalogDatabase="silver", catalogTableName="orders")
sink.setFormat("glueparquet")
sink.writeFrame(dyf)

Now the new partition appears in the catalog automatically - no crawler, no MSCK REPAIR. For catalogs with tens of thousands of partitions, also add a partition index on the table so partition lookups stay fast.

Interactive sessions & getResolvedOptions

Glue interactive sessions give you a Jupyter kernel backed by real Glue workers - develop the script line by line, then paste it into a job. Job arguments come in via getResolvedOptions(sys.argv, ["JOB_NAME", "date"]), and you pass them from MWAA's GlueJobOperator(script_args={"--date": "{{ ds }}"}). The -- prefix on the AWS side maps to the bare name in getResolvedOptions.

❯ Short version

The Data Catalog makes S3 files look like tables to every engine; crawlers populate it. A Glue job is serverless PySpark: GlueContext for catalog-aware I/O, DynamicFrame for messy schemas (convert to DataFrame fast), job bookmarks for incremental runs, Connections for JDBC/VPC. Give the job a tight IAM role, monitor it through the Spark UI, and tune it exactly like any Spark job.

11. Amazon EMR - a real Spark cluster

EMR (Elastic MapReduce) is managed Hadoop & Spark on EC2: you get an actual cluster you can configure, keep alive, and pack with other frameworks.

Where Glue hides the cluster, EMR gives you one. AWS installs Spark, Hadoop, Hive, Presto/Trino, HBase, Flink and more on a fleet of EC2 instances, wires them together, and lets you SSH in, change configs, add software with bootstrap actions, and scale nodes up and down. You run it when you need control Glue doesn't offer, or the lowest cost on very large, steady workloads.

The three node roles

NodeRunsIf you lose it
Primary (master)The YARN ResourceManager, the HDFS NameNode, the Spark driver (in cluster mode), job coordinationThe cluster is down. Usually 1 node; can be 3 for high availability.
CoreYARN NodeManagers and HDFS DataNodes - they run tasks and hold the local distributed filesystemYou can lose data-in-flight and running tasks. Don't run Core on Spot.
TaskYARN NodeManagers only - pure compute, no HDFS storageNothing permanent. Perfect for Spot Instances - cheap, interruptible extra horsepower.

Hadoop & YARN, the minimum you need

  • HDFS - Hadoop's distributed filesystem across the Core nodes' local disks. On EMR you mostly ignore it and read/write S3 directly via the s3:// connector (EMRFS); HDFS is just fast scratch space for shuffle and temp files.
  • YARN - the cluster resource manager. It hands out "containers" (chunks of CPU + memory on a node) to applications. When you spark-submit, YARN finds room for the driver and executors and launches them. Spark's own executor/core/memory settings are requests YARN fulfils.

Running a job

# SSH to the primary node, or add a "Step" to the cluster:
spark-submit --deploy-mode cluster \
  --conf spark.sql.shuffle.partitions=400 \
  s3://lake/code/daily_revenue.py --date 2026-04-01

# EMR uses the Glue Data Catalog as its metastore when you set:
#   hive.metastore.client.factory.class = ...AWSGlueDataCatalogHiveClientFactory
# -> the same tables Athena and Glue see, no separate metastore to run

An EMR job is submitted as a Step (a queued command on the cluster) or via spark-submit over SSH, or from Airflow with EmrAddStepsOperator. Cluster lifecycle: a transient cluster is created for one batch and auto-terminates when its steps finish (cheapest for scheduled jobs); a long-running cluster stays up for interactive or streaming use. Auto Scaling / managed scaling adds and removes Task nodes based on YARN pending work. Bootstrap actions are scripts that run on every node at launch to install extra packages or drop in config.

EMR Serverless (conceptual): the same Spark/Hive runtime with no cluster to size or launch - you submit a job to an "application", it scales workers automatically and bills per vCPU-second used. It sits between Glue and classic EMR: more Spark control than Glue, none of the EC2/YARN management of EMR. Reach for it when you've outgrown Glue's knobs but don't want to run a cluster.

IAM, logging, cost

  • Two roles minimum: the EMR service role (lets EMR create EC2, security groups, etc.) and the EC2 instance profile (the role every node assumes - this is what needs s3:GetObject/PutObject on your prefixes and glue:Get* on the catalog). EMR Serverless uses a single job execution role.
  • Logging: point the cluster at a s3://.../emr-logs/ path at launch - step stderr/stdout, YARN container logs and the Spark event log all land there and survive termination. The persistent Spark History Server in the console reads that event log.
  • Cost levers: transient clusters (pay only for the batch); Task nodes on Spot (50–90% off, and losing one just reruns a few tasks); managed scaling with a sane max; right-size instance families to the workload (memory-heavy shuffles → r family); and use S3, not oversized HDFS.

EMR vs Glue vs Databricks. Glue: least ops, great default, per-second billing, limited config. EMR: full cluster control, many frameworks, cheapest at large steady scale with Spot - but you own tuning and lifecycle. Databricks: a managed lakehouse platform (notebooks, Delta Lake, Unity Catalog, Photon engine, collaboration) that runs its own compute on your AWS account - more productivity and performance features than either, at a platform fee on top of the EC2 cost.

Release labels & configuration classifications

An EMR cluster pins a release label (emr-7.5.0) that fixes the versions of Spark, Hadoop, Hive and everything else on the box. You set engine config at launch with configuration classifications - JSON blocks that land in the right config file on every node:

[

      "spark.sql.shuffle.partitions": "400",

Instance fleets, Spot, and managed scaling

  • Instance fleets let one node group draw from several instance types and both On-Demand and Spot, with a target capacity and an allocation strategy (capacity-optimized picks the Spot pool least likely to be reclaimed). This is how you get cheap, resilient Task capacity.
  • Managed scaling adds and removes Task (and optionally Core) nodes between a min and max based on YARN pending/allocated memory. Set a sane max so a runaway job can't scale to a fortune.
  • Keep Core nodes On-Demand (they hold HDFS and shuffle data); put Task nodes on Spot. A reclaimed Task node just loses its running tasks, which Spark reruns.

EMR Serverless & EMR on EKS, in one line each

  • EMR Serverless - submit a job to an application; it scales workers automatically and bills per vCPU/GB-second used, with optional pre-initialised capacity to kill cold-start. No EC2, no YARN, no cluster lifecycle. The middle ground between Glue and classic EMR.
  • EMR on EKS - run Spark jobs as pods on a Kubernetes cluster you already operate, so data and services share one platform. For teams already deep in EKS.

A cost worked example

Nightly job: read 400 GB, join, aggregate, write 60 GB. ~35 min of real Spark work.

Troubleshooting a slow or failing EMR job

  • Container killed by YARN for exceeding memory limits - executor asked for more than its container. Raise spark.executor.memoryOverhead, or use fewer cores per executor, or a memory-heavy instance family (r).
  • ExecutorLostFailure / tasks retrying - often a reclaimed Spot node; usually self-heals. If it's on Core nodes, you put Core on Spot - don't.
  • One stage runs forever, most tasks done - skew. Salt the key or enable AQE skew join (spark.sql.adaptive.skewJoin.enabled).
  • Cluster idle but not terminating - you launched a long-running cluster and forgot auto-termination; add --auto-terminate for batch, or an idle-timeout rule.
  • Read the persistent Spark History Server in the console (backed by the event log in your S3 log path) exactly as you'd read any Spark UI.

EMR = managed Spark/Hadoop on EC2 you can actually configure. Primary coordinates, Core runs tasks + holds HDFS (never on Spot), Task is pure compute (put it on Spot). Read S3 directly; use the Glue Catalog as the metastore; submit jobs as Steps or via Airflow. Save money with transient clusters, Spot Task nodes, and managed scaling. Choose EMR over Glue when you need control, extra frameworks, or lowest cost at large steady scale.

12. AWS Lambda - event-driven glue code

A function that runs on an event, for up to 15 minutes, with no server. For a data engineer it's the trigger and the wiring - not the ETL engine.

Lambda runs a single function in response to an event: an object landing in S3, a message on a queue, an EventBridge schedule, an API call, an Airflow task. You upload the code (or a container image); AWS runs it on demand, scales out to thousands of concurrent copies automatically, and bills per millisecond. You never see the machine.

The anatomy

PieceMeaning
HandlerThe function AWS calls: def handler(event, context). event is the trigger payload (which bucket/key, which message); context has the request ID and time left.
RuntimeThe language environment - Python 3.13, Node, Java, or your own container image.
Memory128 MB–10 GB. CPU scales with memory, so raising memory often makes a function both faster and cheaper per run.
Timeout1 s–900 s (15 min) hard max. If your task can exceed that, it isn't a Lambda job.
ConcurrencyHow many copies run at once. Default account limit ~1000; you can reserve some for a critical function or cap one that would overwhelm a database.
Environment variablesConfig passed in without code changes (target bucket, Glue job name). Secrets via Secrets Manager, not plaintext.
Execution roleThe IAM role the function runs as - always logs:* for CloudWatch, plus exactly what it calls (S3, glue:StartJobRun, ...).
Layers (conceptual)A shared zip of libraries mounted into many functions, so common dependencies aren't packaged into every one.
Cold start (conceptual)The first call after idle pays a startup delay (a few hundred ms for Python; seconds for a big Java or heavy-dependency function). Irrelevant for batch data work; matters for user-facing APIs.

Where it fits in a pipeline

# S3 -> Lambda -> Glue : "a file landed, kick off the ETL"
def handler(event, context):
    rec = event["Records"][0]["s3"]
    bucket, key = rec["bucket"]["name"], rec["object"]["key"]
    if key.endswith("/_SUCCESS"):
        boto3.client("glue").start_job_run(
            JobName="orders_clean",
            Arguments={"--date": key.split("dt=")[1][:10]})
    # also common: Lambda -> S3 (rename/repartition one small file),
    # Lambda -> SNS/Slack (alerts), MWAA -> Lambda (a quick validation step)

What Lambda is not. Not an ETL engine. 15-minute cap, limited memory, no distributed compute - trying to process a large dataset in Lambda means either it times out or you build a fragile fan-out of thousands of functions. The moment the work is "read a big table, join, aggregate," it belongs in Glue or EMR. Lambda's job is to start those and react to their results.

Errors & logs

Uncaught exceptions are retried automatically for async invokes (twice, then to a dead-letter queue or on-failure destination if configured). Every invocation writes to a CloudWatch log stream; log structured JSON with the S3 key / job run ID so a failure is traceable. Set an alarm on the Errors and Throttles metrics.

Lambda vs Glue vs EMR. Lambda: event-driven, <15 min, small data, no cluster - triggers and wiring. Glue: scheduled Spark ETL, GBs–hundreds of GBs, serverless. EMR: TB-scale or multi-hour Spark, full cluster control. If you're asking "can Lambda do this ETL?", the answer is almost always "use Glue."

Package types & limits

Zip packageUp to 50 MB zipped / 250 MB unzipped (code + layers). boto3 is already on the runtime.
Container imageUp to 10 GB. Use it when dependencies (pandas + pyarrow + numpy) blow the zip limit, or you want the same image locally.
Ephemeral storage (/tmp)512 MB default, up to 10 GB. Scratch space for a downloaded file you reshape and re-upload.
Memory / CPU128 MB–10 GB; CPU (and network) scale with memory. A CPU-bound function is often cheaper at higher memory because it finishes faster.

How it's triggered: three models

  • Synchronous - caller waits for the return (API Gateway, a direct Invoke, an MWAA LambdaInvokeFunctionOperator). Errors go back to the caller; no automatic retry.
  • Asynchronous - the event is queued and the caller gets a 202 (S3 notifications, EventBridge). Lambda retries a failure twice, then sends it to a dead-letter queue or an on-failure destination.
  • Event source mapping - Lambda polls a stream/queue for you (SQS, Kinesis, DynamoDB Streams). You tune batch size and batch window; a whole batch fails and retries together unless you return a partial batch response naming just the bad records. The settings that matter for Kinesis and SQS are in §05 and §08.

Concurrency: reserved vs provisioned

  • Reserved concurrency - a cap and a guarantee: this function can use at most N, and those N are always available to it. Use it to protect a downstream database from a fan-out spike.
  • Provisioned concurrency - keep N instances warm so there's no cold start. Costs money while idle; only worth it for latency-critical, user-facing functions - almost never for batch data work.

The S3 fan-out anti-pattern

"An S3 event per file → one Lambda per file" looks clean until 50,000 files land at once: you hit the concurrency limit, throttle, retry, and hammer whatever the function calls. Fixes: route S3 events to an SQS queue and let one Lambda process them in batches; or aggregate with a scheduled Lambda that lists new keys and starts one Glue job; or use a Step Functions Map state with a concurrency limit. The rule holds: Lambda reacts and dispatches, it doesn't do the bulk work.

VPC-attached Lambda

A function that needs to reach a private RDS or Redshift runs in your VPC. Modern Lambda attaches the network interface almost instantly (the old multi-second penalty is gone), but the function still needs a route to the internet or a VPC endpoint to reach S3/other AWS APIs - a NAT gateway or an S3 gateway endpoint.

Cost

Bill = requests ($0.20 / million) + GB-seconds (memory × duration). A 512 MB function running 2 s costs ~1 GB-s. The free tier (1M requests + 400,000 GB-s per month) covers most glue-code workloads outright - which is part of why Lambda is the right tool for the small stuff.

❯ Short version

Lambda = one function, one event, ≤15 minutes, no server, scales itself. Handler + runtime + memory (which also sets CPU) + timeout + concurrency + an execution role. In a data platform it does the triggering and glue: S3-event → start a Glue job, MWAA → a quick check, job-done → Slack. Anything that reads a big dataset is Glue or EMR, not Lambda.

13. Transformation Design Patterns

Glue, EMR and Lambda are the engines. This section is how to use them well: ETL vs. ELT, incremental loads, upserts, history, late data and modeling.

Transformation is where raw data becomes correct, conformed and modeled. The engines (Glue, EMR, Lambda, Athena, Redshift) matter less than the patterns, which are the same on every one of them. An interview answer that names "Glue" but cannot explain how it avoids reprocessing everything each night, or what happens on a rerun, is incomplete. This section covers those patterns.

Choosing the engine

ETL vs. ELT

ETL transforms before loading into the target (Glue reads S3, cleans, writes Parquet). ELT loads raw or lightly-processed data into the target first and transforms inside it with SQL (COPY into Redshift staging, then SQL). The modern default is both, by layer: heavy, schema-fixing work in Spark (ETL) to produce clean silver data, then business modeling in SQL (ELT) close to where it is queried. ELT wins when the target engine is powerful and the team thinks in SQL; ETL wins when data needs heavy parsing, joins across huge files, or non-SQL logic.

Incremental processing: do not reprocess the world nightly

A job that rebuilds five years of history every night works for a month and then does not. Three ways to process only what is new:

TechniqueHow it worksWatch out for
Partition-parameterized runsThe job takes a date (or range) parameter, reads only those partitions, and overwrites only those output partitions.The most robust option: reruns and backfills are just runs with a different parameter. Requires a partitioned layout.
Glue job bookmarksGlue remembers which S3 objects (by modification time) or JDBC rows (by a key column) were processed and reads only new ones.Needs a transformation_ctx on each read; late-arriving files with old timestamps or in-place file rewrites can be missed; resetting a bookmark reprocesses everything. Upserts, deletes and history on S3

Plain Parquet files on S3 are immutable, so an update means rewriting files. Two ways to cope:

  • Rebuild the affected partitions (above) when changes cluster by date. Simple, but wasteful when changes are scattered across history.
  • Use a table format with row-level operations (Apache Iceberg is the AWS-favored choice, supported by Athena, Glue, EMR, Redshift Spectrum and Firehose). MERGE, UPDATE and DELETE work, with snapshot isolation and time travel (§09).
-- Athena / Iceberg: SCD Type 2 in two steps. Step 1: close the current row for customers whose tracked attributes changed.
MERGE INTO gold.dim_customer t
USING staging.customer_changes s
ON t.customer_id = s.customer_id AND t.is_current = true
WHEN MATCHED AND (t.email <> s.email OR t.tier <> s.tier)
  THEN UPDATE SET is_current = false, valid_to = s.change_ts;

-- Step 2: insert a new current row for every changed or brand-new customer (those with no open row now).
INSERT INTO gold.dim_customer
SELECT s.customer_id, s.email, s.tier, s.change_ts AS valid_from, CAST(NULL AS timestamp) AS valid_to, true AS is_current
FROM staging.customer_changes s
LEFT JOIN gold.dim_customer t ON t.customer_id = s.customer_id AND t.is_current = true
WHERE t.customer_id IS NULL;

SCD Type 1 overwrites the old value (no history); Type 2 keeps every version as a row with validity dates; the choice is a business question about whether anyone needs to ask "what was this customer's tier last March?".

ELT in Redshift: stage, then merge

-- Load a day of clean Parquet into a staging table (parallel, from S3), then upsert into the target.
COPY staging.orders
FROM 's3://lake/silver/orders/order_date=2026-09-23/'
IAM_ROLE 'arn:aws:iam::123456789012:role/RedshiftLoadRole'
FORMAT AS PARQUET;

MERGE INTO analytics.orders
USING staging.orders s ON analytics.orders.order_id = s.order_id
WHEN MATCHED THEN UPDATE SET status = s.status, amount = s.amount, updated_at = s.updated_at
WHEN NOT MATCHED THEN INSERT VALUES (s.order_id, s.status, s.amount, s.updated_at);

TRUNCATE staging.orders;                                     -- staging is disposable: rerun-safe

Late-arriving and out-of-order data

  • Decide how late is too late (a business rule): for example, accept events up to 3 days late.
  • Batch: re-process a rolling window (the last N days) each run, using idempotent partition overwrite or MERGE, so late rows correct earlier results.
  • Streaming: use event-time windows with a watermark (Flink or Spark Structured Streaming); events later than the watermark go to a side output for reconciliation rather than silently dropped.
  • Dimensions arriving after facts: assign the fact to an "unknown" dimension member and repair it when the dimension row lands.

Modeling the curated layer on AWS

Silver is cleaned, typed, de-duplicated, conformed data (often near-normalized). Gold is shaped for reading: star schemas (one fact table at a declared grain plus conformed dimensions with surrogate keys) or wide denormalized tables. In Redshift, model with DISTKEY/SORTKEY aligned to join and filter columns (§15); on S3 with Iceberg or partitioned Parquet, partition by the filter column (usually date) and compact regularly. The modeling theory (grain, facts, dimensions, slowly changing dimensions) is the same as on any platform: see Data Modeling for Data Engineers.

Quality gates and tests between zones

  • Gate each zone promotion with checks (row counts against the source, null keys, duplicate keys, referential integrity, value ranges): Glue Data Quality rules or Deequ (§19). Fail the run, or quarantine bad rows to a side prefix, per rule severity.
  • Reconcile totals across layers (sum of amounts in silver equals bronze minus rejects) and write the result to a metrics table.
  • Unit-test the pure transformation functions without a cluster (§23).

Compaction and table maintenance

Streaming writes and frequent merges create many small files, which slow every reader. Iceberg tables need periodic maintenance:

OPTIMIZE curated.orders REWRITE DATA USING BIN_PACK;         -- compact small files into larger ones
VACUUM curated.orders;                                       -- expire old snapshots and remove orphaned files

Schedule these like any other job (weekly is common), and consider S3 Tables, which provides Iceberg tables with automatic compaction and snapshot management, removing that chore.

Interview angle. "How does your nightly job avoid reprocessing everything, and what happens if it reruns?" Parameterize by date and read only that partition; write with dynamic partition overwrite (or MERGE on a key) so the same run twice yields the same output; keep bronze immutable so a logic bug is fixed by replay; process a rolling window for late data; and add quality gates and reconciliation between zones. Mentioning idempotency, partition pruning and a rerun scenario shows you have operated pipelines, not just written them.

~/aws ❯ SQL without a database

14. Amazon Athena - serverless SQL on S3

Point SQL at files in S3 and get results. No servers, no loading. You pay for the bytes each query scans - so the whole game is scanning less.

Athena is a serverless query engine (Trino/Presto under the hood) that runs SQL directly against S3. It reads table definitions from the Glue Data Catalog - the same tables Glue and Redshift Spectrum use - so a table is just "schema + S3 location + format + partitions". Nothing is copied into Athena; it opens the S3 files, filters, joins and aggregates on the fly, and drops the result set into an S3 query result location you configure.

Tables are external; partitions must be known

CREATE EXTERNAL TABLE silver.orders (
  order_id string, customer_id string, amount double)
PARTITIONED BY (order_date string)
STORED AS PARQUET
LOCATION 's3://lake/silver/orders/';

# Athena only reads partitions it knows about. Three ways to register them:
MSCK REPAIR TABLE silver.orders;                 # scan S3 for date=... folders (slow on big tables)
ALTER TABLE silver.orders ADD PARTITION (order_date='2026-04-01')
  LOCATION 's3://lake/silver/orders/order_date=2026-04-01/';
# or partition projection: tell Athena the pattern and it computes partitions, no registration
TBLPROPERTIES (
  'projection.enabled'='true',
  'projection.order_date.type'='date',
  'projection.order_date.range'='2024-01-01,NOW',
  'projection.order_date.format'='yyyy-MM-dd');

Partition projection (conceptual): instead of storing a partition list, Athena is told the naming pattern and range and derives the folder paths per query. It removes the "run a crawler / MSCK after every load" chore and makes planning instant on tables with tens of thousands of partitions.

The cost model - one idea, everything follows from it

  • Partition pruning - a WHERE order_date = '2026-04-01' reads one folder, not the table. Requires the filter column to be a partition column.
  • Columnar formats - Parquet/ORC let Athena read only the columns your SELECT names. A SELECT sum(amount) over a 60-column table reads one column's bytes. CSV/JSON force a full-row read every time.
  • Compression - Snappy (fast) or Zstd (smaller). Always compress; it cuts scanned bytes and speeds I/O.
  • Avoid SELECT *, and use LIMIT only after filters (it doesn't reduce scan on its own).

CTAS, views, workgroups

  • CTAS (CREATE TABLE AS SELECT) - runs a query and writes the result to S3 as a new Parquet, partitioned table. The standard way to build a Gold table or convert raw CSV to Parquet with pure SQL.
  • Views - saved query text, no storage; handy for a canonical join or a security filter.
  • Workgroups (conceptual) - isolate teams: separate result locations, a per-query or per-workgroup data-scanned limit (a hard cost guardrail), and separate cost reporting.

Athena vs Redshift vs Spark. Athena: ad-hoc SQL over the lake, zero infra, pay-per-scan - best for exploration, light BI, and "query the S3 data as-is." Redshift: a loaded warehouse with indexes-by-design (dist/sort keys), consistently low latency, high concurrency - best for dashboards and heavy repeated analytics. Spark (Glue/EMR): when the work is transformation and pipelines, not interactive queries, or needs Python/ML, not just SQL.

Engine v3 and Iceberg tables

Athena's current engine (v3, based on Trino) adds INSERT INTO, UNLOAD, MERGE, and full support for Apache Iceberg tables. Iceberg turns the read-only lake into something transactional:

CREATE TABLE silver.orders (order_id string, customer_id string, amount double, order_date date)
PARTITIONED BY (order_date)
LOCATION 's3://lake/silver/orders/'
TBLPROPERTIES ('table_type'='ICEBERG', 'format'='parquet');

-- now these work, on the lake, with ACID guarantees:
MERGE INTO silver.orders t USING staging.orders s ON t.order_id = s.order_id
  WHEN MATCHED THEN UPDATE SET amount = s.amount
  WHEN NOT MATCHED THEN INSERT VALUES (s.order_id, s.customer_id, s.amount, s.order_date);

SELECT * FROM silver.orders FOR TIMESTAMP AS OF (current_timestamp - interval '1' day);  -- time travel

Iceberg also removes the "register the partition" chore - it tracks its own file list in a metadata layer, so new data is visible immediately and partition evolution is safe.

CTAS options in full

CREATE TABLE gold.daily_revenue
WITH (
  format = 'PARQUET',
  parquet_compression = 'SNAPPY',
  external_location = 's3://lake/gold/daily_revenue/',
  partitioned_by = ARRAY['order_date'],
  bucketed_by = ARRAY['country'], bucket_count = 16      -- optional: co-locates joins

SELECT order_date, country, sum(amount) revenue FROM silver.orders GROUP BY 1, 2;

CTAS is the standard way to convert raw CSV/JSON to partitioned Parquet, or to build a Gold table - pure SQL, no Spark. Note a plain CREATE TABLE ... AS write is capped at 100 partitions per statement; loop by date, or use Iceberg.

Result reuse, EXPLAIN, and approximate functions

  • Query result reuse - enable it on the workgroup and Athena returns a cached result (up to an age you set) for an identical query, for zero scan cost. Huge for dashboards that re-run the same query.
  • EXPLAIN / EXPLAIN ANALYZE - shows the plan and, with ANALYZE, real rows and bytes per operator; use it to confirm a filter actually pruned partitions.
  • approx_distinct(), approx_percentile() - when "roughly" is fine, they're far cheaper than exact count(distinct) on a big table.

Cost guardrails

Set a per-query data-scanned limit and a per-workgroup limit so one bad SELECT * can't scan a petabyte. Route each team to its own workgroup for separate result locations and separate cost reporting. Tag workgroups for cost allocation.

Errors you'll actually see

HIVE_PARTITION_SCHEMA_MISMATCHA partition's Parquet schema differs from the table's (a column added/renamed). Fix the files, or drop and re-add the partition, or use Iceberg.
HIVE_BAD_DATA / GENERIC_INTERNAL_ERRORA corrupt or wrong-format file under the location. Find it with "$path" in the SELECT and quarantine it.
Query returns 0 rows but data existsPartitions not registered. Run MSCK REPAIR, add them, or switch to partition projection.
SYNTAX_ERROR ... exceeded partition limitA CTAS/INSERT writing > 100 partitions at once. Batch it.

❯ Short version

Athena = serverless SQL on S3 via the Glue Catalog. Tables are external (schema + location + format). Cost = bytes scanned ($5/TB), so: partition by what you filter on (or use projection), store Parquet, compress, and never SELECT *. Use CTAS to build tables, workgroups to cap spend. It's for ad-hoc and light BI; Redshift is for heavy repeated dashboards.

~/aws ❯ the fast warehouse

15. Amazon Redshift - the loaded warehouse

A columnar MPP (massively parallel) database. You load data into it, and its speed depends almost entirely on two choices: how rows are distributed across nodes and how they're sorted inside each node.

Redshift is a data warehouse built for analytics: columnar storage (only the columns a query needs are read), compression on every column, and query work split across many nodes running in parallel. Unlike Athena, data lives in Redshift after a load - which is what buys the consistent low latency and high concurrency dashboards need.

Architecture

  • Leader node (conceptual) - parses SQL, builds the plan, coordinates, and merges results. Runs no user data.
  • Compute nodes - hold the data (split into slices, one per core) and do the scanning, joining and aggregating. More nodes = more parallelism.
  • RA3 nodes (conceptual) - compute with managed storage: hot data on local SSD, the rest transparently on S3, so you size compute and storage independently. The current default node type.
  • Redshift Serverless (conceptual) - no cluster to manage; capacity is measured in RPUs and scales with load, billed per second. Good for spiky or unpredictable usage.

Distribution styles - where each row lives

StyleWhat it doesUse when
KEY (DISTKEY)Rows with the same key value go to the same sliceThe big fact table and the table it's most often joined to - set the same DISTKEY (e.g. customer_id) on both so the join happens locally, no data movement
EVENRound-robin, evenly spreadA table with no dominant join column, or staging data
ALLA full copy of the table on every nodeSmall dimension tables (a few million rows max) joined to everything - every join is local
AUTORedshift picks and may switch as the table growsThe sensible default until you have a measured reason to pin one

A bad DISTKEY causes two problems: skew (one slice holds most rows and becomes the bottleneck) or a broadcast/redistribute step on every join (rows shipped across the network mid-query). Both show up in EXPLAIN as DS_BCAST_INNER / DS_DIST_BOTH.

Sort keys, compression, maintenance

  • SORTKEY - the column data is physically ordered by on disk, almost always a timestamp. Redshift stores min/max per block, so WHERE event_date > ... skips blocks entirely (same idea as partition pruning). Compound sort key for filter-then-filter patterns.
  • Compression (encoding) - per-column; COPY picks good encodings automatically on first load. Smaller blocks = fewer reads.
  • VACUUM (conceptual) - reclaims space from deleted rows and re-sorts new rows into SORTKEY order. ANALYZE (conceptual) - refreshes the statistics the planner uses. Modern Redshift runs both automatically in the background; you rarely call them by hand.
  • WLM (Workload Management, conceptual) - query queues with memory and concurrency limits so a heavy report can't starve the dashboards. Auto WLM handles this for most clusters.

Getting data in and out

# COPY: the ONLY fast way to load - parallel read from S3 across all slices
COPY analytics.orders FROM 's3://lake/gold/orders/'
IAM_ROLE 'arn:aws:iam::111122223333:role/RedshiftCopyRole'
FORMAT AS PARQUET;

# UNLOAD: parallel export back to S3 (Parquet, partitioned)
UNLOAD ('SELECT * FROM analytics.daily_revenue')
TO 's3://lake/exports/daily_revenue/'
IAM_ROLE '...' FORMAT PARQUET PARTITION BY (order_date);

# Spectrum: query S3 without loading, via an external schema on the Glue Catalog
CREATE EXTERNAL SCHEMA lake FROM DATA CATALOG DATABASE 'silver' IAM_ROLE '...';
SELECT * FROM lake.orders WHERE order_date = '2026-04-01';   # joins to native tables too

Never INSERT row by row. COPY from S3 is the load path; single-row inserts are orders of magnitude slower. Redshift Spectrum lets a query reach into S3 (through the same Glue Catalog Athena uses) for cold data, and join it to loaded tables in one statement - the practical bridge between the warehouse and the lake.

Redshift vs Athena vs Snowflake vs Databricks. Athena: no infra, pay-per-scan, best for ad-hoc. Redshift: loaded, tuned by dist/sort keys, best for steady high-concurrency dashboards on AWS. Snowflake: a warehouse with near-zero tuning (no dist/sort keys), instant elastic "virtual warehouses", multi-cloud - trades AWS-nativeness and some cost control for simplicity. Databricks: a Spark-based lakehouse - one platform for ETL, SQL, and ML on open Delta tables, strongest when pipelines and data science share the stack.

COPY, in detail

COPY analytics.orders
FROM 's3://lake/gold/orders/'
IAM_ROLE 'arn:aws:iam::111122223333:role/RedshiftLoadRole'
FORMAT AS PARQUET;                # Parquet: schema + types come from the files

# CSV/JSON load with the options that matter:
COPY analytics.events FROM 's3://lake/raw/events/'
IAM_ROLE '...'
CSV GZIP DELIMITER ','
DATEFORMAT 'auto' TIMEFORMAT 'auto'
MAXERROR 100                      # tolerate up to 100 bad rows, log them
COMPUPDATE ON STATUPDATE ON       # pick column encodings + refresh stats during load
MANIFEST;                         # the S3 path is a manifest file listing the data files

Every COPY reads from S3 in parallel across all slices and commits as one transaction - it either all lands or none does. Load errors land in STL_LOAD_ERRORS with the row and reason. Never load with row-by-row INSERT; it's single-threaded and orders of magnitude slower.

Reading an EXPLAIN plan

The one thing to look for is data movement between nodes mid-query:

DS_DIST_NONENo movement - both sides already co-located on the join key. This is the goal.
DS_DIST_ALL_NONEOne side is DISTSTYLE ALL (copied everywhere) - also fine, for small dimensions.
DS_BCAST_INNERThe inner table is broadcast to every node for this query. OK if it's small; a warning if it isn't - consider DISTSTYLE ALL.
DS_DIST_INNER / DS_DIST_BOTHRows redistributed across the network mid-query - the expensive case. Usually means the join key isn't the DISTKEY on one or both tables.

Diagnostics live in the system tables: SVV_TABLE_INFO (size, skew, unsorted %, whether stats are stale), SVL_QUERY_SUMMARY / SYS_QUERY_HISTORY (per-step rows, bytes, time, is_diskbased for a step that spilled).

AUTO optimization & materialized views

  • AUTO dist/sort - leave a table at DISTSTYLE AUTO and Redshift starts it ALL, switches to EVEN or KEY as it grows and as it observes your joins, and maintains the sort order in the background. Pin a specific style only when you have a measured reason.
  • Automatic table optimization also runs VACUUM, ANALYZE and encoding changes for you during low-load windows - the manual maintenance from older Redshift is mostly gone.
  • Materialized views precompute a heavy join/aggregate and can auto-refresh incrementally; the optimizer can even rewrite a matching query to use one transparently.
  • Concurrency Scaling - spins up transient extra clusters to absorb a spike of read queries so dashboards don't queue; billed per second used, with a monthly free credit.

Spectrum external tables

The usual design: keep the last N months loaded for speed, leave the rest in S3, and union them through a view.

❯ Short version

Redshift = columnar MPP warehouse; you COPY data in from S3. Performance is distribution (co-locate the big join - same DISTKEY on fact + main dimension; ALL for tiny dimensions; EVEN otherwise) plus SORTKEY (usually a date, for block skipping). Serving analytics well

  • Serve curated, aggregated tables. Dashboards should read gold aggregates, not scan raw events. A slow dashboard is almost always a modeling problem, not a chart problem.
  • Match the engine to the concurrency. Athena is priced and throttled per query; it is not designed for hundreds of simultaneous dashboard users. Redshift is.
  • Cache. QuickSight SPICE, Athena result reuse and Redshift result caching turn repeated questions into free ones.
  • Isolate workloads. Athena workgroups and Redshift workload management (or separate serverless workgroups) stop an analyst's heavy query from starving the executive dashboard.
  • Row-level security for multi-tenant or regional access: QuickSight dataset rules, Lake Formation row filters, or Redshift row-level security policies.

Serving machine learning

ML needs consistent features between training and inference. Compute features once in the pipeline, store them (S3/Iceberg for the offline store, SageMaker Feature Store or DynamoDB for online), and reuse the same definitions for both. Point-in-time correctness matters: a training row may only see feature values that existed at its event time, or the model learns from the future. For retrieval-augmented generation, the same pipeline discipline applies to documents: parse and chunk, embed, index in a vector store (OpenSearch or a Bedrock knowledge base), and keep the index in step with the source, including deletions.

Reverse ETL: pushing data back into operational tools

Reverse ETL moves curated data (a customer's lifetime value, a churn score) into the systems where people act on it. It looks easy and is full of production traps:

  • Idempotency: the API will be called again after a partial failure. Send upserts keyed on the target's id, never blind inserts.
  • Diffing: push only what changed since the last successful sync (keep a sync-state table with a payload hash) to respect rate limits.
  • Rate limits and batching: throttle, batch, back off on HTTP 429, and make the job resumable.
  • Audit and privacy: record what was sent and when; send only the fields the destination needs.
  • Ownership: agree with the destination team what the field means (a contract).
# Lambda / Glue Python: push only changed accounts, with idempotent upserts, then record success
def sync(changed_accounts):                                  # rows that differ from the sync-state table
    for batch in chunks(changed_accounts, 200):               # batch to respect API limits
        for attempt in range(5):

17. Security for Data Platforms: Layers, KMS, Lake Formation & Networking

Security is the first undercurrent and the one that cannot be bolted on. IAM mechanics have their own section next; this one is the layered view a design review expects.

Security on AWS is a shared responsibility: AWS secures the infrastructure (data centers, hardware, the services themselves); you secure what you put in and how you configure it (who can access which data, how it is encrypted, how the network is exposed). A data platform concentrates valuable, often regulated data, so a good design applies defense in depth: several independent layers, so that one mistake does not become a breach. The guiding rule is least privilege: every person and every job gets only the access it needs, no more, for no longer than it needs it.

The layers, from identity to audit

Identity and access

  • Roles, not users, for machines. Every Glue job, Lambda, EMR cluster and MWAA environment runs as an IAM role and gets temporary credentials from STS. There are no long-lived access keys to leak (§18).
  • People sign in through IAM Identity Center (federated with your identity provider), with MFA, and receive short-lived sessions mapped to permission sets.
  • Guardrails at the organization level. Service Control Policies cap what any account can do (deny public buckets, deny leaving approved regions, deny disabling CloudTrail), even for administrators.
  • Separate duties by account and environment. Production data accounts are not writable from developer roles; CI assumes a deploy role instead.

Network: keep data traffic off the public internet

  • Private subnets for Glue connections, EMR, MWAA workers, Redshift and RDS. No public IPs.
  • VPC endpoints. Fine-grained data access with Lake Formation

IAM decides who may call an API; it is coarse (a bucket or a prefix). LF-tags (tag-based access control) scale this: tag a column sensitivity=pii, grant a role access to sensitivity=public, and every table you tag the same way is governed automatically.

18. IAM - users, roles, policies & AssumeRole

Every AWS call - a Glue job reading S3, Airflow starting that job - is authorised by IAM. Understand roles and AssumeRole and most "Access Denied" mysteries solve themselves.

The building blocks

TermPlain meaning
UserA permanent identity for a human or a legacy script. Has long-lived access keys (an ID + secret). Minimise these.
GroupA bucket of users that share policies (DataEngineers). Purely for convenience; you can't assign a role to a group.
RoleAn identity with permissions but no permanent credentials. A service (Glue, EMR, Lambda, MWAA) or a user assumes it and gets temporary credentials. This is how almost everything in a data platform authenticates.
PolicyA JSON document listing Allow/Deny on Actions for Resources, optionally with Conditions. Attached to an identity or a resource.

Two directions a policy can face

  • Identity-based policy - attached to a user/group/role. "This role may call glue:StartJobRun and read s3://lake/silver/*."
  • Resource-based policy - attached to the resource itself (an S3 bucket policy, an SQS queue policy, a KMS key policy, a Lambda resource policy). "Account 4444 may read this bucket." Resource-based policies are what make cross-account access possible.

Evaluation: a request is allowed only if there is an explicit Allow and no explicit Deny, across every policy that applies. A Deny always wins.

Role, trust policy, AssumeRole, STS

A role has two policies. Its permissions policy says what the role can do. Its trust policy says who is allowed to assume it - e.g. "the Glue service" (glue.amazonaws.com) or "account 1111's AirflowRole". When something assumes the role, it calls STS (Security Token Service) AssumeRole; STS checks the trust policy and hands back temporary credentials (access key + secret + session token) that expire in an hour or so. Those creds carry the role's permissions. Nothing long-lived is stored anywhere.

Least privilege, in practice

Least privilege = grant exactly the actions and resources a job needs, nothing wider. Concretely: never "Action": "s3:*" on "Resource": "*"; scope to s3:GetObject on arn:aws:s3:::lake/silver/*. One role per job or per pipeline, not one shared "data role". Use Condition keys (source VPC, a tag, TLS-only) to tighten further.

When service A hands service B a role to use ("Glue, run this job as GlueJobRole"; "EMR, launch nodes with this instance profile"), A needs iam:PassRole for that specific role ARN. The anatomy of a policy

  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [{
    "Sid": "ReadSilver",
    "Effect": "Allow",                       # Allow or Deny
    "Action": ["s3:GetObject", "s3:ListBucket"],
    "Resource": [
      "arn:aws:s3:::lake",                    # the bucket, for ListBucket

Managed vs inline: an AWS managed policy is maintained by AWS (AmazonS3ReadOnlyAccess); a customer managed policy is your own reusable one; an inline policy is embedded in a single role and dies with it. Prefer customer-managed - you can see everywhere it's attached.

The full evaluation order

Condition keys a data engineer actually uses

aws:SecureTransportDeny when false → force TLS on a bucket.
aws:SourceVpce / aws:SourceVpcOnly allow S3 access through a specific VPC endpoint - data can't leave the network.
s3:prefixRestrict ListBucket to one prefix so a role can't enumerate the whole bucket.
aws:PrincipalTag / aws:ResourceTag"A role tagged team=finance may only touch resources tagged team=finance" - attribute-based access control.
kms:ViaServiceOnly allow a KMS key to be used through S3 (or Redshift), not directly.

Cross-account, concretely

Account A's Glue job needs to read Account B's bucket. Both sides act: B adds a bucket policy allowing arn:aws:iam::A:role/GlueJobRole; A adds s3:GetObject on that bucket to GlueJobRole's identity policy. Cross-account always needs the grant on both ends - the resource policy opens the door, the identity policy lets the caller walk through. For a third-party vendor assuming a role in your account, also require an sts:ExternalId so a confused-deputy can't reuse the ARN.

Debugging "Access Denied"

  • Run aws sts get-caller-identity inside the job - confirm which role you actually are.
  • Read the error: it usually names the action and resource. Missing s3:ListBucket when you have s3:GetObject is the classic (they're separate actions on separate ARNs).
  • Missing iam:PassRole? The error mentions the role you tried to pass.
  • Denied despite an Allow? Something has an explicit Deny - often an SCP or a bucket policy TLS/VPC condition.

❯ Short version

Roles, not users, run your platform. A role has a permissions policy (what it can do) and a trust policy (who may assume it). Assuming a role means calling STS and getting temporary credentials. Grant least privilege - narrow actions, specific resource ARNs, one role per job. Remember iam:PassRole whenever one service launches another. Resource-based policies (bucket, KMS, queue) enable cross-account access.

Decide per rule what a failure means: warn (publish the result and continue), quarantine (route bad rows to a rejected prefix with the reason, keep good rows flowing), or fail (stop the run when wrong output is worse than none). A quarantine prefix reviewed by a steward is usually the right compromise. Results are published to CloudWatch and EventBridge, so failures alarm like any other pipeline event.

Data contracts and schema evolution

A data contract is an explicit, versioned agreement between producer and consumers: schema, meaning, quality expectations, freshness, ownership, and how changes are announced. It turns "the upstream team changed a column and broke us" from a surprise into a process.

  • Streams: register schemas in the Glue Schema Registry and set a compatibility mode. Backward compatible changes (new optional fields) are accepted; incompatible ones are rejected at the producer, before bad data enters the stream.
  • Batch: keep the contract (a YAML file) in Git next to the pipeline; a CI test checks the producer's sample output against it; ingestion jobs fail or route to quarantine on violations.
  • Organizational half: a named owner, a version number, a deprecation window (for example 30 days' notice for breaking changes), and somewhere consumers are told.

Lineage and impact analysis

Lineage answers two questions: impact before a change ("what breaks if I rename this column?") and root cause after an incident ("which upstream table fed this wrong number?"). On AWS, DataZone/SageMaker lineage captures OpenLineage events from Glue, EMR, Redshift and Airflow. When tooling is missing, a cheap and effective substitute is disciplined run metadata: every job writes a row (job, inputs, outputs, run id, time) to an operational table you can query.

Erasure ("right to be forgotten") is harder than it looks. Deleting a row from an Iceberg table hides it, but old snapshots and files still contain it until snapshots expire and files are rewritten; raw S3 copies, derived aggregates, caches, backups and logs may also hold it. Build a deletion pipeline: a governed request table, a job that fans out over every table and prefix holding the subject key, snapshot expiry and compaction, and an audit record proving completion. For hard cases, crypto-shredding (encrypt each subject's data with its own key and delete the key) makes every copy, including backups, unreadable at once.

Master data: one record per real-world entity

When the same customer exists in CRM, billing and support with different keys and slightly different names, analytics double-counts. Master data management builds a single "golden record": standardize (case, whitespace, address formats), match (deterministic rules on keys, fuzzy matching within blocks), merge with survivorship rules (which source wins per attribute), and publish a mapping from every source key to the master key. AWS Entity Resolution offers managed matching; otherwise implement it in Spark. Treat the mapping table as a governed, versioned dataset.

Ownership: the human side

  • Every governed table has a named owner (accountable for meaning and quality) and a steward or team on call.
  • Organize by domain so ownership follows the business, not the platform team.
  • Provide an access request and approval path (DataZone subscriptions do this) and log decisions.
  • Publish a light governance charter: naming, documentation, PII handling and deprecation standards, owned by a small council.

Interview angle. "How do you make sure data across the company can be trusted?" Ownership and contracts at the source; quality checks at each zone boundary with quarantine; a shared Glue catalog with descriptions and LF-tags for discovery and governance; lineage for impact analysis; retention and defensible deletion for lifecycle; master data where entities span systems; and monitoring to prove it stays true. Say which you would implement first: contracts and quality gates are the cheapest, highest-return start.

Apply DevOps, agile and lean habits to data: build and release quickly and safely, watch continuously, recover fast.

DataOps has three technical pillars: automation (CI/CD, everything as code), observability and monitoring (know when data is late, missing or wrong), and incident response (recover quickly and learn) plus a cultural one: shared ownership, clear communication and blameless learning. It is what an interviewer means by "how do you run this in production?" and it is the difference between a set of scripts and a data service.

Automation: shorten the distance from commit to production

  • Everything as code: infrastructure (CDK, CloudFormation, Terraform), pipeline definitions, IAM, alarms and dashboards, all in Git (§23).
  • Automated gates on every change: lint, unit tests, template validation, a deploy to a dev account and an integration test on real data, contract and data-quality checks.
  • Environment parity: the same templates deployed to dev, staging and prod accounts with different parameters, so "works in dev" means something.
  • Keyless CI: GitHub Actions or CodeBuild assume a deploy role through OIDC, with no long-lived AWS keys stored anywhere.
  • Safe releases: small changes, deploy by role, know the rollback before you ship (Lambda aliases, job versions, previous template).
  • Self-healing where safe: retries with backoff for transient faults, DLQs for poison messages, automatic restart of streaming consumers; never auto-retry a logic bug.

Incident response: the 3 a.m. runbook

1

Detect

An alarm (job failure, freshness SLO breach, anomaly), or worse, a consumer report. Track how often consumers find problems before you do: that ratio measures your monitoring.

2

Triage and contain

Set severity by consumer impact. Stop the spread: pause downstream schedules and dashboard refreshes, or flag the affected tables as suspect. Use lineage or run metadata to find what is affected.

3

Mitigate and recover

Pick the fastest safe fix: rerun a failed task after fixing the cause (Airflow "clear", Step Functions redrive); roll back a bad table write (Iceberg snapshot rollback, S3 versioning restore); redeploy the previous template; or backfill the affected dates from raw with the same idempotent code. Streams can be replayed from a checkpoint within the retention window.

4

Communicate

Tell consumers what is wrong, what is affected, and when to expect a fix; tell them again when resolved. Silence destroys trust faster than the outage.

5

Learn: a blameless postmortem

Write the timeline, the root cause (the system, not a person), what worked, and follow-ups with owners and dates: a missing test, a missing alarm, an unclear contract. Fix the class of problem, not just the instance.

Backfills, reprocessing and change management

  • Design for reprocessing from day one: parameterize jobs by date range, keep raw immutable, make writes idempotent, so a backfill is a normal run with different parameters (§13).
  • Backfill safely: throttle so you do not starve production, use separate compute or a lower priority queue, and refresh downstream aggregates that included the wrong data.
  • Schema changes use expand and contract: add the new column (compatible), migrate consumers, then remove the old one after a deprecation window announced through the contract.
  • Keep a change log (deploy events, migrations) so an incident can be correlated with a release.

Interview angle. "A dashboard shows wrong revenue this morning. Walk me through it." Contain (pause refresh, flag the table); triage with lineage to the first bad table; fix the cause; recover with a rollback or an idempotent backfill from raw; verify against a control total; communicate; then hold a blameless postmortem that adds the missing test or alarm. Stating SLOs, error budgets and the postmortem shows you run data as a service, not a set of scripts.

21. Orchestration: Principles & Choosing MWAA, Step Functions, EventBridge

Orchestration runs the whole pipeline in the right order at the right time. The next section goes deep on MWAA; this one is the tool-independent thinking and the AWS comparison.

Whatever tool you pick, good orchestration rests on the same ideas. Interviewers ask "MWAA or Step Functions?" not to hear a favorite but to hear whether you understand what an orchestrator must provide and which AWS service provides it most cheaply for the situation.

Principles (tool-independent)

  • Dependencies as a DAG. State what must finish before what, with no cycles; independent branches run in parallel.
  • Idempotent, deterministic tasks. Any task can be retried or re-run for a past date and give the same result. This is what makes retries, redrives and backfills safe.
  • Time triggers and event triggers. Schedules for a regular cadence; event triggers (S3 object created, upstream job finished) for data that arrives when it arrives. Avoid polling loops with sleeps.
  • Retries with backoff, timeouts and SLAs. Retry transient faults only; every task has a timeout; a missed SLA raises an alert instead of silence.
  • Backfills are first-class. Running a range of past dates with the same code and parameters must be routine.
  • Separate orchestration from computation. The orchestrator starts and watches work (a Glue job, an EMR step); it should not do heavy processing itself.
  • High availability and visibility. The scheduler is a critical service: it needs redundancy, run history, logs, and alerts on its own health.
  • Keep graphs small and readable. Several small, independently testable pipelines beat one enormous DAG.

Step Functions in practice

A state machine is a graph of states: Task (call a service), Choice (branch), Parallel, Map (loop over items; Distributed Map for very large fan-out such as one item per S3 object or per date), Wait, Succeed/Fail. Two features make it a good data orchestrator: service integrations (start a Glue job, an EMR step, an Athena query, a Lambda, an ECS task, and wait for completion using the .sync pattern with no polling code) and built-in Retry and Catch per state. Standard workflows run up to a year, are exactly-once, and are priced per state transition; Express workflows suit high-volume short work.

Notice the design choices worth saying aloud: the run date is an input (so reruns and backfills are the same machine with different input), the Glue step retries only on task failure with backoff, every failure path ends in an alert, and the quality gate is an explicit state rather than something buried in a script. If a run fails, Step Functions can redrive it from the failed state instead of starting over.

Interview angle. "MWAA or Step Functions?" It depends on the workflow's complexity, the team's skills, and cost shape. Step Functions for AWS-centric, event-driven flows with no servers and pay-per-use; MWAA when workflows are complex, span many systems, need first-class backfills, or the team already runs Airflow; EventBridge for pure triggers. Whichever you choose, tasks must be idempotent, retries bounded, failures alerted, and backfills routine. Stating those requirements shows you are choosing by need, not habit.

~/aws ❯ managed Airflow

22. Amazon MWAA - managed Airflow

You already know Airflow - the DAG model, operators, sensors, TaskFlow, retries, catchup, XCom, Connections and Variables. MWAA is those same concepts with AWS running the scheduler, webserver, workers and database for you. Focus on what changes.

MWAA (Managed Workflows for Apache Airflow) provisions an Airflow deployment inside a VPC you designate, patches and scales it, and ships its logs to CloudWatch. You never touch the scheduler / metadata DB / executor; you hand MWAA an S3 bucket of DAGs and it runs them.

What's the same, what's different

Same as any Airflow (see the Airflow guide)MWAA-specific
DAGs, tasks, operators, sensors, TaskFlow APIDAGs, plugins.zip and requirements.txt are synced from an S3 bucket - deploy = aws s3 cp, no server to push to
Scheduling (cron / timetable), retries, execution_timeoutEditing requirements.txt or plugins.zip triggers an environment rebuild and a short scheduling pause - validate them with the AWS "local-runner" image first
Catchup & backfill semanticsThe executor is a fixed managed Celery fleet; you set min/max workers and an environment class (size tier), not an executor type - autoscaling is on queue depth
Airflow Connections & Variables (in the UI or CLI)Can be backed by AWS Secrets Manager so secrets never live in the metadata DB; AWS API calls need no stored credentials at all - the tasks use the environment's IAM role
XCom for small values between tasksThe Airflow UI is reached over a private or public endpoint you choose; component logs go to CloudWatch Logs, not local files

The one IAM role that matters

The MWAA execution role is what every task runs as. Because there is exactly one per environment, it must carry every permission any DAG needs:

# the execution role's policy, in spirit

MWAA vs Step Functions vs Glue Workflows. Glue Workflows: fine for chaining a few Glue crawlers/jobs, weak on visibility and backfill. Step Functions: AWS-native state machine (JSON), pay-per-transition, great for event-driven AWS-only flows, no built-in backfill. MWAA: Python DAGs, a huge provider ecosystem, mature scheduling / retries / backfill, one UI for everything - the default when a team already thinks in Airflow or the pipeline spans more than AWS.

Troubleshooting

  • A DAG doesn't appear: it failed to parse. Check the DAGProcessing CloudWatch log group - the Airflow UI can't show you a file it never loaded.
  • Tasks stuck queued: workers are maxed. Raise max workers or the environment class; check for long poke-mode sensors eating slots (use reschedule or deferrable).
  • Import errors: a package in requirements.txt didn't install or conflicts - reproduce with the local-runner image before shipping.
  • Access Denied inside a task: add the missing action (or iam:PassRole) to the execution role.

The S3 bucket layout

├── dags/                     # every .py DAG; synced every ~30s
│   └── nightly_pipeline.py
├── plugins.zip               # custom operators/hooks/macros (optional)

Overriding airflow.cfg

You can't edit airflow.cfg directly, but any setting maps to an environment variable you set in the MWAA config: core.parallelism → AIRFLOW__CORE__PARALLELISM, celery.worker_autoscale, core.dagbag_import_timeout, etc. This is how you raise concurrency limits or the DAG parse timeout.

Reaching a private UI

If you chose the private webserver endpoint, the Airflow UI is only reachable from inside the VPC. Options: a bastion host with SSH port-forwarding, AWS Client VPN, or an internal load balancer / VPN back to the corporate network. Generate a login token from the CLI with aws mwaa create-web-login-token.

The wait pattern for long AWS jobs

An operator that starts a Glue or EMR job returns immediately. To make the DAG actually wait for it, either set wait_for_completion=True on the operator, or start it and follow with a sensor (GlueJobSensor, EmrStepSensor). Prefer the deferrable variants (deferrable=True) - they hand the wait to the triggerer and don't hold a worker slot for the hour the Spark job runs. A dozen non-deferrable job-sensors polling in parallel can eat every worker and stall the whole environment.

❯ Short version

MWAA = Airflow you don't operate. Deploy DAGs by copying to an S3 bucket; requirements.txt/plugins.zip changes rebuild the environment. There's one execution role that must hold every permission any DAG needs (plus iam:PassRole). Tasks call AWS with no stored credentials. Logs are in CloudWatch. Use the Amazon provider's Glue / EMR / Lambda operators to orchestrate the compute; choose MWAA over Step Functions / Glue Workflows when you want real backfill and a rich ecosystem.

from pyspark.sql import DataFrame, functions as F

def clean_orders(df: DataFrame) -> DataFrame:
    return (df.where(F.col("order_id").isNotNull())
              .withColumn("order_amount", F.col("order_amount").cast("decimal(12,2)"))
              .dropDuplicates(["order_id"]))

# jobs/bronze_to_silver.py : the shell (Glue entry point)

Technical debt in data systems

It accumulates in recognizable ways: copy-pasted scripts, undocumented business rules, tables nobody dares delete, hard-coded dates and paths, pipelines with no tests, logic duplicated across dashboards. Manage it deliberately: a visible backlog, delete unused datasets using usage and lineage data, refactor when you touch code, and reserve capacity for it. Ignoring it does not save time; it borrows from your future incident rate.

~/aws ❯ design principle 9: FinOps

24. Cost Optimization & FinOps on AWS

Cost is an architectural dimension, managed continuously: measure first, then pull the few levers that matter.

"How would you reduce our AWS data bill by 30%?" is a standard senior question. The instinct to start rewriting code is wrong; the right instinct is to measure, allocate, then optimize. Most of a data platform's bill comes from a handful of jobs, clusters and queries, plus a few structural mistakes (always-on clusters, scans without partitions, traffic through a NAT gateway). Find those first.

The FinOps loop

  1. Make cost visible and attributable. Tag every resource (team, project, environment, pipeline) and activate the tags as cost allocation tags. Use separate accounts per environment or domain. Query the Cost and Usage Report with Athena for anything Cost Explorer cannot slice.
  2. Find the top spenders. Group spend by service, then by tag; the top ten items are usually most of the bill.
  3. Fix structural waste first (below), then tune workloads, then negotiate commitments.
  4. Govern. Budgets with alerts, Cost Anomaly Detection, and policy that prevents the expensive mistake (deny unapproved instance types, require tags).

Structural mistakes that dominate real bills

  • Long-running clusters used for scheduled work. A persistent EMR cluster idling 20 hours a day costs several times a transient cluster. Use transient clusters or serverless.
  • Unpartitioned, uncompressed data queried through Athena. Every query scans everything; converting to partitioned Parquet often cuts scan cost by an order of magnitude.
  • Small files. Millions of tiny objects inflate request costs and slow every engine.
  • S3 traffic through a NAT gateway. A single Spark job in a private subnet reading terabytes can generate a surprising NAT bill. Add the S3 gateway endpoint.
  • Full reprocessing every night where incremental would do (§13).
  • Oversized always-on warehouses or MWAA environments sized for a peak that happens once a month.
  • Forgotten dev and test resources and unattached storage. Tag with an owner and an expiry, and clean up automatically.

The interview question, answered end to end

"My Airflow DAG starts a Glue job. How does AWS authenticate and authorise that?"

1. The MWAA task runs boto3 with no stored keys - it picks up the MWAA execution role's temporary credentials from the environment.
2. Those credentials call glue:StartJobRun. IAM checks the execution role's identity policy allows that action, and that it has iam:PassRole for GlueJobRole (because the DAG tells Glue to run the job as that role).
3. Glue starts the run and assumes GlueJobRole. STS checks that role's trust policy permits glue.amazonaws.com, then issues fresh temporary credentials carrying GlueJobRole's permissions.
4. The job script uses those credentials to read s3://lake/bronze/* and write s3://lake/silver/*. Each S3 call is allowed only if GlueJobRole's S3 policy permits it and the bucket's resource policy doesn't Deny it (e.g. non-TLS).
5. Writing to the SSE-KMS bucket also needs kms:GenerateDataKey on the key. Reading the JDBC secret needs secretsmanager:GetSecretValue.
Every hop is role → STS temporary credentials → API call → policy check. Nothing long-lived, nothing shared.

Framework and system design

What is the data engineering lifecycle, and what are the six undercurrents?

The lifecycle is the path data takes: generation (source systems), ingestion, transformation and serving, with storage underlying all of them. The six undercurrents cut across every stage: security, data management, DataOps, data architecture, orchestration and software engineering. A design is complete only when both the flow and the undercurrents are addressed (§01).

How do you structure an answer to "design a data platform for X"?

Clarify requirements and estimate scale; walk the lifecycle with a concrete AWS component and reason per stage; sweep the six undercurrents; then name failure modes and what would make you revisit the design (§27).

Name principles of good data architecture.

Choose common components wisely; plan for failure; architect for scalability; architecture is leadership; always be architecting; build loosely coupled systems; make reversible decisions; prioritize security; embrace FinOps. Tie each to a concrete choice, such as open formats and IaC for reversibility (§02).

Data warehouse, data lake, lakehouse: what is the difference and when would you use each on AWS?

Warehouse (Redshift): modeled, SQL-first, fast concurrent BI, weak for unstructured data. Lake (S3 + Glue + Athena): any data, cheap, but no transactions or governance by default. Lakehouse (S3 + Iceberg + catalog + Lake Formation): lake storage with ACID tables, time travel and governance, one copy for BI and ML. Most AWS platforms are a lakehouse with Redshift on top for the hottest, most concurrent workloads.

Lambda vs. Kappa architecture?

Lambda keeps separate batch and speed layers merged at query time (accurate plus fast, but two code paths). Kappa treats everything as a stream and reprocesses by replay (one code path, needs a durable log and stateful streaming skills). On AWS, Kinesis or MSK as the log plus Flink is Kappa-style; Glue/EMR batch plus a streaming layer is Lambda-style. Choose separate paths only if real-time and batch have genuinely different definitions of correctness.

How do you decide between batch and streaming?

Ask what decision depends on the freshness. If minutes-old data changes nothing, micro-batch or hourly batch is cheaper and simpler. Choose streaming only when a latency requirement justifies always-on compute and stateful complexity. It is a continuum: Firehose with a 60-second buffer already gives near-real-time without Flink.

How do you design for failure and disaster recovery?

Get RTO and RPO from the business first. Make pipelines idempotent, keep raw immutable for replay, use multi-AZ services, enable S3 versioning and cross-region replication, and define everything in infrastructure as code so a second region can be rebuilt. Pick a strategy (backup and restore, pilot light, warm standby, active-active) by cost versus recovery target, and rehearse it (§02).

What is a data mesh, and how would you build one on AWS?

Domain teams own data as products on a self-serve platform, with federated governance. On AWS: producer accounts per domain, a central governance account with the Glue catalog and Lake Formation (LF-tag policies), sharing through cross-account grants and resource links, DataZone for discovery and access requests, and SCPs and templates as guardrails. It needs organizational maturity; do not adopt it for the name alone.

Ingestion

What questions do you ask about a source system before ingesting from it?

Who owns it and how changes are announced; append-only or mutable; a reliable change signal or log; volume and velocity; delivery guarantees (duplicates, ordering); event vs. ingestion time and time zone; tolerable load and whether a replica exists; whether history can be replayed; PII; schema stability (§03).

Ways to do CDC, and which do you prefer?

Log-based (reads the database log: low impact, sees deletes; DMS), query-based on a timestamp or version column (simple, misses deletes), trigger-based (adds source load), snapshot diff (heavy). Prefer log-based via DMS, or zero-ETL when the target is Redshift; fall back to a watermark pull from a replica for simple tables.

How do you get effectively-once results when sources deliver at least once?

Make writes idempotent: MERGE on a business key with a sequence column, partition overwrite, or dedupe on an event id within a watermark. At-least-once delivery plus an idempotent sink behaves like exactly-once from the consumer's view. Even S3 event notifications and DMS can duplicate.

Kinesis Data Streams vs. Firehose vs. MSK?

Kinesis Data Streams: a durable, ordered log you consume with your own code or Flink (shards, 1 MB/s in each, retention up to a year). Firehose: managed delivery of a stream to S3, Redshift, OpenSearch or Iceberg with buffering and format conversion; near real time, no consumer code. MSK: managed Kafka for teams that need the Kafka ecosystem, larger messages or existing clients.

How do you choose a Kinesis partition key and avoid hot shards?

Use a high-cardinality key that spreads load evenly but keeps related events together when ordering matters (for example user id). A skewed key (country, a single tenant) concentrates traffic on one shard and throttles it. Watch per-shard metrics, add random suffixes for pure throughput, or use on-demand mode.

An S3 upload triggers your Lambda twice. What do you do?

S3 notifications are at-least-once, so make the handler idempotent: derive a deterministic id from the object (bucket, key, ETag or version id), use it as a Step Functions execution name or a conditional write to a tracking table, and ignore duplicates. Also ignore partial files (write to a temp name then rename, or send a success marker last).

Streaming, messaging and CDC

Stream, queue, topic, event bus: what is the difference?

A stream (Kinesis, Kafka) is a replayable log that many consumers read at their own positions. A queue (SQS) hands each message to one consumer and deletes it. A topic (SNS) pushes each message to every subscriber. An event bus (EventBridge) routes events to targets by content and can schedule. Choose by whether you need replay and ordering (stream), work distribution (queue), fan-out (topic) or routing (bus) (§08).

What is event time versus processing time, and why does it matter?

Event time is when the thing happened; processing time is when your job saw it. Late, retried and out-of-order data make them differ, so aggregating on processing time gives results that change with load and outages. Aggregate on event time, use a watermark to decide when a window is complete, and send events that arrive after that to a side output for reconciliation (§07).

What is a watermark?

The engine's estimate that no more events older than time T are coming, typically the maximum event time seen minus an allowed lateness. When it passes a window's end the window closes and emits. A larger lateness gives more complete but later results; a smaller one gives faster results and more late events to handle.

Is exactly-once real in streaming?

Delivery from sources is at-least-once. Engines such as Flink and Spark checkpoint state and source positions, so a restart replays from a consistent point; with an idempotent or transactional sink (Iceberg commits, Kafka transactions, MERGE on a key) the visible result is exactly-once. Side effects outside that boundary, like sending an email, still need their own idempotency key.

Consumer lag keeps growing. What do you check?

Compare arrival rate with processing rate. Look for a slow sink (batch the writes, add capacity), too little parallelism (more shards or partitions, more consumers, Lambda parallelization factor), a hot key or partition, long garbage-collection pauses or rebalances, backpressure from downstream, or a poison record retried forever. Watch iterator age on Kinesis and consumer-group lag on Kafka, and alarm before users notice (§05, §06).

How do you handle a poison message?

Bound retries, then move it aside: a dead-letter queue for SQS, an on-failure destination and bisect-on-error for Lambda on Kinesis or Kafka, a dead-letter topic in Kafka. Alarm on the dead-letter depth, keep the payload and error for diagnosis, fix the cause and redrive. Without bounded retries one bad record can block an entire shard or partition.

How do you choose the number of shards or partitions?

From the numbers. Kinesis: peak MB/s divided by 1 MB/s per shard, also checking records per second, or use on-demand. Kafka: enough partitions for the consumer parallelism you will need at peak, with headroom, while remembering you cannot reduce them and that more partitions cost metadata and recovery time. In both, a skewed key defeats the count.

Flink or Spark Structured Streaming?

Flink for event-at-a-time processing with low latency and rich state and time control (alerts, fraud, live features); Spark Structured Streaming when seconds-to-minutes latency is fine and the team wants one Spark codebase for batch and streaming (Glue streaming, EMR). On AWS, Managed Service for Apache Flink removes the cluster.

How do you land a stream in S3 without creating millions of small files?

Buffer before writing: Firehose with a size of 64 to 128 MB and a 60 to 300 second interval, or a longer Flink checkpoint or Spark trigger interval. Convert to Parquet, partition on the query column, write to Iceberg, and run scheduled compaction. If a minute of delay is acceptable, Firehose to Iceberg needs no processing engine at all.

How would you replay or backfill a streaming pipeline after a bug?

Fix the code, start a new job from an earlier offset or timestamp (or from raw S3 if the stream's retention has passed), write to a new table or version, compare against the old output, then switch consumers. This needs replayable sources, deterministic logic and idempotent sinks, which is why raw data is kept immutable.

DMS or Debezium for CDC?

DMS: managed, files in S3 or a stream, simple to run, good for database-to-lake copies and migrations. Debezium on MSK Connect: Kafka-native, before and after images, several real-time consumers, more to operate. Use zero-ETL when the warehouse is the only consumer and current state is enough (§04).

What can go wrong with log-based CDC from Postgres?

The replication slot retains WAL until the consumer confirms it, so a stopped consumer fills the source database's disk. Tables without primary keys cannot be merged safely, large transactions cause bursts, schema changes break consumers, and silent gaps happen if a task is restarted wrongly. Alarm on slot lag and log disk usage, require keys, use a data contract, and reconcile counts on a schedule.

SQS, SNS or EventBridge for "a file landed, three things must happen"?

Publish the event to SNS and give each consumer its own SQS queue with a dead-letter queue (fan-out plus per-consumer durability). EventBridge is the alternative when routing by content or reacting to AWS service events; use it to start the flow. Do not chain Lambdas: one slow or failing step then blocks the others.

Storage: S3 and table formats

How do you lay out data in S3 for performance and cost?

Columnar Parquet, partitioned by the column you filter on (usually date, low cardinality), files of roughly 128 MB to 1 GB, lifecycle rules by prefix, and separate zones for raw, silver and gold. Avoid high-cardinality partitions and the small-files problem (§09).

What is the small-file problem, and how do you fix it?

Thousands of tiny objects make every listing, open and task slow, and inflate request costs. Fix by buffering on ingest (Firehose hints), coalescing on write, and scheduled compaction (Iceberg OPTIMIZE or a compaction job). S3 Tables automates compaction.

Why use Iceberg on S3 instead of plain Parquet?

Plain Parquet files are immutable and unmanaged. Iceberg adds ACID transactions, row-level MERGE/UPDATE/DELETE, schema and partition evolution, snapshot isolation and time travel, with metadata that lets engines skip files efficiently. It is supported by Athena, Glue, EMR, Redshift Spectrum and Firehose, and keeps the data open (reversible choice).

How do S3 storage classes and lifecycle policies reduce cost?

Standard for hot data; Intelligent-Tiering when access patterns are unknown; Standard-IA for older, occasionally read partitions; Glacier tiers for archives. Lifecycle rules move or expire objects by prefix and age and clean up old versions and incomplete multipart uploads. Retrieval fees and minimum durations mean cold tiers only pay off for genuinely cold data.

Transformation: Glue, EMR, Lambda

Glue vs. EMR vs. Lambda: how do you choose?

Lambda for small event-driven steps (under 15 minutes, no big data). Glue for scheduled Spark ETL from gigabytes to hundreds of gigabytes with no cluster to run. EMR (or EMR Serverless) for very large or long jobs, custom libraries, fine tuning and Spot savings. Redshift or Athena SQL when the work is relational and data is already there (§10, §11, §13).

How does your nightly job avoid reprocessing everything, and what happens on a rerun?

Parameterize by date and read only that partition; write with dynamic partition overwrite or MERGE so the same run twice gives the same output; keep raw immutable so a logic bug is fixed by replay; process a rolling window for late data. Glue bookmarks are a convenience, but an explicit date parameter is more transparent and safer for backfills.

How do you handle late-arriving data?

Decide how late is too late (a business rule). Batch: reprocess a rolling window each run with idempotent overwrite or MERGE. Streaming: event-time windows with a watermark, sending later events to a side output for reconciliation. Facts arriving before dimensions go to an "unknown" member and are repaired later.

How do you implement SCD Type 2 on the lake?

With an Iceberg table and two statements: MERGE to close the current row (set is_current = false, valid_to) where tracked attributes changed, then INSERT a new current row for changed and new keys. Use Type 1 (overwrite) when nobody needs history.

A Glue job is slow. How do you diagnose it?

Check the Spark UI and job metrics: data skew (a few tasks much slower), shuffle spill, too few or too many partitions, small input files, no partition pruning, reading unneeded columns, Python UDFs. Fix the cause (broadcast small tables, repartition, salt skewed keys, push filters, compact inputs) before adding workers; then right-size the worker type or enable auto scaling.

Serving: Athena and Redshift

Athena or Redshift?

Athena for occasional, ad hoc SQL over S3 with no infrastructure, priced per TB scanned. Redshift for many concurrent users, steady load and low latency dashboards. Spectrum lets Redshift query cold S3 data. It is often both: Athena for exploration, Redshift for the executive dashboard (§14, §15).

How do you make Athena queries cheaper and faster?

Parquet or ORC, partition on filter columns (with partition projection for many partitions), select only needed columns, compress, avoid tiny files, use CTAS to precompute, workgroup data-scanned limits, and result reuse. Cost follows bytes scanned, so anything that reduces scanning reduces cost.

How do you design a Redshift table (distribution and sort keys)?

DISTKEY on the join column of the largest joined tables so matching rows co-locate; DISTSTYLE ALL for small dimensions; SORTKEY on the common filter column (often a date) so blocks can be skipped; let AUTO decide when unsure. Load with COPY from Parquet, never row-by-row INSERT.

What are the pitfalls of reverse ETL?

Non-idempotent writes after partial failure, flooding the target with unchanged records, rate limits, missing audit trail, sending more personal data than needed, unclear ownership of the field. Use keyed upserts, diff against a sync-state table, throttle with backoff, log what was sent (§16).

Security and IAM

How do you secure a data lake on AWS?

Layered: roles and Identity Center plus SCPs for identity; KMS encryption, TLS enforcement, Block Public Access, versioning and Object Lock for data; private subnets and VPC endpoints for network; Lake Formation column and row controls with LF-tags for fine-grained access; Secrets Manager for credentials; CloudTrail, Config, GuardDuty and Macie for audit and detection. A request must pass every layer (§17).

My Airflow DAG starts a Glue job. How does AWS authenticate and authorize it?

The MWAA task uses the execution role's temporary credentials; IAM checks it may call glue:StartJobRun and iam:PassRole for the job role; Glue assumes the job role (trust policy allows glue.amazonaws.com) and gets fresh STS credentials; each S3 call must be allowed by the role's policy and not denied by the bucket policy; SSE-KMS adds kms:GenerateDataKey. Full trace in §26.

IAM policy vs. bucket policy vs. Lake Formation: who wins?

All that apply are evaluated: an explicit Deny anywhere wins; otherwise access needs an Allow. Identity policies say what a principal can do; bucket policies say who can touch the bucket (and enable cross-account access); Lake Formation adds table, column and row permissions on cataloged data. For Lake Formation-governed data, engines get access through Lake Formation-vended credentials.

How do you give another AWS account access to a table?

Share through Lake Formation (cross-account grant on the Glue database or table, optionally with column/row filters, using AWS RAM); the consumer creates a resource link and its principals get local grants. For raw files, a bucket policy plus the consumer's IAM policy (and KMS key policy if encrypted) all have to allow it (§18).

How do you handle PII in the pipeline?

Minimize what you ingest; classify (Macie, tags); protect per column (encryption, tokenization or salted hashing, masking or generalization); control with Lake Formation and Redshift masking; monitor with CloudTrail; delete on request across every copy with an erasure pipeline (§19).

Why do NAT gateways show up in cost reviews?

They charge per hour and per GB processed. A Spark job in a private subnet reading terabytes from S3 through a NAT gateway generates a large, avoidable bill. An S3 gateway endpoint is free and keeps that traffic on the AWS network.

Data management and quality

How do you make sure data can be trusted across the company?

Ownership and contracts at the source; quality checks at each zone boundary with quarantine; a shared catalog with descriptions and LF-tags; lineage for impact analysis; retention and defensible deletion; master data where entities span systems; monitoring to prove it stays true (§19).

What is a data contract and how do you enforce it on AWS?

A versioned agreement between producer and consumers on schema, semantics, quality, freshness, ownership and change policy. Enforce with the Glue Schema Registry (compatibility modes) for streams, schema and DQ checks at ingestion for batch, contract tests in CI, and monitoring in production, backed by a named owner and a deprecation window.

How do you use Glue Data Quality?

Write DQDL rules (completeness, uniqueness, value ranges, freshness, row counts) on a catalog table or inside a Glue job, run them at zone boundaries, publish scores and failures to CloudWatch/EventBridge, and decide per rule whether a failure warns, quarantines rows or fails the run. Anomaly detection can learn baselines for volume.

How do you handle a GDPR deletion request in a data lake?

Delete the subject from every curated table (Iceberg delete), remove or crypto-shred raw copies, expire snapshots and compact so old files are physically removed, handle derived aggregates, caches and backups, and record completion in an audit table, all inside the deadline. Design for it up front with a governed request table and a fan-out job.

Orchestration and DataOps

MWAA or Step Functions?

Step Functions for AWS-centric, event-driven flows with no servers and pay-per-use; MWAA for complex, multi-system workflows with first-class backfills or when the team already runs Airflow; EventBridge for pure triggers. Either way tasks must be idempotent, retries bounded, failures alerted and backfills routine (§21).

What are the principles of good orchestration?

Dependencies as a DAG; idempotent tasks; time and event triggers; retries with backoff and timeouts; backfills as first-class; orchestration separated from computation; a highly available, observable scheduler; small readable graphs.

What are data SLIs and SLOs? Give an example.

An SLI is a measurement (freshness, completeness, validity, pipeline success rate, query latency); an SLO is the agreed target, for example "gold sales is under 6 hours old 99% of the time." The error budget governs whether the team ships features or spends time on reliability. Measure freshness with a scheduled Lambda publishing a CloudWatch metric and alarm on breach (§20).

A pipeline failed at 3 a.m. and a dashboard is wrong. What do you do?

Contain (pause downstream refresh, flag the table), triage with lineage or run metadata to the first bad table, fix the cause, recover by rerunning idempotently, rolling back the table (Iceberg snapshot, S3 versioning) or backfilling from raw, verify against a control total, communicate to consumers, and hold a blameless postmortem that adds the missing test or alarm.

How do you monitor pipelines on AWS?

CloudWatch metrics and alarms (built-in job metrics plus custom freshness and volume metrics), Logs Insights, EventBridge rules on state changes (Glue failed, DQ failed) routed to SNS or chat, DLQ depth and stream iterator-age alarms, Cost Anomaly Detection, and dashboards that show freshness against SLO.

Open source and tooling

When would you choose open source over an AWS-managed service?

For a named reason: a capability the managed service lacks, a portability or exit requirement, an ecosystem the team already knows, or a cost curve that favors it at scale. Even then, run it on a managed substrate (MSK, MWAA, EMR, EKS) before self-hosting, and check the license. The default is the managed AWS service (§25).

Athena or Trino?

Athena is serverless Trino-based SQL on S3 priced per data scanned, ideal for occasional and ad hoc queries. Run Trino yourself (EMR or EKS) for very high query volumes where per-query pricing hurts, custom connectors or federation across many sources, and tighter control of resources, accepting the operations.

Where does dbt fit on AWS?

dbt is the transformation and testing layer for SQL models: it compiles models to SQL and runs them on an engine (Athena, Redshift or Glue through adapters), adding tests, documentation, lineage and code review. Run it from MWAA, Step Functions with a container, or CI. It replaces hand-written SQL scripts, not the engine underneath.

Iceberg, Delta Lake or Hudi on AWS?

Iceberg is the AWS-native default: Athena, Glue, EMR, Redshift and Firehose support it, and S3 Tables manage it. Delta when Databricks is the main engine. Hudi has strengths in record-level upserts on EMR. All three add ACID, schema evolution and time travel over Parquet; open formats keep the engines swappable.

Software engineering and cost

What does good software engineering look like in a data team?

Pure, unit-tested transformation functions with thin I/O shells; Git with pull requests and review; lint, type checks and tests as CI gates; all infrastructure in CDK or Terraform deployed through keyless OIDC roles; config instead of hard-coded values; idempotent writes; structured logging with a run id; versioned artifacts and aliases for rollback (§23).

How do you deploy to AWS from GitHub Actions without storing keys?

Configure an IAM OIDC identity provider and a deploy role whose trust policy allows only your repository and branch. The workflow uses aws-actions/configure-aws-credentials to assume the role and receive short-lived credentials. No long-lived access keys exist to leak.

CloudFormation, CDK or Terraform?

CloudFormation is native and verbose; CDK lets you write infrastructure in a real language with reusable constructs and synthesizes to CloudFormation; Terraform is multi-cloud with a mature module ecosystem and a state file. Choose by team skills and estate; what matters more is that nothing in production is created by hand.

Cut our data platform bill by 30%. Where do you start?

Measure and attribute first (tags, Cost Explorer, CUR in Athena), list the top ten cost items, fix structural waste (persistent clusters, unpartitioned Athena scans, small files, NAT traffic to S3), right-size and use Spot or serverless where they fit, make jobs incremental, then add budgets, anomaly detection and guardrails (§24).

❯ The whole guide in a few lines

Frame every design with the lifecycle (generation, ingestion, storage, transformation, serving) and sweep the six undercurrents (security, data management, DataOps, data architecture, orchestration, software engineering). S3 holds everything as Parquet or Iceberg in Bronze/Silver/Gold zones. Ingest with DMS or Debezium (CDC), AppFlow, and streams (Kinesis, MSK) landed by Firehose or processed by Flink; transform with Glue (everyday Spark), EMR (big or specialized jobs) and SQL engines; serve with Athena, Redshift and purpose-built stores. MWAA or Step Functions sequence and retry; IAM roles plus STS credentials, KMS and Lake Formation secure every step. Reach for open source (Kafka, Flink, Iceberg, dbt, Airflow) on managed AWS substrates when a named need justifies it. Make every task idempotent, keep raw immutable for replay, measure freshness against an SLO, deploy through code, and state the trade-offs and failure modes out loud.
