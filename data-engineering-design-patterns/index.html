Seventy-seven named, reusable solutions to the problems every pipeline eventually hits — how to load data, what to do when a record is unprocessable, how to make a re-run safe, where to put bytes on disk, and how to prove any of it worked. A complete walk through Bartosz Konieczny's Data Engineering Design Patterns (O'Reilly), in plain English, from zero.

Almost every pipeline you will ever write has been written before. Not the business logic — that part is yours — but the shape: the loader that only picks up new rows, the try/catch that parks a poisoned record instead of killing the job, the MERGE that makes a re-run produce the same table instead of doubling it. Those shapes have names. Once you know the names, design conversations get shorter and code reviews get sharper, because "make this idempotent" — make it safe to run twice — stops being a vibe and becomes a choice between seven specific patterns with seven specific costs.

This guide walks through all of them. It reorganizes Data Engineering Design Patterns by Bartosz Konieczny (O'Reilly) — eleven chapters, ten pattern families, seventy-seven patterns — into one reference you can read front-to-back or dip into when something breaks. Every pattern in the book is here. The explanations, examples, diagrams and code are my own; where I disagree with an emphasis or think something needs more warning, I say so.

01.What a "data engineering design pattern" actually is

And why the Gang of Four patterns you already know are not enough

Think about a chess opening. The Queen's Gambit is not a rule you must obey and not a program that plays itself. It is a named, tested, adjustable template: a sequence of moves that responds to a specific situation, that thousands of players have already stress-tested for you, that you can deviate from once you understand why each move is there — and that commits you to consequences, in this case a particular pawn structure you will live with for the rest of the game. Its biggest practical benefit is not even the moves. It is that two players can say "Queen's Gambit" and skip five minutes of describing positions.

A design pattern is exactly that, for engineering. It has four parts: a situation it applies to, a template you adapt rather than copy, reusability across projects that look nothing alike, and consequences you accept on purpose. Plus the shared vocabulary, which is where most of the day-to-day value comes from.

Here is the concrete version. Your job reads semi-structured events and once a week hits a record so malformed that parsing it throws and the whole job dies. You wrap the risky parse in a try/catch, count the failures, and write the bad records somewhere else for later analysis. That is the situation, the template, and — because you have added a second write path and a second thing to monitor — the consequence. That pattern has a name: Dead-Letter. Once you know the name, the next time you meet the same problem in a SQL-only ELT pipeline you already know what you are looking for, even though none of the code will transfer.

So why aren't the software patterns enough?

If you write code you have probably met the classic object-oriented catalogue — Singleton, Factory, Adapter, Template Method, and the rest. Those patterns are about keeping a codebase maintainable. They are genuinely useful in data work too, and you will see two or three of them show up later in this guide as supporting cast.

But they say nothing about the problems that actually wake data engineers up at night, because those problems are not about code shape. They are about the data and the passage of time:

• A task was retried automatically. Did it just write the same 40 million rows twice?

• A provider re-sent yesterday's file with corrections. Do you re-run one partition or fourteen?

• An event arrives with a timestamp of 09:12 — at 11:40. Is it late data, or is it garbage?

• A column silently changed type. Whose fault is it, and which of your six downstream consumers cares?

• The job succeeded, wrote nothing, and nobody noticed for a week.

No amount of clean architecture answers those. They need a second catalogue, which is what this book supplies.

Clean code keeps the pipeline maintainable. Data patterns keep the pipeline correct — under retries, backfills, late arrivals and schema drift.

The ten families, mapped onto a pipeline

The book is organised as a walk down a pipeline: bring data in, survive the errors, make re-runs safe, add value, wire the flow together, secure it, lay it out on disk, prove its quality, watch it in production, and then do all of it again for streams. Ten families sit on that walk. Four attach to a particular stage; the rest cut across every stage, which is exactly why they are easy to forget until they bite.

The running example used throughout this guide

Patterns are hard to hold onto in the abstract, so everything below is explained against one imaginary system: a parcel-tracking analytics platform. It has the three parts almost every real platform has.

Producers, online and offline. Handheld scanners in vans and sorting hubs emit a scan event every time a parcel is picked up, sorted, loaded or delivered — thousands per second, straight into a streaming broker. Separately, slower reference datasets arrive on a schedule from internal systems: the couriers table, the hubs table, the service_levels table. Those change a few times a week, not a few thousand times a second.

A real-time layer. Streaming jobs read the scan events. Some are business-facing (a live "parcels stuck at hub" view). Some are purely technical plumbing (copying events to at-rest storage so batch jobs can query them).

A layered lakehouse. Data lands in three tiers, the now-standard medallion split. Bronze is raw and untouched, quality problems included. Silver is cleaned, deduplicated and enriched. Gold is the shape consumers actually asked for: marts, aggregates, reference datasets. The tiers matter here because patterns land in different tiers — a Dead-Letter store lives in Bronze, a sessionizer writes Gold — and knowing which tier you are in usually tells you how much you are allowed to break.

Two words used constantly below

Backfilling — re-running a pipeline for a past time period, whether or not that period was ever processed before. Strictly speaking, re-processing already-processed data and processing never-processed data are different things, but the operational pain is identical, so this guide calls both backfilling.

Idempotent — running it twice produces the same result as running it once. abs(-3) is idempotent: abs(abs(abs(-3))) is still 3. A pipeline is idempotent when a re-run leaves the target table looking the way one clean run would have left it.

Rule of thumb: every automatic retry is a backfill you didn't schedule. If retries are on and the pipeline is not idempotent, you already have a duplicate problem — you just haven't found it yet.

02.Data Ingestion — eight patterns for the first mile

Full Loader · Incremental Loader · Change Data Capture · Passthrough Replicator · Transformation Replicator · Compactor · Readiness Marker · External Trigger

Data platforms almost never generate their own data. The first technical act is always acquisition, from producers you do not control: another team, a legacy service, a third-party API, a vendor dropping files in a bucket. Each producer has its own constraints and its own idea of what "done" means, and you have to adapt to all of them, because the alternative is having no data.

Ingestion looks like the boring part. It is where a surprising share of production incidents actually start — an incomplete dataset published as if it were complete, a re-run that doubled a table, a bucket that accumulated nine million tiny files until every downstream job spent most of its time listing them.

Loading: how much of the dataset do you take?

Full Loader

Use it when the source gives you no reliable way to tell what changed, and the dataset is small enough or slow-moving enough that reading all of it is cheap.

Our couriers reference table changes a handful of times a week, has under two million rows, and — critically — has no updated_at, no version counter, nothing. There is no question to ask other than "give me everything".

The simplest form is two steps, extract and load, with no transformation in between. That works when source and target are the same kind of store, and it is often literally one command. When they are not the same kind of store you need a thin translation layer between the two steps, which turns the job into a classic ETL and usually means reaching for a processing framework that speaks both ends.

Watch out. Volume drift — a full load on fixed-size compute is fine until the source doubles overnight, then it is slow, then it fails; autoscaling compute is the cheap insurance. Consistency during the swap — the tempting implementation is DELETE then INSERT, which means any reader arriving mid-load sees a half-empty table, or an empty one. Wrap it in a transaction; if your store has no transactions, load into a versioned physical table and repoint a view at it. No undo — a full overwrite destroys the previous version unless your format keeps history (Delta Lake, Iceberg, BigQuery time travel) or you keep the old versioned table around.

-- Full Loader without transactions: load into a versioned table,
-- then flip one view. Readers never see a partial dataset.
CREATE TABLE couriers_v20260820 (LIKE couriers_current);
COPY couriers_v20260820 FROM '/landing/couriers.csv' CSV HEADER;

-- exposition step: atomic from the reader's point of view
CREATE OR REPLACE VIEW couriers AS SELECT * FROM couriers_v20260820;

Incremental Loader

Use it when the dataset grows continuously and you only want the part that appeared since last time.

There are two shapes, and picking the wrong one causes most of the pain.

Delta column. The source has a column that only moves forward — an ingestion timestamp, an auto-increment id, a version number — and you read rows above your last high-water mark. Simple, works on any queryable source, and requires the job to remember where it got to.

Time partitions. The source is already physically split by time, so "what is new" is a directory or a partition name, not a filter. This is strictly nicer: the source has pre-filtered the data for you, and the partition to read is derivable from the run's own scheduled timestamp, so there is no state to keep. Pair it with the Readiness Marker pattern so you never read a partition that is still being written.

Watch out. Hard deletes are invisible. A delta column can show you an updated row; it cannot show you a row that no longer exists, because deleted rows have no column values at all. Either ask the producer for soft deletes (an is_deleted flag set by an UPDATE) or accept an insert-only source and push reconstruction onto consumers. Backfills silently become full loads. Replay a delta-column job after two months and its "everything above the mark" query returns two months of data, on compute sized for one hour. Fix it by bounding both ends of the window. Never use event time as the delta column — late data arrives below your mark and you will never see it.

-- Bad: unbounded. A replay reads everything since the mark.
WHERE ingested_at > :last_mark

-- Good: bounded on both sides. Volume per run is now predictable,
-- a replay reads exactly what the original run read, and several
-- backfill runs can execute concurrently without overlapping.
WHERE ingested_at >= :run_start
  AND ingested_at <  :run_start + INTERVAL '1 hour'

Change Data Capture (CDC)

Use it when you need lower latency than a scheduled query can give you, or when you need to see hard deletes.

The Incremental Loader has two costs baked in: the scheduler's interval and the query's own execution time. If the requirement is "every change visible to consumers within thirty seconds", neither is negotiable. CDC sidesteps both by not querying the table at all.

Instead it reads the database's commit log — the append-only journal the engine already writes for durability and replication, where every insert, update and delete is recorded in order. A CDC client tails that log and republishes each change, usually onto a streaming topic named after the source table. Consumers then choose what to do with it: keep the whole change history, or collapse it to the latest value per key.

Because the log records deletes as first-class events, CDC also solves the hard-delete blindness of the Incremental Loader — no soft-delete negotiation required.

Watch out. Setup is not a data-engineer-only job. Full and incremental loads need nothing from anyone; CDC needs logical replication enabled, an output plugin installed and a replication-privileged user — plan for the ops or DBA conversation. Scope — many clients can only see changes made after they started, so the historical backfill is a separate job using one of the earlier patterns. Payload shape changes — you get operation type, commit version, commit timestamp and often before/after images alongside the data, and your logic has to ignore what it does not need. Semantics change. This is the subtle one: rows that were at rest are now in motion. In a batch join, no match means no matching row. In a stream-to-stream join, no match may only mean not yet.

# Lake-native CDC is much cheaper to set up: no server-side plumbing,
# just a table property and a stream reader.
spark.sql("""ALTER TABLE bronze.parcels
             SET TBLPROPERTIES (delta.enableChangeDataFeed = true)""")

changes = (spark.readStream.format("delta")
    .option("readChangeFeed", "true")
    .option("startingVersion", "0")
    .option("maxFilesPerTrigger", "8")   # throttle the replay
    .table("bronze.parcels"))

# changes now carries _change_type, _commit_version, _commit_timestamp
# on top of your own columns.

Replication: the same dataset, somewhere else

Replication and loading look like the same thing and are not quite. Replication moves data between two stores of the same kind and tries to preserve everything, including metadata that is easy to forget — primary keys, message headers, offsets, ordering. Loading is looser and has no such homogeneity requirement. The distinction matters because replication's failure mode is "the copy is subtly not the original", which is exactly the thing you were trying to avoid.

Passthrough Replicator

Use it when you need the same data in another environment and the process that produced it cannot simply be re-run there.

Concretely: our production job builds the service_levels reference dataset by calling a third-party API that is not idempotent — ask it the same question twice on the same day and you may get two different answers. So you cannot recreate the dataset in staging by re-running the pipeline. You have to copy the bytes.

Two places to implement it. At the compute level it is an extract-and-load job with nothing in the middle. At the infrastructure level it is a replication policy on the storage service, and the provider does the copying for you.

Watch out. Keep the job stupid. If you copy JSON with a JSON reader, your framework will helpfully parse and re-serialise it, and dates get reformatted and floats get rounded. Read it as raw text. If file count or file names matter, a distributed writer will not preserve them. Push, don't pull. The environment that owns the data should push it outward, so a buggy replicator can never destabilise production — and remember a replication job is a real workload that can, say, consume the last free IP in a subnet. PII — if the dataset carries personal data that must not leave production, this is the wrong pattern; use the next one. Latency — infrastructure replication is asynchronous, so read the provider's SLA before you rely on it for anything time-sensitive. Metadata — copying only the Parquet files of a Delta table gives you an unreadable table; copying Kafka keys and values without headers and within-partition order gives you a subtly wrong topic.

Transformation Replicator

Use it when you need production data elsewhere but some of it legally cannot travel.

This is the realistic version of the previous pattern. You want real production data in staging precisely because the provider's data has quirks no synthetic generator will reproduce — but the couriers dataset carries names, phone numbers and home addresses, and staging has looser access controls. So you insert one transformation step between the read and the write: either drop the offending columns, or replace their values.

The transform can be a SELECT projection, a column expression, or a row-level mapping function when the rule is complicated. Column-level access grants are a stricter alternative: rather than the job choosing what not to copy, the source only ever shows it the permitted columns.

Watch out. You are now writing logic, so you can now break the data. Declaring a timestamp column with a format that does not match the source turns the whole column into nulls; keeping fragile fields typed as strings during replication avoids an entire class of silent damage. The PII list drifts. What is not sensitive today may be reclassified tomorrow, and a hardcoded list of columns to drop will not notice. Drive the transformation from a data catalog or data contract where the sensitive fields are tagged, so the rule updates itself.

-- Reduction: keep everything except the fields that can't travel.
SELECT * EXCEPT (courier_name, courier_phone, home_address)
FROM prod.couriers;

# Or in a framework, when the drop list comes from a catalog lookup
# instead of being hardcoded in the job:
sensitive = catalog.tagged_columns("prod.couriers", tag="pii")
staging_df = spark.read.format("delta").table("prod.couriers").drop(*sensitive)

Housekeeping, readiness, and being told when to run

Compactor

Use it when a dataset has become slow to list rather than slow to read.

Our streaming job syncs scan events to object storage every couple of minutes so batch jobs can query them. After three months that directory holds several million tiny files, and the batch jobs now spend the majority of their runtime enumerating files and opening and closing handles — not processing data. On pay-per-request storage that is a bill as well as a delay.

The Compactor rewrites many small files into fewer large ones. Open table formats have this built in and do it transactionally as a new commit — Delta Lake's OPTIMIZE, Iceberg's rewrite-data-files action. Merge-on-read tables in Hudi are a variant: writes land in row format for speed and compaction folds them into the columnar files for read speed. The pattern also exists in key-based log stores, where "compaction" means something different: keep only the most recent record per key and physically discard the rest.

Watch out. Frequency is a genuine trade-off, with no right answer. Compaction is a full data-processing job, so run it rarely to save compute — but then recent data is uncompacted, and recent data is what consumers query. Sometimes the honest answer is to fold compaction into the ingestion job and eat the throughput hit, because penalising twenty consumers is worse than penalising one producer. Compaction alone reclaims nothing. The original small files usually still exist; you need a second cleanup step (VACUUM and friends) to actually free the space — and that step is what destroys your ability to time-travel to versions built on those files. Raw formats are risky. On plain JSON or CSV, readers cannot tell "being compacted" from "ready", which is why this is far safer on an ACID table format. Log compaction is non-deterministic — it runs when the broker feels like it, so you cannot assume one record per key at any given moment.

Readiness Marker

Use it when consumers keep processing your dataset before you have finished writing it.

Two teams read our hourly Silver table to build models and dashboards, and they keep complaining about incomplete data. Their pipelines are physically separate from ours, so we cannot simply trigger them. What we can do is publish an unambiguous signal that says "this partition is finished".

Two implementations. A flag file — an empty marker written only after the data write succeeds. Many engines already do this (_SUCCESS for raw formats; a new commit entry for Delta), and if yours does not, the orchestrator can write one as a final task. Or a convention: with hourly partitions, partition 10 is finished once partition 11 exists. Consumers wait for the successor rather than the marker.

Watch out. There is no enforcement. Nothing stops a consumer reading early — this is a social contract with a technical signal, so the contract has to actually be communicated, along with what breaks if it is ignored. Late data destroys the convention. If partitions are keyed on event time and you later fold a late record into the already-"closed" 09:00 partition, consumers who treated it as immutable now hold stale results. Decide explicitly: either closed partitions are immutable forever, or mutability rules are published and consumers are notified of every change. That choice is what §03 is about.

# Consumer side. The important flag is mode='reschedule': the sensor
# releases its worker slot between checks instead of holding one
# hostage for an hour while other tasks queue behind it.
wait_for_partition = FileSensor(
    task_id="wait_for_scans_partition",
    filepath="/lake/silver/scans/date={{ ds }}/hour={{ execution_date.hour }}/_SUCCESS",
    mode="reschedule",
    poke_interval=60,
)

External Trigger

Use it when the data arrives on an unpredictable schedule and polling for it is mostly wasted work.

The backend team ships new service definitions at most once a week, some time between Monday and Thursday. Our refresh job runs daily, so roughly five runs in seven reload a dataset that has not changed. The Readiness Marker does not help — it is still pull semantics, still a consumer checking. What we want is push: the producer tells us.

Three moving parts. Subscribe to a notification channel. React in a handler that inspects the event and decides whether it warrants work — filtering in the broker if it supports it, in the handler if not. Trigger the orchestrator or the job. Serverless functions are the natural home for the handler, and most orchestrators expose an API to start a run.

Watch out. Push beats pull. A long-running poller is technically valid and spends most of its life fetching zero messages; an event-driven handler that starts, reacts and exits does not. Never trigger blind. The temptation is to make the handler a bare ping. Then a run fails at 3am and there is no record of why it started or what it was meant to process. Pass the notification envelope, the trigger's own version, the event time and the processing time into the run's configuration. The events need protecting too. No event, no work — so the handler itself needs a failure destination, which is the Dead-Letter pattern applied one layer up.

# Handler: pass context, not just a ping. Future-you debugging this
# at 3am needs to know why the run exists.
def handler(event, ctx):
    payload = {
        "file_to_load":   event["Records"][0]["s3"]["object"]["key"],
        "event_envelope": json.dumps(event),
        "trigger": {"fn": ctx.function_name, "version": ctx.function_version,
                      "request_id": ctx.aws_request_id},
    }
    r = requests.post(f"{AIRFLOW}/api/v1/dags/service_levels_loader/dagRuns",
                      json={"conf": payload,
                            "dag_run_id": f"ext-{ctx.aws_request_id}"},
                      auth=CREDS)
    r.raise_for_status()   # let the platform's retry/DLQ handle failure

Ingestion is not only the front door.

Every pattern in this section is also a candidate for the "extract" step of any ETL or ELT job deeper in the platform. A Silver-to-Gold transformation that reads one partition of Silver is using the Incremental Loader; a job that waits for a marker before starting is using the Readiness Marker. These are not beginners' patterns you graduate from.

03.Error Management — seven patterns for when the data fights back

Dead-Letter · Windowed Deduplicator · Late Data Detector · Static Late Data Integrator · Dynamic Late Data Integrator · Filter Interceptor · Checkpointer

You are processing data generated by other people, on networks you do not own, from devices that go offline. That means you inherit their failures as well as their data: retried deliveries become your duplicates, a scanner buffering in a tunnel becomes your late data, a bad release upstream becomes your unparseable record. This family is about surviving all of that without either lying to consumers or paging yourself at 3am.

Transient vs non-transient errors

A transient error fixes itself: a database was briefly unreachable, a connection retried, everything continued. A non-transient error never fixes itself — the classic case being an unprocessable record, sometimes called a poison pill, which will fail identically on every attempt until a human intervenes.

The distinction decides your strategy: retry the transient ones, route the non-transient ones aside. Retrying a poison pill forever is how a streaming job spends a weekend crash-looping.

Unprocessable records

Dead-Letter

Use it when one bad record must not be allowed to stop a pipeline that is otherwise healthy.

Our streaming job writes scan events from a topic to object storage. Recently a producer started emitting occasional malformed payloads, and for three days the fix has been manual: restart the job, hand-edit the committed offsets to skip past the bad message. That does not scale and it loses the record silently anyway.

The pattern has four parts. First, find the places that can fail — usually a mapping or parsing function, sometimes an "error-safe" transformation your engine provides. Second, guard them: a try/catch around a mapping function, or an if/else around an error-safe one. Third, route failures to a separate destination, decorated with enough context to diagnose them later — the original payload, the exception, the job version. Fourth, monitor that destination, because the whole point of the pattern is that failures no longer announce themselves by crashing.

Good dead-letter stores are boring and highly available: an object store, or another topic. Optionally you add a fifth part — a replay pipeline that feeds recovered records back into the main flow — and that is where it gets interesting.

Watch out. Hidden failures. Fail-fast has one great virtue: when the pipeline stops, consumers stop, and nothing wrong propagates. Dead-lettering removes that safety, so pair it with alerting on rate, and be willing to actually halt the job when the rate spikes — one bad record an hour is noise, eighty per cent bad records is an incident. Snowball backfilling. Replaying yesterday's failures writes into partitions your consumers already processed, so they must backfill, and their consumers must backfill, and so on. There is no clean fix; either accept the loss, or notify direct consumers and let them decide. Mark replayed rows so consumers can tell them apart — a boolean, or richer metadata with job name, version and replay time. Ordering and consistency break. If records for 10:00, 10:01 and 10:02 partly fail and the middle one is replayed later, your sink now holds them out of order — and any consumer computing an inactivity-based session over that gap will close the session early and be wrong. Error-safe functions make this harder, not easier. A function that returns NULL instead of throwing gives you nothing to catch: you have to compare input against output ("input present, output null → probably a failure") and understand each function's own definition of safety.

Duplicated records

Windowed Deduplicator

Use it when the upstream delivers at-least-once and your logic must see each occurrence only once.

Exactly-once delivery is genuinely hard in distributed systems, so most real sources give you at-least-once: producers retry, and retries duplicate. Our scan events do exactly this.

Step one is picking the attributes that make a record unique — for a scan, (parcel_id, scan_type, scanner_id, event_time). Step two is deciding the scope in which you look for duplicates, and this is where batch and streaming diverge.

A batch job's natural scope is the dataset in front of it, which the book usefully frames as an implicit global window — that is why the pattern is called "windowed" even for batch. You can widen the scope to include previously processed data, at the cost of reading it. Implementation is DISTINCT, a framework helper, or a window function keeping row_number() = 1.

A streaming job has no complete dataset, so it manufactures one: a time-based window over which it remembers the keys it has already emitted. Remembering means a state store, and the logic becomes "look the key up, emit only if unseen, record it".

Watch out. Space versus time, with no free option. A short deduplication window is cheap and misses duplicates that arrive later; a long one catches more and holds more keys in state. Pick deliberately, based on how far apart real retries actually land. Exactly-once processing is not exactly-once delivery. Deduplicating your input says nothing about whether a retried task wrote your output twice — that is what §04 is for. Automatic retries undermine the whole thing: a restarted execution can reprocess records the deduplicator had already cleared, because the state and the write are not one atomic act.

The three kinds of state store, and what each one costs

Local (memory only) — fastest possible access. Lose the process, lose the state. Fine for a job whose state can be rebuilt cheaply; not fine for anything you bill on.

Local with fault tolerance — lives in memory, is periodically persisted to durable storage. The persistence interval is the trade-off: checkpoint on every update and consistency is solid but the job is slow; checkpoint rarely and it is fast but you can lose recent state on a crash. This is what most frameworks do by default.

Remote — the state lives only in an external store, so fault tolerance comes for free. You pay in latency on every lookup, and in the bill for the store.

-- Batch deduplication with no framework helper available.
-- Works anywhere window functions do.
SELECT parcel_id, scan_type, scanner_id, event_time, hub_id
FROM (
  SELECT *, ROW_NUMBER() OVER (
           PARTITION BY parcel_id, scan_type, scanner_id, event_time
           ORDER BY ingested_at) AS rn
  FROM bronze.scans
) t
WHERE rn = 1;

Late data — the innocent-sounding disaster

Late Data Detector

Use it before you do anything else about late data: you cannot ignore, park or integrate a late record until you can label it.

Most scans reach us within fifteen seconds. But a van drives through a dead zone, the handheld buffers locally, and forty minutes later it flushes a batch of scans stamped with the times they actually happened. Are those late, or on time? The question only has an answer if you have decided three things.

1 — Which column is event time. The attribute saying when the thing happened, as opposed to when you touched it. Without it there is no lateness, only arrival order.

2 — How to aggregate progress per partition. Use MAX(event_time). It must be monotonically increasing: tracked time may move forward, never backwards.

3 — How to combine partitions into one global progress value. Here you have a real choice. MIN across partitions follows the slowest source: more data counts as on-time, but you buffer more. MAX follows the fastest: less buffering, and you drop records from lagging sources. With several partitioned sources you can mix — MAX within each source, MIN across sources, or the reverse.

Finally you subtract an allowed lateness from the tracked event time, and the result is the watermark: the oldest event time still accepted. Anything older is late.

Watch out. Detecting and capturing are different features. Some engines detect lateness and drop it for you without ever handing you the record — great until you need to analyse what you dropped. Others expose it through a side output. Check which one you have before you promise anyone a late-data report. Never use MIN at the partition level. It lets the watermark move backwards, and that produces two specific miseries: an open–close–open loop, where state you already emitted as complete must be reopened; and stuck in the past, where a source that keeps delivering old data means the watermark never advances and your state grows without bound. MAX plus skew is brutally aggressive. If four of five sources are forty minutes behind and one is current, a max-based watermark silently discards the four. There is no silver bullet: monitor drops, and be ready to reintegrate with the next two patterns.

Static Late Data Integrator

Use it when late data is valuable, and you are willing to accept a fixed cut-off after which you stop caring.

Our daily "hub throughput" job publishes numbers the business treats as provisional for fifteen days, because that is the agreed maximum delay for folding in late scans. Anything older is dropped. The naive implementation would be fifteen separate jobs each day; the pattern gives you one.

You define a static lookback window — say fourteen days — and every run reprocesses today plus the previous fourteen partitions. "Static" because the window never changes, even when a record arrives on day sixteen.

Then you choose where in the pipeline the reintegration happens. Late data first, then today. Both at once. Or today first, late data after. For a stateful pipeline the answer is forced: history must be valid before the present is computed, so late data goes first, sequentially. For a stateless pipeline any of the three works, and if consumers want current data fastest, do today first.

Watch out. Snowball backfilling again — you are rewriting fourteen partitions your consumers already read; all you can really do is tell them. Do not backfill a lookback pipeline the way you backfill a normal one. This is the trap. With a four-day window, replaying the runs of Oct 10, 11 and 12 executes Oct 6–9, then 7–10, then 8–11: massive overlap, wasted compute, and a fair chance of inconsistency. Replaying only the latest run covers everything the earlier ones would have. The lookback runs must be part of the main pipeline, not separate triggered pipelines, for exactly the same overlap reason. Most of those runs find nothing. Add a cheap control task that checks whether a partition actually received late data before reprocessing it — or use the dynamic version below. No time concept, no pattern: if the dataset has no event-time notion, there is nothing to be late relative to.

The seductive wrong answer: just partition by processing time

Partitioning on arrival time makes late data disappear from your problem list entirely — every record lands in the partition for the moment you received it, and nothing is ever late. This is real and sometimes correct.

But it does not eliminate the problem, it exports it. Your 09:00 processing-time partition now contains 80% of 09:00 events, 10% of 08:00 and 10% of 07:00. Any downstream consumer that reasons in event time — and someone always does — now has late data to handle, generated by you. If you take this route, take it knowingly and say so in the dataset's contract.

Dynamic Late Data Integrator

Use it when the tolerance for late data has no fixed limit, and you only want to reprocess partitions that genuinely changed.

The product owner now wants all late scans reflected, not just fifteen days' worth, and blindly replaying two weeks every night is both insufficient and wasteful. So instead of a fixed window, keep a small state table: one row per partition, holding when you last processed it and when it was last written to.

Finding the work becomes a query — give me partitions whose last-update time is after their last-processed time, and which are older than the partition I am running now. After a successful run you update the last-processed time. Some stores hand you last-modified-per-partition directly; where they do not, you derive it, for example by walking a table format's commit history and taking the newest version that touched each partition.

Watch out. Concurrency will duplicate your backfills. Four runs in parallel, each spotting the same stale partitions, each launching the same reintegration. The fix is a third column — an in progress flag — added to the selection query, plus three ordering rules: mark the current partition as in-flight before doing anything, make the partition-selection task depend on its own previous success, and clear the flag when the last-processed time is updated. Be aware that this deliberately introduces a sequential bottleneck inside an otherwise concurrent pipeline, and that if the selection task fails, the pipeline wedges until a human unblocks it. Stateful pipelines turn a dynamic window into an unbounded one. If a sessionizer that last ran on Oct 20 discovers late data for Sep 21, correctness requires regenerating every run in between — thirty executions from one late record. Sometimes you must cap even the dynamic window. Getting last-modified per partition can be genuinely fiddly, up to and including maintaining your own tracking table.

-- The whole pattern, in one query. Add the third predicate the day
-- you allow concurrent runs, not the day after you notice duplicates.
SELECT partition_key
FROM   meta.scan_partition_state
WHERE  last_written_at  > last_processed_at   -- it changed
  AND  partition_key    < :current_partition   -- it's in the past
  AND  in_progress      = false;              -- nobody else took it

Seeing your own filters, and surviving a crash

Filter Interceptor

Use it when filtered volume jumped and you cannot tell which condition is responsible.

We shipped a release and the share of scan events filtered out went from 15% to 90% overnight. Is that a data change or a bug we introduced? The execution plan will not tell you, because the optimiser has collapsed six predicates into one filter step and reports one number for all of them.

The pattern makes each condition individually countable. With a programmatic API you wrap each predicate together with a counter that increments when it rejects a row, and gather the counters at the end of the run. In SQL it is clumsier: you promote the conditions to columns in a subquery or temp table — a CASE chain producing a "which rule rejected this" flag — then aggregate that flag for the statistics and use it as the actual filter for the data.

Watch out. Small but non-zero runtime cost — counters are cheap local structures and cheap to combine; the SQL version is more expensive because it usually means materialising an intermediate table. Declarative languages lose here. This is one of the cases where the programmatic API is simply clearer and more maintainable, even if the rest of your codebase is SQL. Use the right tool per job, not per team. Streaming needs time boundaries. A single running total over an unbounded stream tells you nothing about now; scope the statistics to processing-time windows so you can see trends. It may also turn a stateless job stateful, which is not free.

-- SQL version: one pass, two outputs. The status_flag column is both
-- the diagnostic and the filter.
CREATE TEMP VIEW scans_flagged AS
SELECT *,
  CASE
    WHEN parcel_id  IS NULL            THEN 'null_parcel_id'
    WHEN hub_id     IS NULL            THEN 'null_hub_id'
    WHEN event_time > now()           THEN 'event_time_in_future'
    WHEN length(scan_type) < 2       THEN 'scan_type_too_short'
    ELSE NULL
  END AS status_flag
FROM bronze.scans;

-- diagnostic: which rule is eating the data?
SELECT status_flag, count(*) FROM scans_flagged
WHERE status_flag IS NOT NULL GROUP BY status_flag;

Checkpointer

Use it for any continuously running job, because it is the only way to answer "where was I?" after a crash.

A batch job restarting knows what to do: the partition it was given. A streaming job reading an append-only log has no such structure — without recorded progress, restarting means reprocessing from the beginning of time. Checkpointing records the consumed position, and for stateful jobs the computed state as well, into storage that outlives the process.

Two flavours of where. Framework-managed: the processing engine writes progress metadata to durable storage and handles it entirely. Store-managed: you use the data store's own client, and progress lives in the store — a consumer-offsets topic, a lease table.

And two flavours of how. Configuration-driven, where you set an interval or a location and the library does it. Or explicit, where after processing you must actively commit, and forgetting to means seeing the same data again.

Watch out. Guarantee versus latency. Tracking positions is cheap — a handful of numbers per partition. Tracking state is not, because state can be orders of magnitude larger than the offsets. Frequent checkpoints mean slower runs; rare checkpoints mean more reprocessing after a failure. It feels like exactly-once and it is not. Tasks run in parallel and asynchronously; one failing mid-flight after its siblings succeeded means the restart reprocesses records that already landed. Checkpointing gets you at-least-once or at-most-once, and nothing more — the missing half is §04.

The three delivery modes, and which one checkpointing gives you

At least once — you checkpoint after writing. A crash in between means the write happens again: duplicates, no loss. This is the sane default.

At most once — you checkpoint before writing. A crash in between means the write never happens: loss, no duplicates. Almost never what you want for data you are paid to keep.

Exactly once — the write happens once regardless of retries. Checkpointing cannot deliver this on its own; you get there by combining it with an idempotency pattern.

Which one you have is decided by the order of two lines of code, not by a config flag named after the guarantee.

04.Idempotency — seven ways to make a re-run safe

Fast Metadata Cleaner · Data Overwrite · Merger · Stateful Merger · Keyed Idempotency · Transactional Writer · Proxy

Error management buys you survival, not correctness. Automatic retries are a wonderful feature from the engineering side — set a number, walk away — and a consistency hazard from the data side, because a retried task cheerfully re-executes writes that already succeeded. Duplicates are the good outcome, because at least a consumer can remove them. The bad outcome is duplicates you cannot even identify as duplicates.

Idempotency is the property that makes a re-run boring: however many times the job executes for a given period, the target ends up the way one clean run would have left it. Seven patterns get you there, and they split neatly by what you are allowed to touch.

Overwriting: delete then write

Fast Metadata Cleaner

Use it when your "delete the previous run's rows" step has become the slow part of the pipeline.

Our daily job writes 500 GB to 1.5 TB of scan events. For idempotency it does two things: DELETE the rows the previous run added, then INSERT the new ones. This worked for three weeks. Now the table is enormous and the DELETE takes longer than the processing, because a delete is really two operations — find the affected rows, then rewrite every file that contained them.

The insight is that TRUNCATE TABLE and DROP TABLE do the same thing semantically without scanning anything. They are metadata operations: they change the description of the data rather than the data. And you can reach them by changing how you think about the dataset — not one monolithic table, but many physical tables that together form one logical dataset, exposed through a single access point such as a view.

That choice defines your idempotency granularity. Fifty-two weekly tables behind one view means the unit you can cheaply wipe is one week. The orchestration then grows three steps: decide whether this run starts a new granularity period (a branching task on the execution date), create the idempotency context with TRUNCATE or DROP+create, and refresh the exposition layer. With DROP there is a subtlety — a reader touching the view while a member table is missing gets an error, so remove the table from the view first, then drop it.

Watch out. Idempotency granularity is also backfill granularity. Weekly tables mean fixing one bad day requires re-running the whole week — not necessarily the full pipeline for each day, but at minimum the load step. No fine-grained backfills. Metadata operations work on whole tables, so "just re-do customer 4471" is off the table. Quotas are real. Warehouses cap partitions per table and tables per cluster; the numbers look huge until you apply the pattern to twenty pipelines. Mitigate with a freezing step that folds weekly tables into monthly or yearly ones once they can no longer change. It needs metadata operations to exist — fine on warehouses, lakehouses and relational databases, awkward on a bare object store. The exposition layer is now your problem, because users should not have to know about fifty-two tables. Schema evolution splits in two: adding a required field is free, because replaying past runs writes it; adding an optional field to already-frozen tables needs a separate migration pipeline.

Data Overwrite

Use it when metadata operations are not available — typically because you are writing files to an object store.

Same daily job, same duplicate-on-backfill problem, but the target is event-time-partitioned files in a bucket. There is no TRUNCATE to reach for, so you go through the data layer instead, and rely on a native replacement operation.

If you use a processing framework, this is usually one option on the writer — a save mode, a write mode — and the framework removes existing files before writing. Better frameworks let you scope it: overwrite only the slice matching a predicate rather than the whole table. In SQL you have three routes: DELETE plus INSERT (verbose, but you can choose which rows go); INSERT OVERWRITE (concise, replaces the whole target, no row selection); or your store's bulk-load command with a replace flag.

Note that "overwrite" rarely means "gone". On any store with time travel the old blocks survive until a retention period expires or a space-reclamation command runs.

Watch out. I/O grows with the target. An overwrite of a large unpartitioned table gets slower every day, forever. Partitioning is the mitigation — it shrinks the unit you rewrite — which is why §08 exists. Space is not reclaimed automatically. Deleted blocks linger; a vacuum-style command is what actually frees them, and it is also what removes your ability to roll back. Overwrite is not inherently atomic. Whether a half-finished overwrite is visible depends entirely on the target format. On a raw format, it is.

Updates: when you only get an increment

Merger

Use it when you never receive the full dataset — only the rows that changed — and each row has a stable identity.

A CDC stream feeds us parcels changes, and our batch job must keep a Silver table that mirrors the source exactly, with no duplicates. Deleting and rewriting is not an option: we do not have the whole table, only today's deltas.

The first decision is the only one that really matters: which attributes identify a row. A single natural key if one exists, a composite key if not. Get this wrong and every backfill inserts instead of updating, and you have unremovable duplicates. Then express the combination with MERGE (also spelled UPSERT), defining behaviour for three cases: insert when the row is new, update when it exists, and delete — which the pattern cannot see on its own. A missing row in an increment means nothing happened, not "it was removed", so deletes only work if the producer emits them as soft deletes: an UPDATE that sets a flag.

One easily-missed detail: the WHEN NOT MATCHED branch also needs the deleted-flag guard. Without it, the very first run inserts rows that were already deleted upstream, and since no future increment will mention them again, they are immortal.

Watch out. Uniqueness is a hard prerequisite, and it has to be immutable — a key that changes between runs is not a key. It is a data operation, so more expensive than the metadata patterns, though modern engines prune heavily using file statistics before touching data. Backfilling an incremental dataset is temporarily wrong. This is the pattern's real flaw. The table always holds the latest state, so replaying from 08:00 merges an old increment into a newer table: consumers briefly see rows that should not exist yet, and the table only converges once the last period is replayed. If the problem was the producer's data, don't replay at all — ask for a corrected increment and merge it as new. That works as long as row identity did not change.

MERGE INTO silver.parcels AS t
USING staging.parcels_delta AS s
   ON t.parcel_id = s.parcel_id            -- immutable identity

WHEN MATCHED AND s.is_deleted = true  THEN DELETE
WHEN MATCHED AND s.is_deleted = false THEN UPDATE SET
       status = s.status, current_hub = s.current_hub, updated_at = s.updated_at

-- the guard people forget: without it, the first run inserts rows
-- that were already deleted upstream, and nothing ever removes them
WHEN NOT MATCHED AND s.is_deleted = false THEN INSERT *;

Stateful Merger

Use it when consumers require the table to be correct at every moment, including during a backfill.

A week after the merge pipeline went live we found a bug, and the business asked for a backfill — but with the table restored to its last known-good state first, not merged forward from today. Plain MERGE cannot do that, so you extend it with a small amount of memory.

Add a state table with two columns: the pipeline's execution time, and the target table version that execution produced. The pipeline gains a step at each end. At the start, a conditional restore. At the end, record the version the merge just created.

The restore logic is a version comparison. If there is no recorded version for the previous execution, this is either a first run or a backfill of the very first run — truncate and merge. Otherwise compare the version recorded for the previous execution against the table's current latest version: equal means you are running forward normally, so do nothing; different means you are running backwards, so roll the table back before merging.

Watch out. You need a versioned store — a table format, or a warehouse with real versioning. Without one, the workaround is to keep an append-only raw history table stamped with execution time, detect backfilling by asking "do rows exist for execution times in the future?", delete those, and rebuild the target from history using the Windowed Deduplicator. Retention silently caps your reach. Vacuum removes files older versions depend on, so once retention passes, the version you wanted to restore is gone. Extend retention and pay for storage, or accept a backfill horizon. Non-data operations create versions too. Compaction touches no rows but does produce a new commit. So if you restore "the version recorded for the previous run", you silently discard every housekeeping operation that happened since. Restore the version before the current run's version instead.

Letting the database do the work

Keyed Idempotency

Use it when the target is keyed and you can compute the same key from the same logical input, every time.

Our streaming job buffers scans per parcel and writes a "parcel journey" record to a key-value store. If a task retries, we want the write to land on the same key and simply overwrite, rather than creating a second journey.

So the whole pattern reduces to key generation from immutable attributes. Sometimes you are lucky and a natural key exists. Often you have to compose one — and that is where the trap is. The obvious composite for a journey is (parcel_id, first_event_time), and event time is mutable: a late scan with an earlier timestamp arrives, the job restarts, and the "first" event time is now different, so the key is different, so you have two journeys for one parcel.

Use an append time instead — the moment the broker physically accepted the record, which no late arrival can change. Every streaming platform exposes it under some name; at-rest stores have the same idea as an insertion or ingestion timestamp. The identical logic generalises beyond keys to containers: name a file, partition or table after the run's immutable execution time and a replay produces one file, not two.

Watch out. It is only as idempotent as your database's write semantics. A key-value store overwrites, which is exactly what you want. A relational database raises a primary-key violation, so the write must become a MERGE and you are back to the earlier pattern's complexity. An append-only log accepts both copies and relies on asynchronous compaction to collapse them later — so consumers will see duplicates for a while, though at least they share a key and are recognisable. A mutable source can invalidate the key. If compaction or retention removes the very first record you derived the key from, the next run derives a different key. Arguably correct — the input genuinely changed — but it is not the guarantee you thought you had.

Transactional Writer

Use it when partial results must never be visible, particularly on infrastructure that can vanish mid-run.

We moved a batch job onto pre-emptible capacity and cut infrastructure cost by 60%. Now, whenever the provider reclaims a node, its tasks die and reschedule elsewhere — and consumers see duplicates and truncated data, because the dead tasks had already written some files.

Transactions give all-or-nothing visibility: changes exist but stay private until a commit makes them public, and a failure means a rollback or, at worst, orphaned data nobody can read. Three steps — begin, write, commit — with the begin sometimes explicit and sometimes handled for you.

In a distributed job there are two levels to choose between. A task-local transaction, where each parallel task commits independently — simple, and it stops protecting you the moment the whole job restarts. Or a job-wide transaction, which is the stronger guarantee and harder to achieve: with a table format the commit is the moment a new log entry appears, and if that step fails you are left with data files needing manual cleanup.

Watch out. Commit costs latency. Raw files are visible the instant they land; transactional files are visible only after the commit, which means consumers wait for the slowest task in the job. That is the price of never seeing a partial dataset. Support is uneven. Table formats are well covered by major engines; streaming-broker transactions are supported by some frameworks and not others, which quietly rules the pattern out of some stacks. Idempotency is scoped to the transaction and nothing wider. A backfill opens a brand-new transaction and inserts the same rows again, perfectly transactionally. And with task-local transactions, a job restart rewrites the data from tasks that had already committed. Readers can opt out. A consumer that sets its isolation level to read-uncommitted sees your in-flight rows — the classic dirty read — including ones you are about to roll back.

When you are not allowed to change anything

Proxy

Use it when the dataset must be write-once, but consumers still want a single stable place to read from.

Legal now requires every historical version of a full-refresh dataset to be retained, so overwriting is forbidden. The pattern's motto is the old engineering joke: any problem can be solved with one more level of indirection.

Two halves. Immutability: each run writes to a new location — a timestamped or versioned table — and write permissions are removed once it exists, so it is physically writable exactly once. On object storage you can go further with a write-once-read-many lock, which every major provider offers under its own name. Indirection: a single access point that resolves to the newest version. Usually a passthrough view with no transformation in the SELECT; several NoSQL stores offer the same idea as an alias. If your store has no view concept, the fallback is a manifest file naming the current locations — and that manifest should be produced by a task separate from the processing job, so a manifest failure does not force you to recompute the data.

There is also an almost-free variant. On a format that keeps history natively, every overwrite already creates a new version with the old data intact, so time travel is the proxy and you write no indirection at all.

Watch out. Views are not universal, and a manifest is a clumsier substitute that consumers must be taught to read. Orchestration alone cannot enforce immutability. Configuring the writing task is not the same as preventing a write; you need object locks or revoked permissions, which usually means involving whoever owns the infrastructure. The free variant has a short memory. Native versioning is bounded by retention, and that retention is sometimes fixed and not configurable — a poor fit for "keep every version forever".

Where this idea came from

If you want the philosophy behind this whole family, the book points at Maxime Beauchemin's 2018 essay on functional data engineering, which is what made "your pipelines should be idempotent" a mainstream expectation rather than a niche opinion. The patterns here are the mechanics; that essay is the argument.

05.Data Value — ten patterns for turning rows into answers

Static Joiner · Dynamic Joiner · Wrapper · Metadata Decorator · Distributed Aggregator · Local Aggregator · Incremental Sessionizer · Stateful Sessionizer · Bin Pack Orderer · FIFO Orderer

Data sitting in storage is not yet an asset. A raw scan event tells you a great deal about one moment — a parcel, a scanner, a timestamp, a hub — and almost nothing about anything else. It cannot tell you which courier handled it, how long the parcel has been in transit, or whether this hub is slower than usual, because each event arrives as an isolated item with no relationship to any other.

This family closes that gap in two opposite directions. You can add information — join in context, or compute and attach new attributes. Or you can remove it — aggregate thousands of events into one number, or collapse a stream of scans into one parcel journey. Both create value. Then there is a third thing consumers ask for that is neither: order.

Enrichment: joining in the context

Static Joiner

Use it when the thing you are joining to sits still — a reference table, a dimension, an API's snapshot.

Analysts want to correlate a parcel's delivery time with the courier's tenure, and tenure only exists in the couriers reference dataset. The dataset is at rest, which makes this the easy case — and slightly surprisingly, it stays the easy case even when the enriched side is a stream.

You need the join keys, and often a time condition as well. That second part matters more than people expect: if the reference dataset tracks history, "the courier's tenure" is only meaningful as of some date. That is what slowly changing dimensions are for — type 2 keeps validity dates on each row, with the current row's end date left open; type 4 keeps a current-value table plus a full history table. The query shape is the same for both.

The reference data does not have to be a table. Enrichment from an HTTP API works too, and comes in two shapes: call it live from the job, or materialise its response into a table in a pre-processing step and join to that.

Watch out. Consistency under late data. The ideal is that a courier's profile change and their next scan arrive in step. They will not. For streaming, the next pattern handles this properly; for batch, orchestration does — wait for the reference dataset with a Readiness Marker before joining. Idempotency is the real gotcha. If you replay last month's pipeline against today's reference table, you produce different output than the original run did. To get repeatability you need the enrichment side to answer time-based questions, and if the provider will not, the fix is to bring it into your own storage as a slowly changing dimension. For an API this is the only option — materialise every fetched snapshot with a validity window, and join on the run's execution time rather than on now(). Live API calls need batching: one request per row is the difference between a job that finishes and one that does not.

-- SCD type 2 join, anchored to the run's execution time rather than
-- now(). This is the line that makes a replay reproducible.
SELECT s.parcel_id, s.event_time, s.hub_id, c.courier_code, c.tenure_band
FROM   silver.scans s
JOIN   silver.couriers c
       ON  c.courier_id = s.courier_id
       AND :run_ts BETWEEN c.valid_from AND c.valid_to;

Dynamic Joiner

Use it when both sides are moving, and a naive join would mostly find nothing.

Courier profile changes now arrive as their own CDC stream, thousands a week. Joining a stream to a stream with only a key condition produces mostly empty results — not because there is no match, but because one side has not arrived yet. The fix is time boundaries.

You buffer unmatched records on each side for a bounded period, so the faster stream can wait for the slower one to catch up. That buffer window is, in practice, your estimate of the normal latency difference between the two sources: if profile changes usually trail scans by an hour, buffering scans for an hour is what makes the join succeed.

A buffer you never empty is a memory leak with extra steps, so the pattern includes a garbage-collection watermark: the rule that evicts records too old to plausibly find a partner. Every eviction is a join you have given up on, deliberately. Frameworks implement all of this for you — your job is to choose the numbers.

Watch out. Space versus exactness, and there is no formula. Bigger buffers catch more joins and cost more memory; smaller buffers are cheap and miss matches when the latency gap widens. The right answer comes from measuring the actual gap between your two sources, and revisiting it. Late data still loses. Once the GC watermark has moved past a record's window, a genuinely late partner arrives to find nothing waiting. Neither joiner pattern gives you a 100% match rate on its own — that needs the late-data patterns from §03.

Decoration: attaching computed context

Wrapper

Use it when you must add computed fields without losing or obscuring the original ones.

Scan events arrive from three generations of handheld firmware with three different schemas. We need one normalised shape downstream, but we also need the original payload intact for debugging, because "the parser produced nonsense" is only diagnosable if you kept what it parsed.

So instead of replacing the record, you put an envelope around it: one section holding the untouched input, another holding everything you derived — normalised fields, plus execution context like job version or processing time. For document formats this is literally a nested object. For tables you have four choices: raw columns flat with computed ones nested; computed flat with raw nested; everything flat at one level; or two tables joined by a key. The first two denormalise and read fast; the last normalises and is the right call when you are not allowed to alter the original structure at all.

Watch out. Domain split. Courier attributes now live in two places — raw and computed — and consumers have to know that. A reasonable compromise is to treat wrapped data as a Silver-layer artefact and hand users a flattened Gold view, rather than exposing the split to people who did not ask for it. Payload size. You are storing the input twice over, in effect, and that shows up in storage and network. Columnar storage with projection pushdown takes most of the sting out for readers who only touch a few columns. If you go flat, name things unmistakably — a table where hub_id and hub_id_resolved sit side by side with no naming convention is a support ticket generator.

Metadata Decorator

Use it when the context you want to attach is for you, not for the consumer.

We release the streaming job weekly and want every record to carry which version produced it. Consumers do not care and should not have to look at it — putting job_version in the payload with the Wrapper would just be noise in their schema. So put it in the metadata layer instead.

How easy this is depends entirely on the store. Some support it natively — a streaming record with optional key/value headers alongside its key and value is the ideal case. On object storage, per-file tags work when the context applies to every row in the file. Where there is no metadata concept at all, which covers most relational and NoSQL databases, you simulate it: keep the context in a column and hide that column behind a view, or block it with column-level permissions. Or normalise it — one small technical table keyed by a context id, referenced from the data table, living in a schema only your team can see. That version does not duplicate the context on every row.

Watch out. Native support is patchy, and not every broker has headers, which can rule the pattern out entirely on some platforms. Never put business attributes here. Metadata means data about data. A shipment value or an address hidden in a header is data nobody will ever find, because no consumer thinks to query the metadata layer. If a human will need it to answer a business question, it belongs in the payload.

Aggregation: removing information on purpose

Distributed Aggregator

Use it when the rows that need to be summed together are scattered across many machines.

The hub-performance cube needs counts and average dwell times across several axes — hub, service level, day, courier region. On one machine this is a group-by and a reduce and there is nothing to discuss. Across a cluster, the rows for one hub start out spread over every node, so before they can be reduced they have to be brought together — and that movement over the network is the shuffle.

The API usually looks identical to the single-machine version, which is exactly why the cost is easy to miss. It is very often the first thing to blame when a job that used to take four minutes takes forty.

The good news: any aggregation that can be computed in parts gets optimised automatically. A count does not need every raw row shipped to one node; each node counts what it holds locally and ships a number. Sums, mins and maxes behave the same way. Distinct counts and medians do not, which is why they hurt.

Watch out. Two network hops, not one. The first brings data to compute (unavoidable in a separated storage/compute world); the second is the shuffle. Only the second is yours to remove. Skew. If one key has vastly more rows than the rest, one node does most of the work while the others idle, and the job's duration is that node's duration. The standard mitigation is salting: add a small random component to the grouping key, aggregate on the salted key, then aggregate the results again on the real key. Modern engines also do adaptive skew handling for you. Nodes cannot be released. Shuffle output must survive in case a downstream task fails, so a node that has finished its work is still holding data and cannot be reclaimed while the job runs. An external shuffle service decouples the two and lets you actually scale down.

Local Aggregator

Use it when the input is already partitioned by exactly the key you are grouping on.

Our streaming job windows scans by parcel, and the topic is already partitioned by parcel id. Every record for a given parcel is guaranteed to be in one partition, so every record the reduce needs is already on the machine reading that partition. The shuffle the framework would insert is pure waste.

There is a second bonus beyond saving the network hop: tasks become fully independent. Nobody waits for anybody, which matters a lot in streaming, where one slow unit otherwise holds up the whole iteration.

Most of the work happens on the producer side — it must guarantee that a given key always lands in the same physical partition, which means a stable per-record partition key and a fixed partition count. On the consumer side, some libraries expose a "group by the key it is already keyed on" operation that skips the exchange. Where there is no such hint, per-partition operations plus your language's own grouping gets you there, and bucketed tables with matching bucket keys and counts achieve the same for at-rest joins.

Watch out. You have frozen your ability to scale. The pattern's correctness depends on the partition count never changing, because changing it changes which partition a key maps to — and then one logical key produces two groups. Rescaling means a storage reorganisation job that rewrites every record's partition assignment, and in streaming it means a stop-the-world moment while old partitions drain. Everyone must want the same grouping key. The layout can only serve one. If another team needs the same records grouped by courier rather than parcel, they get the Distributed Aggregator, or you write the data twice.

Sessionization: collapsing a stream into a story

Incremental Sessionizer

Use it when you need sessions from a batch, partitioned dataset and hourly latency is acceptable.

A parcel's journey spans its first pickup scan to its delivery, with a rule that closes the journey after two hours of no scans. Journeys typically run from minutes to three hours, which means one journey's events can be spread over three consecutive hourly partitions. Analysts currently reconstruct this by hand across partitions, badly.

The pattern needs three storage areas. Input — the raw hourly partitions. Completed sessions — the public output. Pending sessions — a private area holding journeys still open, which will close in some later run. Keeping pending sessions private matters: it is your internal working state, its shape will change as your logic changes, and consumers should never have been reading it.

Each run combines the new partition with the previous run's pending sessions, per entity, producing one of three outcomes: a brand-new session, a resumed session with new activity, or a resumed session with no new activity (about to expire, or expiring now). Then apply the session rules — initialisation (what starts one), accumulation (what a new event does to an open one), finalisation (what closes one, whether an explicit terminal event or an inactivity timeout). Completed sessions go public; the rest go back to pending.

Watch out. The inactivity period costs money. Longer means more late scans included and more pending state carried; shorter means cheaper and more truncated journeys. Freshness. Hourly partitions mean at best hourly insight. Publishing partial sessions helps, but a partial session is not a final one — a parcel flagged "delayed" after one partition may look perfectly on time after the next, and a consumer that treats partial as final acts on the wrong answer. Flag them explicitly with something like is_complete = false. Sessions are forward-dependent, and this is the expensive one. The session state at 10:00 feeds 11:00, which feeds 12:00. So integrating a late scan into the 09:00 partition means every partition after it must be regenerated. Replaying everything downstream is simple and costly; detecting only the affected entities and replaying just those is cheap and complicated. There is no third option.

Stateful Sessionizer

Use it when hourly is not good enough and you need journeys within seconds.

Same logic, same three conceptual zones, one substitution: the private pending store becomes a state store attached to a streaming job, and instead of one big hourly iteration you get thousands of tiny ones. Create or resume the session, apply your accumulation rule, then either emit it and clear the state or write the updated state back.

Two levels of API to choose from. Session windows are declarative: you give a gap duration, and the engine opens a new window whenever two events for the same key are further apart than the gap. Very little code, and it covers the common case. Arbitrary stateful processing hands you the state object and the timers and lets you define expiry yourself — including per-key dynamic rules, like a longer inactivity window for international parcels. More code, more power.

Watch out. At-least-once, always. State is checkpointed periodically, not on every update, so a restart resumes from the last checkpoint and re-applies whatever came after. Do not build session identity out of anything that changes between runs — wall-clock time being the obvious offender; see Keyed Idempotency. Scaling means rebalancing state. Adding workers redistributes state keys, and the job cannot process a key until its state has moved. Possible, but far more costly than scaling a stateless job. Expire on event time, not processing time. Processing-time expiry looks simpler and breaks under any unexpected delay: a write retry stalls the job, wall-clock keeps moving, and sessions close early on data that was about to arrive. Event time keeps expiry tied to the data's own progress.

Ordering: when sequence is the value

Partial commits — the reason ordering is hard at scale

Bulk write APIs optimise network use by sending many records per request. Many of them, in exchange, have three outcomes rather than two: all written, none written, or some written. Several popular streaming and NoSQL bulk APIs behave exactly this way.

Why the third one breaks ordering: send records stamped 10:00, 10:10, 10:20 and suppose only 10:20 lands. You retry the other two, they succeed, and the store now holds them in the order 10:20, 10:00, 10:10. You cannot fix this after the fact, because you never knew which ones failed until the response came back.

Bin Pack Orderer

Use it when you need ordered delivery and bulk throughput, into a store with partial commits.

We expose scan events to partner systems through a store whose bulk API can partially fail. The job windows ten minutes of events, aggregates per minute, and must deliver them per partner in event-time order. Sending one record at a time is correct and far too slow.

The trick is to build batches in which a retry cannot reorder anything, and the way to guarantee that is to allow at most one record per entity in each batch. Sort by entity and time. Then deal the sorted records into bins like cards: each entity's first record into bin 1, its second into bin 2, and so on. Deliver bins strictly in sequence, and do not start bin N+1 until bin N is fully written. Any retry inside a bin can only re-send a record that has no same-entity sibling in that bin — so ordering per entity is preserved no matter what the store does.

Watch out. The guarantee stops at the job boundary. Ordering holds within one execution. If the whole pipeline fails and retries, it re-emits records it already delivered, and the overall order breaks anyway — so pair this with an idempotency pattern. It is real code. A sort is a function call; bin packing is a loop with bookkeeping you have to test.

FIFO Orderer

Use it when order matters, volume is modest, and you would rather have simple code than maximum throughput.

A streaming job picks out exception scans — damaged, refused, misrouted — and forwards them to an alerting stream in processing order, as fast as possible. Buffering to save network round-trips is explicitly not wanted here.

There is no algorithm. Send one record, wait for the acknowledgement, send the next. That wait is the entire pattern; without it you have concurrent in-flight requests and no ordering at all. You can implement it with a single-record API, or with a bulk API whose concurrency is pinned to one. Some platforms offer a middle path — an idempotent producer that permits a handful of concurrent requests and still guarantees order — which is strictly better when available.

Watch out. One request per row is expensive in both latency and I/O, and it shows up immediately on a throughput dashboard. Multithreading helps but reintroduces the ordering question — the trick is to make each thread own all records for a set of entities, so order is preserved within an entity even though threads are unaware of each other. That is bin packing again, one entity per container. FIFO is not exactly-once. The classic bug: send succeeds, then the acknowledgement of the source record fails, so after a restart the producer re-sends something already delivered. In order, and twice.

# FIFO: the flush() is the pattern. Correct, and slow.
for scan in exception_scans:
    producer.produce(topic="scan-exceptions", key=scan.parcel_id, value=scan.json())
    producer.flush()          # block until the broker confirms

# Better, where the platform supports it: bounded concurrency plus an
# idempotent producer keeps order without one round-trip per record.
producer = Producer({
    "enable.idempotence": True,
    "max.in.flight.requests.per.connection": 5,
    "queue.buffering.max.ms": 2000,
})

06.Data Flow — eight patterns for who runs what, and when

Local Sequencer · Isolated Sequencer · Aligned Fan-In · Unaligned Fan-In · Parallel Split · Exclusive Choice · Single Runner · Concurrent Runner

So far every pattern has been about one job's relationship with its data. This family is about jobs' relationships with each other — chaining steps, merging branches, splitting them, and deciding how many copies of a pipeline may run at once.

Each of these operates at two levels, and knowing which level you are on prevents most of the arguments. At the orchestration level, the pattern shapes a pipeline graph — tasks, dependencies, triggers. At the processing level, the same shape appears inside a single job — a chain of transformations, a UNION, an if/else. The same word, two very different blast radii.

Sequence: one thing after another

Local Sequencer

Use it when one giant step should be several smaller ones inside the same pipeline.

We inherited the oldest job in the platform. Hundreds of lines, three times the transformations it started with, fails regularly, and every failure means starting from the beginning. Nothing can be removed, because it is all load-bearing.

The fix is the oldest engineering move there is: decompose into steps that run in order, with dependencies matching data dependencies — if B needs what A produced, B runs after A. Three questions tell you whether a boundary belongs in the orchestration layer or inside the job:

Separation of concerns. The smell is naming difficulty. If you cannot name the step in a few words, or the name has an "and" in it, you have merged two steps.

Maintainability. Anything before a failure gets recomputed on retry. If the readiness check calls ten billed APIs and the load fails three times, you paid for thirty API calls to load one file. Splitting them means you pay once.

Implementation effort. Orchestrators already ship abstractions for running a query, calling an API, waiting for a file. Merge everything into one task and you reimplement all of it.

Watch out. Boundaries are the whole difficulty. The most useful lens is restart boundaries: what should be able to fail and be retried on its own? Put the line before expensive operations, so a failure late in the job does not re-run the costly part. The complementary lens is transaction boundaries: two operations that must either both happen or neither belong in one unit — a job that processes data and records its own progress version should not be able to do the first without the second. Too granular is also wrong. Every task has scheduling overhead, and a hundred-task pipeline can spend more time being coordinated than doing work.

Isolated Sequencer

Use it when the dependency crosses a pipeline boundary — usually a team boundary.

Our team cleans and enriches scan data. The BI team turns it into dashboard views. We agreed the BI transformation does not belong in our pipeline, because we will not be the ones maintaining it. So there are two pipelines, and one depends on the other.

Boundaries are easy here: they follow ownership. The harder question is the trigger, and there are two answers with genuinely different politics.

Data-based. The producer publishes a Readiness Marker; the consumer watches for it and starts. Loosely coupled: the only contract is the marker's existence. The consumer can switch to a different dataset, or stop consuming entirely, without telling anyone. Downside: nothing in either pipeline's definition records that the other exists.

Task-based. The producer directly triggers the consumer's pipeline. Tightly coupled: the dependency is explicit and visible, and renaming a task on the consumer side breaks the producer's run. Its real use case is when there is no shared dataset at all — the consumer needs to be told to do something, not given something.

Watch out. Task-based coupling drags schedules together. Ideally both pipelines share a schedule so the producer can trigger the consumer directly. When they do not, someone has to add conditional logic — the producer skipping the trigger on some runs, or the consumer running on the producer's cadence and doing real work only when needed. The binding constraint is communication, not code. This pattern's failure mode is an organisational one: a team changes an output and finds out from an incident. You can build technical guards, and they are expensive and sometimes impossible when the other side accepts no inbound connections. The durable fix is that every team knows its inputs and outputs and who is on the other end of each — and a lineage tool (§10) is what makes that knowable at all.

Fan-in: many branches, one child

Aligned Fan-In

Use it when a downstream step needs every upstream branch to have succeeded.

Consumers want a daily hub aggregate, but our scan data is partitioned hourly. Running one job over 24 hours of data in a single shot works and is a bad idea; running 24 hourly jobs that merge into one daily rollup is the same computation with much better properties.

In orchestration it is branches converging on a common task. In the processing layer you additionally choose how they combine: UNION stacks datasets vertically (more rows, same columns) and JOIN combines them horizontally (more columns, fewer rows). Both are fan-in; they answer different questions.

Two benefits are worth being explicit about. Faster feedback — 24 small jobs surface a problem in the last hour within minutes, while one big job surfaces it at the end of a long run. Cheaper recovery — if only hour 24 failed, you replay hour 24. In the single-job version you replay all 24.

Watch out. Infrastructure spikes. Twenty-four simultaneous jobs is fine on elastic compute and a denial-of-service against yourself on fixed capacity. Cap the concurrency, or go fully incremental — run each hourly branch as its hour arrives and only aggregate in the last run of the day. Scheduling skew. The child starts when the slowest parent finishes, so one pathological branch sets the whole pipeline's latency. Scheduling overhead is real at high task counts. Readability degrades as branches multiply, and there is no universal right number — decide per pipeline.

Unaligned Fan-In

Use it when a partial result today beats a complete result tomorrow.

The hourly-to-daily rollup has been running for weeks, and occasionally one hour fails and no aggregate is published at all. After a conversation with consumers we agreed something is better than nothing: publish what we have, fill the gap later.

So you relax the trigger condition from "all parents succeeded" to something weaker. That enables two distinct scenarios: run the child on partial input when some parents failed and that is acceptable; or run a different child on failure, as a fallback or error-handling path. Orchestrators express this as a trigger rule on the task; less declarative environments make you evaluate the parents' outcomes yourself.

Watch out. The rule is invisible. A pipeline graph with both a success-path child and a failure-path child looks ambiguous, and you cannot tell what will actually run without reading code. Use the orchestrator's dedicated failure hooks for error paths rather than a second normal-looking task, so the graph stays honest. Partial data must announce itself. If 12 of 24 hours made it in, consumers need to know. Options, roughly in order of robustness: a completeness metric in a companion table (12/24 = 50%), a metadata tag on the written objects, an active notification, or — the safest — do not publish at all: write the incomplete result somewhere private and only promote it at 100%.

-- If you do publish partial data, compute the completeness in the same
-- statement so it can never drift from the rows it describes.
INSERT INTO gold.hub_daily (hub_id, scans, avg_dwell_min, is_approximate)
SELECT hub_id, count(*), avg(dwell_min),
       (SELECT count(DISTINCT hour_id) < 24
          FROM silver.scans WHERE day_id = :day) AS is_approximate
FROM silver.scans WHERE day_id = :day
GROUP BY hub_id;

Fan-out: one branch, many children

Parallel Split

Use it when several independent things all need the same upstream result.

We are replacing a legacy processor written in a language nobody here reads any more. Reverse-engineering may not be perfect, so during migration we write the processed dataset to both the old location and the new one, and consumers cut over when they are ready. One parent, two independent children.

At the orchestration layer this is trivial — declare two dependencies on the same task. At the processing layer there are three things to get right. Read the input once: two writers over the same source will happily read it twice unless you materialise the shared intermediate (a temp table, a cached dataset). Avoid shared mutable state: anything the branches both touch must be read-only, or safely concurrent. Size the compute for both branches running at once, not one.

Watch out. Blocked execution. In a pipeline where each run requires the previous one to have succeeded, the whole schedule now advances at the pace of the slowest branch — and if that branch fails, later runs do not start at all. If one branch is reliably slow, export it to its own pipeline with a dataset- or task-based dependency. Hardware mismatch. If one child is CPU-bound and the other memory-bound, a single job cannot be right-sized for either. Generate the intermediate dataset, then launch the two children as separate jobs on appropriate hardware. Double writes on retry. Two writers plus a retry is a duplicate factory. Some formats offer write-deduplication keyed on an application id plus a batch version, which makes a replayed write a no-op; where that is unavailable, use §04.

Exclusive Choice

Use it when exactly one of several branches should run, decided at runtime.

The migration worked. Now the new job should be used for runs from 1 January onward, while backfills of earlier dates must still use the old one — and we want one pipeline, so the execution history stays in one place.

Structurally it is the Parallel Split plus one extra task in front: a condition evaluator that inspects the run's context and returns which branch to follow. Every mainstream orchestrator has a first-class construct for this — a branch operator, an if-condition activity. In the processing layer, it is your language's if/else, or a factory that returns the right writer for the configured output type.

Watch out. Complexity compounds fast. Nested if/else in code is normal; nested if/else in a pipeline graph is a maze, because each condition is a visible branch that merges or splits again. There is no magic threshold — use the rubber-duck test: explain the pipeline out loud to an imaginary new joiner, and if the explanation needs a diagram and five clarifications, it is too complex. Sometimes the right answer is two pipelines with different validity windows rather than one with a router. Hidden logic. A condition buried in the job means a job with several possible outcomes and nothing in the graph saying so. In three months nobody, including you, will remember. Prefer evaluating the condition in the orchestration layer and invoking a different entry point. Heavy conditions cost real money. A condition that reads data to decide something adds a data pass to every run; a condition on metadata — a schema, a partition's existence, a row count from statistics — is orders of magnitude cheaper. Prefer metadata.

Orchestration: how many at once?

Single Runner

Use it when a run depends on the previous run's output, so overlapping runs would produce wrong answers.

The Incremental Sessionizer is the canonical case: pending sessions from run N are input to run N+1. Two concurrent runs would each read a stale pending set and each write a conflicting one. So the pipeline is configured to allow exactly one active run, and each task additionally requires its own previous execution to have succeeded.

Where an orchestrator has no such setting, you can emulate it with a Readiness Marker that waits on the previous run's completion — with the caveat that it does not prevent future runs from being scheduled, it only makes them queue.

Watch out. Backfills are as slow as they are long. Thirty days of history means thirty sequential runs, and there is no shortcut, because the sequentiality is a correctness requirement rather than a configuration choice. The one thing worth checking is whether you need the whole pipeline: if only the load step's configuration changed, replay the load step and skip the processing. Stragglers compound. An hourly pipeline that starts taking ninety minutes does not just run late once — it runs later and later, and every downstream consumer inherits the drift. That is a scaling or optimisation problem, and it needs to be caught by the SLA detector in §10 rather than by a consumer's complaint.

Concurrent Runner

Use it when runs are genuinely independent, and serialising them is just slow.

The ingestion pipeline pulls independent reference datasets from external sources. Usually 30–60 minutes, occasionally much longer — and because it was configured as a Single Runner, one slow run delays every following delivery for no reason at all. Nothing about dataset N depends on dataset N−1, so raise the concurrency limit and let the orchestrator pick up the next scheduled run while the current one is still going.

Watch out. Resource starvation, especially on a shared orchestrator. Several teams with generous concurrency limits all backfilling on the same afternoon can leave the scheduler with no capacity for anyone else. The proper fix is workload management — allocating a share of capacity per team, so a high local limit cannot consume more than the team's slice. Serverless orchestrators mostly make this a non-issue with very high concurrency ceilings. Shared state is where this bites. Any pipeline touching a common structure becomes non-deterministic under concurrency. The Dynamic Late Data Integrator is the exact example: without its in-progress flag, concurrent runs either trigger the same backfill several times or, worse, all skip it. If your pipeline has a state table, concurrency is a design decision, not a config value.

07.Data Security — nine patterns for deletion, access, protection and connections

Vertical Partitioner · In-Place Overwriter · Fine-Grained Accessor for Tables · Fine-Grained Accessor for Resources · Encryptor · Anonymizer · Pseudo-Anonymizer · Secrets Pointer · Secretless Connector

A valuable dataset is also a liability. Privacy law gives individuals rights over data about them — including the right to have it deleted — and gives you obligations regardless of how inconvenient your storage layout is. Beyond compliance there are three more concerns: access control (a neighbouring team should not be able to overwrite your table by accident), data protection (someone with the files should still not have the data), and connectivity (the credentials your job uses to reach a database should not be sitting in a Git repository).

These nine patterns cover all four, and unlike most of this guide they are as much about infrastructure as about pipelines.

Data removal: honouring a deletion request

Vertical Partitioner (the security specialisation)

Use it when you are designing storage now, and want deletion requests to be cheap later.

Design review on our new deletion pipeline turned up an obvious inefficiency: several attributes of a scan never change and are repeated on every single row — the courier's name, their staff id, their contact details. Two thousand scans for one courier means two thousand copies of the same personal data. Storing each immutable property once means having far less to delete.

So split the row. Vertical partitioning divides a record by attribute and writes the parts to different places, as opposed to horizontal partitioning, which moves whole rows. Pick the columns to separate — typically mutable versus immutable, or ordinary versus sensitive — and pick the key that lets you rejoin them. Then the ingestion job issues several projections instead of one write, each landing in its own store, and the sensitive attributes exist exactly once per person.

The deletion pipeline becomes almost trivially cheap. It also gains a second benefit: because personal data now lives in its own physical store, you can give it its own retention rules and its own access policy, which is much harder when it is smeared across a fact table.

Watch out. Reads get slower. You have normalised, so reconstructing a full row means a join, and in a distributed engine a join means network traffic where previously everything was local. Queries get more confusing. Consumers must know that half the entity is elsewhere. Mitigate with a view that presents the whole row, documentation in a catalog, and visible lineage — see §10. Polyglot storage multiplies the work. If the same record is also served from a search index and a key-value store for latency reasons, you need a removal path in each. Raw data is not covered. The pattern starts at the first transformation; the untouched Bronze copy still holds everything. Short retention on raw data is the usual answer, though it reduces how far back you can backfill.

In-Place Overwriter

Use it when the layout already exists and nobody is funding a migration.

We inherited terabytes of time-partitioned data with no personal-data strategy at all, still heavily used, and now in scope for a deletion obligation. There is no clever layout to exploit, so you delete the hard way.

If the store supports in-place operations this is a DELETE ... WHERE, followed by a space-reclamation step — because on a format with time travel, deleting rows without reclaiming their blocks means the personal data is still readable in an older version of the table. If the store does not support deletes, which is the case for plain JSON or CSV files, you simulate it: read everything, filter out the affected records, write the result. That is a full pass over the dataset.

Do not overwrite the public location directly. A crash halfway through a rewrite loses data with no way back. Write the filtered result to a private staging area, and only when it is complete promote it into place. The promotion can still fail, but a failed promotion leaves a fully computed correct dataset sitting in staging, ready to retry.

On a key-based log store there is a neat variant: send a tombstone — the record's key with a null payload — and let compaction remove the older versions. It requires key-based records and a compaction policy, and it only really works when there is one record per key.

Watch out. I/O, and lots of it. Reading and rewriting can nearly double storage while it runs. Columnar formats with per-block statistics soften this considerably: if the engine can see from the metadata that a file contains no rows for the affected person, it skips the file entirely. Cost scales with occurrences. Two thousand scans for one courier means two thousand rows touched, versus one row under the Vertical Partitioner. Batch deletion requests together rather than running the pipeline per request. Rollback is gone. Once you have overwritten the public dataset you cannot re-run a buggy deletion job against the original — so put the Proxy pattern or storage-level versioning in front of it before you need it. Retention as a strategy: if your retention window is shorter than the legal deadline, the data expires on its own. That is a legitimate design and absolutely one to confirm with legal and your data protection officer rather than decide in a code review.

Access control: who may see what

Fine-Grained Accessor for Tables

Use it when table-level permissions are too coarse — a user may query the table but not every column or every row.

Users and groups with grants on silver.scans is the easy half. The awkward half is that a hub supervisor should see rows for their own hub and not others', and should not see the courier contact columns at all.

Column level has three implementations. The simplest is a grant naming the permitted columns, so a SELECT * from an unauthorised user fails outright. The second uses a data catalog: tag the protected columns with a policy tag, then grant users a reader role per tag. The third is masking: everyone can select the column, and its content is replaced with a placeholder unless the caller is in the right group — friendlier, because queries do not break, and weaker, because the column's existence is public.

Row level works by injecting a predicate into every query on the protected table. Different platforms call it a row filter, row-level security, or a row access policy; the mechanic is the same. Where it does not exist natively, a view with a session-based condition gets you most of the way — the same view returns different rows per caller.

Watch out. Row-level rules can only see what the session knows — user name, group membership, sometimes IP. If the rule needs business context that is not in the session, the pattern will not express it. Nested types resist column permissions. A field three levels inside a struct is not a column, so you cannot grant on it; you have to flatten it out into its own column or expose it through a materialised dataset that does support permissions. Query overhead. Predicates and masking functions are evaluated on every query. If that becomes measurable, materialising a per-group dataset is the escape hatch — at the price of duplicated data and more policies to govern, which is how you end up with the governance problem you were avoiding.

-- Column level: the user can no longer run SELECT *.
GRANT SELECT (parcel_id, event_time, hub_id, scan_type)
  ON silver.scans TO hub_supervisor;

-- Row level: one policy, evaluated per caller, on every query.
ALTER TABLE silver.scans ENABLE ROW LEVEL SECURITY;
CREATE POLICY own_hub_only ON silver.scans
  USING (hub_id = current_setting('app.hub_id'));

Fine-Grained Accessor for Resources

Use it when the thing to protect is not a table — it is a bucket, a stream, a queue, a function.

A security audit found that any of our processing jobs could overwrite any dataset in the object store, because they all share one broad role. The recommendation is least privilege: each component gets only the permissions it actually needs, so a job can touch its own dataset and nothing else.

Cloud providers offer two models, and it is worth knowing which one you are using. Resource-based attaches the policy to the thing being protected: the bucket itself says who may read it. Identity-based attaches the policy to the actor: the job assumes a role, and the role says what it may reach. Both express the same intent from opposite ends, and both support conditions rather than just names — a prefix, or a runtime condition on a tag or key, which gives you row-level-style control at the infrastructure layer.

Watch out. Least privilege versus maintainability. Done strictly, you get hundreds of tiny policies nobody can audit. The pragmatic move is prefix wildcards — one policy covering scans-* instead of forty — which does technically violate least privilege, because it also grants access to resources that do not exist yet. That is a conversation to have with your security team, not a shortcut to take quietly. Do not mix the two models in one project if you can avoid it; two sources of truth for "who can read this" is how access reviews become archaeology. Quotas exist. Providers cap custom policies and custom roles, and least privilege is exactly the practice that generates lots of both.

Data protection: making the bytes useless to strangers

Encryptor

Use it as the layer that survives an access-control failure.

Fine-grained access controls the logical path to the data. They do nothing about someone who obtains the physical files, or who intercepts traffic between our jobs and the broker. Encryption makes both cases useless to the attacker.

At rest comes in two shapes that differ only in who holds the key. Client-side: the producer encrypts before sending, and manages the key. Server-side: the store does the encryption and decryption, and a managed key service holds the key. Server-side sounds harder and is dramatically easier in practice — cloud providers offer it on essentially every storage and streaming service, and the flow is fully abstracted: a request arrives, the store asks the key service for the decryption key, the key service checks whether this caller is authorised to decrypt, the store decrypts and returns. Your job is to configure the strategy and manage two independent permissions — access to the store, and access to the key.

In transit is simpler still: enable secure transport in the client and enforce a minimum protocol version on the service, so old clients cannot negotiate a weak channel.

Watch out. CPU. Every read and write does extra work. Usually acceptable, occasionally not, and worth measuring rather than assuming. Losing the key is losing the data. This is the pattern's real risk and it locks out authorised users. Managed key services mitigate it with a mandatory deletion grace period during which an accidentally deleted key can be restored — set that window deliberately, do not leave it at whatever the default is. In-transit encryption is a maintenance commitment. Protocol versions get deprecated after vulnerabilities are found, and anything pinned to an old one has to be upgraded. On managed services this is usually a one-line change, which is not the same as no work.

Anonymizer

Use it when a dataset must leave your boundary and the recipient must not be able to identify anyone.

An external analytics firm has been contracted to study delivery-failure patterns. Our data carries plenty of personal attributes, and not every individual consented to third-party sharing, so the shared extract has to be genuinely anonymous.

Three techniques, in increasing sophistication. Removal — drop the columns; easiest and most destructive. Perturbation — inject noise so the value loses its meaning while keeping its rough shape. Synthetic replacement — substitute values from a generator that understands the column's type, so a country becomes a different plausible country and an address becomes a different plausible address. The first two are column transformations you can write today; the third usually means a model, and a conversation with the data science team.

Watch out. Information loss is the point and also the problem. The recipient cannot use the removed or scrambled columns — which is fine until someone builds a model on the survivors and reports conclusions that were never supported by the data they had. Say clearly, in the dataset's documentation, which columns are synthetic or perturbed. A silently perturbed column is worse than a missing one.

Pseudo-Anonymizer

Use it when the recipient still needs the column to behave like the original — to group by it, join on it, or match it.

The anonymised extract came back with a complaint: too many columns are gone, and most of the questions cannot be answered. So we need protected values that are still usable, which means pseudo-anonymisation and four choices.

Masking — replace part of the value with placeholder characters, keeping a recognisable remainder. Tokenisation — swap real values for fake ones and keep the mapping in a secured vault; reversible by design, so the vault's access control is the security boundary. Hashing — replace irreversibly, while keeping equality, so the same person hashes to the same token in every table and joins still work. Encryption — reversible for whoever holds the key.

Watch out. The false sense of security is the headline risk, and it comes from combining datasets. Take two pseudo-anonymised tables. One says user 1000 likes carrots and broccoli. The other says user 1000 is a C******h P*******r i******r living in S*n M****o. Neither identifies anybody alone. Together, with a shortlist of countries matching that mask, they identify a specific person. True anonymisation would have replaced those fields with "Europe" and "Software engineer", which survives the combination. Pseudo-anonymised personal data is still personal data — treat it as in scope for the same rules. Collisions and loss. Masking maps different inputs onto the same output, so two distinct people can become indistinguishable — which silently corrupts counts. Type loss. Generalising a numeric salary into a band turns an integer into a string, and every downstream cast breaks.

Connectivity: reaching the database without leaking the keys

Secrets Pointer

Use it when a login and password are unavoidable, and you refuse to keep them in code.

Our enrichment job calls a third-party geolocation API that authenticates only with a login and password pair. Last year a similar credential leaked from a repository and, because the API bills per request, the leak became an invoice. So the code should reference the secret rather than contain it.

Store the value in a secrets manager and put its name in the code. At runtime the job asks the manager for the value, optionally caching it briefly. You get three things: one central place to audit access, painless rotation (change it once, no consumer changes), and two independent layers of protection — the caller must be allowed to read the secret and the secret must be valid.

There is a nice side effect for multi-environment setups: keep the same secret names everywhere and let your infrastructure-as-code generate different values per environment. The code needs no per-environment configuration at all.

Watch out. Caching versus rotation. Cache the credential and you cannot know whether it is still current; do not cache it and you pay a lookup per use. For a long-running streaming job this matters: the simplest correct answer is to let the job fail on an auth error and reload on restart — which is only acceptable if the job is idempotent, and is annoying if rotation is frequent. An async refresh is possible and opens a window where a connection is mid-write when the credential changes. Logs. The secret is safe in the manager and entirely unsafe in a debug log line that prints the connection properties. Someone still writes the secret. The pattern removes consumers from the equation, not producers — a human administrator or an IaC stack still generates the value, and that path needs the same care.

Secretless Connector

Use it when there is a way to have no credential to manage in the first place.

A small team is onboarding a new managed service, every example uses API keys, and they do not want to own key rotation. On a cloud platform they do not have to.

Two implementations. Identity-based: the job runs as something — a service account, an assumed role — and the platform's identity service decides what it may reach. There is no credential in the job at all; the request arrives at the service, the service asks the identity layer whether this caller is permitted, and answers accordingly. Certificate-based: the same flow with a certificate authority in the middle, validating certificates before the connection proceeds.

Watch out. "-less" does not mean effortless. You still configure the identity, its trust relationship and its permissions — the assume-role setup that lets a service fetch temporary credentials on the job's behalf is real work, just work you do once. Certificates need rotating, and rotating anything shared with consumers is a three-step dance: issue and distribute the new credential, support old and new simultaneously while consumers migrate at their own pace, and only then retire the old one. Skipping the middle step is how a rotation becomes an outage.

~/dedp/storage ❯ where the bytes actually go

08.Data Storage — nine patterns for layout, skipping and shape

Horizontal Partitioner · Vertical Partitioner · Bucket · Sorter · Metadata Enhancer · Dataset Materializer · Manifest · Normalizer · Denormalizer

There are two ways to make a query faster. Add compute — quick, requires no coordination with anyone, and you are usually doing it under pressure after users have already complained. Or lay the data out so the query has less to read — slower to arrange, needs to be decided before the data is written, and it is the one that keeps paying.

This family is the second kind. It splits into four questions: how do you divide a dataset, how do you colocate records inside a division, how do you avoid reading things at all, and what shape should the tables be.

Partitioning: dividing the dataset

Horizontal Partitioner

Use it when queries consistently restrict on the same low-cardinality column — most often time.

A job computes rolling four-day aggregates. It ran fine for months; now the filter that discards rows older than four days is the slowest thing in it, because it inspects the whole table to throw away most of it. More compute papered over it and raised the bill.

Horizontal partitioning gives that filter nothing to do. You choose a partitioning column — also called a distribution key — and the store writes rows into a physically separate location per value. A query restricting on that column then reads only the relevant locations, and the ones it does not need cost nothing.

Time keys dominate, and it is worth being precise about which time. From the execution context: every row a run writes goes into the partition named after the run, so one run touches exactly one partition. From the data: the partition comes from each row's own event time, so a single run can write into several — which is exactly how late data ends up modifying old partitions. Non-time keys work too — a partner id, a region — and can be nested, giving paths like date=2026-08-20/region=north.

Two ways to declare it. Declaratively, in the table definition, where the producer needs to know nothing. Or producer-driven, where the writing job names the partition column, possibly computing it. Producer-driven is more flexible — the partition value can be derived — and it means the layout logic lives in a job rather than in the schema.

Worth remembering: partitioning is also what makes idempotency cheap. The Fast Metadata Cleaner from §04 exists only because partitions and tables can be dropped wholesale.

Watch out. Granularity is the mistake everyone makes once. Partition our scans by parcel_id and you get one partition per parcel — millions of directories holding one file each, catastrophic listing times, and the small-files problem the Compactor exists to fix. The rule of thumb: partition on low-cardinality columns. Event time truncated to the hour or the day is ideal because many rows share each value. High-cardinality columns want the Bucket pattern instead. Partitioning does not guarantee balance. One much larger partition sets the pace for everything, and in a micro-batch streaming model it blocks the next batch. A backpressure buffer helps — hold the excess and process it next round — which delays the skewed partition on purpose so the others stay near real time. Changing the key is expensive. It means physically relocating every existing row. Some formats let you evolve the partition spec instantly, but only at the metadata level: old files stay where they are, and only new data follows the new layout.

Partitioning vs sharding

Sharding splits a dataset across machines — a physical division at the hardware level. Horizontal partitioning splits it across locations, which may all live on the same machine.

So sharding is a special case of horizontal partitioning, not a different idea. The words get used interchangeably and the distinction only matters when someone says "we're partitioned" and means "we're spread over twelve nodes".

Vertical Partitioner (the storage specialisation)

Use it when the same unchanging attributes are repeated on every row of a large table.

You met this pattern in §07, where the motivation was cheap deletion. The mechanism is identical; only the reason changes. Here the reason is storage and write throughput: a scan carries a handful of fields that change per event (time, hub, scan type) and a handful that are constant for the whole parcel journey (origin address, service level, dimensions). Storing the constants on every event wastes space and makes updating them a mass rewrite.

Classify the attributes into groups, pick the key that rejoins them, and have the producer write each group to its own location. As a bonus you can now apply different retention and access policies per group, which is nearly impossible while everything shares one row.

Watch out. Domain split — related attributes now live apart, and only documentation makes that discoverable. Querying — the full picture needs a join, so give consumers a view that does it for them. The producer pays too. It can no longer take a row and write it; it has to split, and issue several writes, at some extra network cost. That cost is real and is usually smaller than what the readers save.

Records organisation: colocating inside a division

Bucket

Use it when the column queries filter on has too many distinct values to partition by.

Eighty per cent of queries on our scan table filter on parcel_id. That is precisely the column we cannot partition on — millions of distinct values means millions of partitions and a metadata quota breach.

Bucketing colocates groups of values rather than single values. Choose the bucket column, choose a bucket count, and the store places each row using a hash of the key modulo the count. If the table is already horizontally partitioned, treat the bucket key as a secondary grouping key inside each partition.

That layout unlocks two optimisations. Bucket pruning: given parcel_id = 'X', the engine computes X's bucket directly and skips every other bucket — no scan, just arithmetic. Shuffle elimination: if two tables are bucketed on the same column with the same bucket count, a join between them can pair up matching buckets locally, avoiding the network exchange from §05.

Watch out. The scheme is effectively immutable. Changing the column or the count changes where every key hashes to, so it means rewriting the table. Choosing the count is a guess about the future. Size it for today's volume and tomorrow's buckets are enormous; size it for a forecast you got wrong and you have thousands of near-empty ones. Both are acceptable; neither is right. A bucket is not a key lookup. Pruning narrows you to one bucket, and that bucket still holds many keys, so you scan it. Bucketing reduces the search space; it is not an index.

Sorter

Use it when you know which columns queries filter and order by, and pruning at partition level is not enough.

We moved to weekly tables for the Fast Metadata Cleaner and maintenance got much easier, but query time did not improve — the weekly table is still large, and most queries filter on event_time inside it. Sorting on disk fixes that.

Declare the sort column or columns; the store then writes rows in that order. Because each physical block records the range of values it holds (the next pattern), a query on a sorted column can eliminate whole blocks by looking only at those ranges — the values it wants simply are not in there.

Two flavours. Lexicographic sorting is the classic top-to-bottom order and is what warehouses expose as clustered or sorted tables. Curved sorting — Z-order being the famous example, available natively in modern table formats and as interleaved sort keys in some warehouses — arranges rows so that multi-dimensional locality is preserved: values close together in several columns at once end up in the same blocks. Explaining the curve properly needs its own article; the practical consequence is what matters.

Watch out. The composite-key prefix rule catches everyone. Sort lexicographically by (event_time, hub_id) and a query filtering on event_time prunes beautifully, while a query filtering only on hub_id prunes almost nothing — because for any given hub, matching rows are scattered across every time range. Curved sorting is precisely the fix for that case. Recent data is unsorted. Newly written blocks are not magically in order, so the newest data — the data most queries want — benefits least until a sort or compaction job runs. Scheduling that job inside the writer costs write latency; scheduling it outside leaves a window. Changing sort keys can rewrite the table.

Read optimisation: not reading things

Metadata Enhancer

Use it — usually by choosing the right format — when queries load everything and then throw most of it away.

Our JSON dataset is partitioned by event time, which helped the batch jobs enormously. Then analysts arrived, querying small slices within a partition, and complaining about latency and per-byte-scanned charges. Their queries read every partition file and filter afterwards. We want the filtering to happen before the read.

The pattern is to keep statistics about the stored records next to the records. In a columnar file format this is free and automatic: each file carries a footer with per-column summaries — minimum, maximum, null count, value count. A query with a predicate checks the footer first, and if the requested range is not present, the whole file is skipped. The footer is tiny compared with the data, so the check is cheap even multiplied across thousands of files.

Table formats layer more on top, recording per-commit information — rows written, per-column min and max, null counts — so some queries (a count, a filter on a value that does not exist) can be answered from metadata without touching data at all. In relational databases and warehouses the same idea lives in the catalog, and the query planner uses it to choose a plan.

Watch out. Writers pay. Computing statistics per column per file adds work at write time. Small, and real. Database statistics go stale. Refresh is usually triggered by thresholds — this many rows changed — so a table that receives steady trickles of small changes may never trip the threshold, and the planner keeps optimising against a picture of last month's table. That is a classic cause of "the same query got slow and nothing changed". A manual statistics refresh fixes it, at the price of a read pass over the table.

Dataset Materializer

Use it when the same expensive query runs over and over and the answer barely changes between runs.

We built a view unioning three weeks of partitioned tables so consumers had one place to query. They were unimpressed, correctly — a view re-executes its query every time, so it made access simpler without making it faster. Materialising the result trades storage for latency.

Identify the expensive datasets, write the query, and persist its output as either a materialised view or a plain table. The difference is refresh, and it is a real fork in the road. Materialised views can refresh automatically, which is lovely and unpredictable — the platform decides when, based on load and data volume, so it is eventual rather than immediate. A table means you own refresh entirely, and in exchange you get every storage optimisation in this section: partitioning, bucketing, sorting, all of which are typically unavailable to a materialised view.

Watch out. Refresh is the whole cost. A full refresh re-runs a query you already described as expensive, and competes with everyone else on the database. Incremental refresh is the fix and it is conditional: it works cleanly for insert-only sources where history does not change, and platforms that support it support only a subset of SQL, silently falling back to a full refresh when your query steps outside. Verify what actually happens rather than trusting the feature name. Governance flattens. The materialised dataset combines several tables, and a user with access to it effectively has access to all of them. If they were not allowed one of the inputs, the materialisation has just granted it. Storage. Materialising selectively — some parts persisted, others recomputed — is a legitimate middle ground.

Manifest

Use it when listing the files is a meaningful share of your query time.

Our Parquet dataset made batch jobs fast and cheap. Then we exposed it to a warehouse layer for analysts, and it was slow again — this time not because of reading data, but because of enumerating which files exist. On object storage that is a lot of API calls.

The answer is to record the file list once instead of discovering it every time. Table formats already do exactly this: each commit writes the set of files it created into the metadata location, so a reader assembles the current file list from commit records with no listing whatsoever. That commit log is a manifest. Where you are not using such a format, you can maintain one yourself — worth it particularly when many readers share one dataset, as in the fan-out patterns from §06.

Manifests are also a writing tool. A bulk-load command that takes an explicit list of files to load is idempotent by construction: replay it with the same manifest and you load the same files, no more and no fewer. That makes it a neat companion to the idempotency patterns.

Watch out. One more step in the flow — though listing recently written files is about as simple as pipeline steps get, and far more predictable than a slow full listing. Manifests grow. With many small files or a continuous streaming writer, a manifest can reach gigabytes; early versions of at least one popular streaming framework had jobs that could not restart because the manifest was too large to load. That specific bug is fixed, and the general shape is not — check whether your implementation has a size cap or a retention policy for manifest entries.

Data representation: what shape should the tables be?

Normalizer

Use it when consistency matters more than query latency — typically for anything that gets updated.

A colleague reviewed our scan model and pointed at obvious duplication: every scan row repeats the courier's depot city and the service level's SLA hours. Both are properties of something else, both are repeated millions of times, and updating either means a mass rewrite.

Normalising means representing each fact once. Whichever variant you pick, the design process is the same: list the business entities, describe each one's attributes, then define the relationships between them.

Normal forms are the transactional variant. Three of them cover the vast majority of real work:

1NF — no repeating groups; atomic values; each row uniquely identified. Broken: a parcels table with a scan_history array column. Fix: give scans their own table.

2NF — every non-key column depends on the whole key. Broken: a table keyed on (hub_id, day) that also carries hub_country — which depends on the hub alone, not on the day. Fix: move it to a hubs table.

3NF — no transitive dependencies between non-key columns. Broken: parcels(parcel_id, courier_id, courier_depot_city) — the depot city depends on the courier, which depends on the parcel. Fix: a couriers table.

The analytical variant is the snowflake schema: one fact table describing an observation (a scan), surrounded by dimension tables describing it (hub, courier, date, service level), where dimensions may themselves be described by further dimensions — a date dimension normalised into month and quarter sub-dimensions. Same principle, different vocabulary.

Watch out. Joins, and in a distributed engine joins mean network traffic. Two mitigations both work: colocate small dimension tables with the big fact table so joining stays local, or broadcast the small tables to every node so no exchange is needed. If a table is too big to broadcast, filter it first. Dimensions change over time. If the service level's SLA hours changed in March, "what was the SLA for this parcel" needs the value as of the parcel's date, not today's — which is slowly changing dimensions again, from §05.

Denormalizer

Use it when read latency is the binding constraint and the data is effectively a snapshot.

A team asked for help: a relational model on their warehouse, fine for months, now unusable because 80% of their queries join eight tables and the data volume finally caught up with them.

Denormalising flattens joined values into one row so there is nothing to exchange. Two techniques. Regular columns: every column from every joined table copied in, accessible directly — the layout usually called One Big Table. Or nested structures: the joined rows collapsed into a struct-typed column, so consumers reach into the column instead of joining. The dimensional-model version is the star schema: like the snowflake, but dimensions never describe other dimensions — the sub-dimension attributes are flattened into the top-level dimension table.

The two patterns are not rivals. A common and sensible arrangement is a normalised model as the private source of truth and a denormalised model built on top of it for serving, kept in step by the sequence patterns from §06.

Watch out. Updates get expensive. A courier changes depot and you rewrite every row carrying the old one. The clean escape is to define the denormalised table as a snapshot — what the world looked like at time T, never updated — which turns the problem into a non-problem. Otherwise, accept slower writes for faster reads. Storage grows, though dictionary encoding takes most of that back: repeated long strings are stored once and referenced by a small integer, which also lets the engine answer some existence checks from the dictionary alone. One Big Table becomes an anti-pattern without domain discipline. If a table gathers a parcel's current scan, the customer's past orders, and their marketing preferences, it is not a table, it is a bin bag — technically queryable, semantically meaningless. The naming test works well: try to name it, and if you need "and" or "with" more than once, you have combined unrelated things.

~/dedp/quality ❯ earn the trust

09.Data Quality — six patterns for datasets people can rely on

Audit-Write-Audit-Publish · Constraints Enforcer · Schema Compatibility Enforcer · Schema Migrator · Offline Observer · Online Observer

A dataset nobody trusts is worse than no dataset, because people build on it anyway and then discover the problem downstream of a decision. Trust is lost to three specific things: incompleteness, inaccuracy and inconsistency. This family attacks them at three levels — gates in the pipeline, constraints in the store, and observation that keeps your gates and constraints current as the data changes underneath them.

Quality enforcement: stopping bad data at the door

Audit-Write-Audit-Publish

Use it when publishing something wrong would cost more than publishing late.

Our daily hub statistics showed unique-courier counts dropping 50% for a week. The operations team treated it as a real signal and launched an intervention. It was a bug in our aggregation. The ask afterwards was reasonable: make sure that cannot happen quietly again.

The pattern adds gates around the transformation — assertions, but on real data rather than fixtures. The first gate audits the input before you spend anything transforming it, and should stay cheap: file format valid, size in a plausible range, schema present and correct, the header actually has the columns you expect. You can validate the full input here, and then you have read the dataset twice, so usually you should not.

The second gate audits the output. This is the expensive one and where the real value is: no nulls in non-nullable columns, volume within a sane band of yesterday, keys distinct, aggregates non-negative. Then, and only then, publish.

Resist the urge to call a repeated check redundant. Validating nulls on the input asks "did the provider hold up their end?"; validating nulls on the output asks "did my transformation invent them?". Same function, different question, different owner. Where you truly cannot afford both passes, put the check in the more exhaustive place — the output gate catches both causes at once.

Failure is not the only outcome. You can dispatch: publish the valid rows, park the invalid ones (which looks like Dead-Letter and differs in intent — this is a deliberate rule firing, not an unexpected exception). Or run a non-blocking audit: publish anyway and annotate the dataset with what was wrong, so consumers who do not use the affected column can proceed and those who do can decide.

Streaming works too, in two shapes. Window-based: the job accumulates a processing-time window, audits the buffer when it closes, and applies one of the three outcomes. Staging-based: the job writes to a private staging table unchanged, and a separate audit job promotes from staging to the public table. Note that streaming naturally drops the input audit — there is no "file" to inspect, only a continuous arrival — which is that exhaustiveness rule applying itself.

Watch out. Compute cost. Metadata-level checks are nearly free; row-level checks are a full pass. That is the price of not publishing nonsense. Your rules are a snapshot of today's understanding. An AWAP-guarded pipeline is not "100% reliable", it is "reliable against the rules you thought of". Which is exactly why the observer patterns below exist. Streaming latency — a distribution check over a window delays delivery by the window's length. A detected issue may not be an issue. A parcel-volume spike from a successful campaign trips a volume check while being completely correct. So not every audit failure should halt the pipeline; some should raise an alert and ask for a human look.

Constraints Enforcer

Use it when a rule is universal enough that the store should refuse violations, not your job.

A batch pipeline has been writing the scan table cleanly for months. Suddenly nulls appear in required fields. The job is already complicated and nobody wants to add a validation layer to it. So do not — let the table refuse the write.

Identify the attributes that need rules — a business decision driven by the product or by regulation, with no generic answer — and attach constraints of four kinds:

Type — this column is always a timestamp. Part of the schema, and the foundation the next two patterns build on.

Nullability — required or optional. Doubles as documentation: an optional column tells consumers they must handle missing values.

Value — a comparison against an expression. event_time <= now() stops timestamps from the future; weight_kg BETWEEN 0 AND 1000 stops nonsense.

Integrity — a referenced value must exist elsewhere. A scan referencing a hub that is not in the hubs table is rejected. Mostly a transactional-database feature, and mostly paired with the Normalizer.

This is not only a database feature. Table formats expose value checks; serialisation formats enforce types natively and, with an extra validation library, value rules too. And the pattern does double duty — it is informative for consumers, who learn the dataset's shape from it, and interactive for producers, who get told immediately when they break it.

Watch out. All-or-nothing, plus stop-at-first-error. One bad row rejects the whole transaction, and the store usually reports only the first problem — so a file with six distinct issues becomes six round trips. If that loop is painful, run the same rules producer-side to compile the full list first, accepting that you lose the informative-and-interactive benefit of having them declared on the store. It is producer-oriented. A column the table calls optional may be mandatory for one particular consumer, who therefore still needs their own filter. Constraints reduce validation work; they do not remove it. Coverage gaps. Table formats often cannot express integrity constraints at all, so some rules have to stay in the AWAP audit.

Schema consistency

Schema Compatibility Enforcer

Use it when a producer can break your job by changing their schema, and you would rather they were stopped than apologise.

Our sessionizer ran for months, then failed repeatedly because the upstream team removed fields they believed were unused. They were used. The fix is not a better conversation; it is a mechanism that makes the breaking change impossible to ship.

Three enforcement modes, depending on your stack. External service or library — a schema registry that versions every schema and validates each change against a configured compatibility rule, which is the most explicit and most controllable option. Implicit at insert time — a table format or relational database that rejects data not matching the declared schema; effective, with no way to declare an explicit compatibility policy. Event-driven on DDL — triggers that run before a schema-changing statement commits and can roll it back; and for the blunt version, simply not granting anyone permission to alter the table.

Then choose what evolution consumers should be prepared for:

BackwardDelete a field · add an optional fieldA consumer on the new schema can read data written with an old one
ForwardAdd a field · delete an optional fieldA consumer on an old schema can read data written with a new one

…and each can be transitiveSame list of actionsNon-transitive checks only against the neighbouring version. Transitive checks against all versions

The transitive distinction confuses people because the allowed actions are identical. An example makes it concrete. Start with Parcel { parcel_id LONG required }. Backward compatibility means a new field must be optional, so v1 adds weight_kg DOUBLE default 0.0. Then the product team asks for the default to be removed, because a zero weight produces misleading analytics — so v2 makes weight_kg required. Against v1 that is fine: a v2 consumer reading v1 data gets the default filled in. Against v0 it is not: v0 data has no weight at all and no default to fall back on. Same change, allowed non-transitively, forbidden transitively.

Watch out. Producing data now costs an extra round trip. With a registry in the loop, the producer has to check every record against the newest schema version before it can send anything — a small price per record, and one more service that has to be up for your producers to work at all. Evolution gets harder, and that is the deal. Every change now has to satisfy the compatibility level you picked, so the obvious one-liner is often simply forbidden: renaming a field becomes "add the new one, deprecate the old one" instead of a single statement. That friction is what you bought — it is the reason nobody is woken at 4am — and working within it is exactly what the next pattern is for.

Schema Migrator

Use it when you need a genuinely breaking change — a rename, a retype, a removal — without breaking anyone.

Our scan event has grown to sixty attributes because we kept adding fields rather than bothering consumers. Now nobody can find anything, and consumers are asking us to group related attributes — a courier object rather than six loose courier_* columns. The Compatibility Enforcer cannot help: its job is to prevent exactly this.

The pattern is a transition window, applied to three scenarios. A rename, when a name is wrong or unclear. A type change, when you are restructuring or optimising — a heterogeneous datetime string becoming a proper timestamp. A removal, easy if genuinely nobody reads the field and impossible if somebody does.

For renames and retypes: add the new field alongside the old, agree a deadline with consumers, emit both for the whole window, and only then publish a version without the old one. For removals: agree the deadline, then drop. In both cases the deadline is the entire pattern, and it needs to be a real agreement rather than an announcement.

Note the prerequisite: this only works with non-transitive compatibility. Under transitive rules no removal or rename is ever permitted, because compatibility with the very first version is guaranteed forever.

Watch out. Size, during the window and sometimes after. Emitting old and new means paying for both in storage, network and I/O. Some serialisation formats warn explicitly against hundreds of fields, because each one costs bytes even when unpopulated, and generated builders can hit compiler limits. There is a subtler variant: some table formats collect column statistics only for the first N columns by default, so a migration that pushes columns past that boundary silently loses the data-skipping from §08. Some fields cannot be removed. If a consumer depends on one and you have no substitute, the honest outcome is that the removal does not happen — and knowing whether anyone depends on it is what the column-level lineage in §10 is for.

How not to do it

The tempting one-liner is ALTER TABLE ... RENAME COLUMN from_hub TO origin_hub. It runs instantly, looks clean, and every consumer query referencing from_hub now fails with "column does not exist" — including the ones that were going to run at 4am, and the ones belonging to teams that have never heard of you.

The migration version is ALTER TABLE ... ADD COLUMN origin_hub, populate both, wait out the agreed window, then drop. Slower, boring, and nobody is woken up.

Quality observation: keeping your rules honest

Observation is not auditing

An audit validates and blocks — it detects a problem and stops the pipeline. Observation measures and does not block — it makes problems visible and lets the pipeline proceed.

Why you need both: audits only catch what you thought to check for. Observation is how you find out what you should have been checking — a null rate creeping up, a distribution shifting, a new field appearing in the input that nobody has told you about.

Offline Observer

Use it when the measurement must not be able to affect the pipeline it measures.

A new pipeline is running cleanly: fully structured data, all business rules enforced. From experience, this will not last — the upstream dataset will drift over the coming months. So we want to track properties over time (value distributions, null counts per column, fields appearing in the input that we do not consume yet) with zero risk to the production flow.

Build the observation as its own pipeline, on its own schedule, writing to your monitoring layer. It reads what the generator produced and computes whatever your context makes meaningful. Because it is decoupled it can run whenever suits — for example overnight, when the generators are quiet, so the two never compete for resources.

One implementation detail worth stealing: make the observation itself idempotent by recording the boundaries of what it examined — the first and last row ids, or a version range — before computing anything. Then a re-run analyses exactly the same rows and produces exactly the same numbers, even though the underlying table has moved on.

Watch out. Insight arrives late by construction. A weekly observer finds Monday's problem on Sunday, by which time every downstream consumer has processed it. Running it less often can cost more, not less. An hourly generator with a daily observer means the observer processes 24 hours of data in one go and needs compute sized for that. Sampling is the obvious mitigation and it trades away the thing you wanted — rare anomalies are exactly what a sample misses.

Online Observer

Use it when finding out a week later is not acceptable.

Analysts reported an unexpected postcode format. Our offline observer did catch it — six days after it started, and one day after the analysts did. Nothing about that ordering is acceptable, so the observation moves inside the pipeline.

Same metrics, different placement: the observation job becomes part of the generating pipeline, so insight lands moments after the data. For a batch pipeline you attach it after the transform, as either a Parallel Split (observation and loading run at once — faster) or a Local Sequencer step (observation runs after loading — slower, and it observes what consumers will actually see).

For streaming there is no choice: the observation logic lives inside the job.

Watch out. Sequencer placement adds latency to every run, forever. Parallel Split placement can observe the wrong thing. Subtle and important: if you observe the transformed dataset in parallel with the load, and the load is what drops values — a datetime format the target rejects, silently nulled — the observer sees a clean dataset and the consumers see a broken table. Observing after the load avoids it. In streaming, the observer can take the job down with it. An out-of-memory error or an unhandled exception in observation code kills a job that was doing its real work fine. Sampling reduces the exposure and the insight. In batch, guard against it with a final no-op task whose trigger rule ignores upstream failure, so an observation problem does not mark the whole run as failed.

Observability is wider than the data.

Everything above is about observing content. The same observers are also where technical metadata belongs — CPU, memory and disk of the tools themselves — and because those are near-real-time measurements, they naturally fit the online variant. §10 takes that further and observes the pipeline rather than the rows.

10.Data Observability — six patterns for detecting and tracing

Flow Interruption Detector · Skew Detector · Lag Detector · SLA Misses Detector · Dataset Tracker · Fine-Grained Tracker

Quality patterns check the data. They cannot check the things that happen around the data — and the most embarrassing incidents live there. A perfectly-written AWAP pipeline detects every issue it was designed for and detects nothing at all on the day it does not run, because an upstream flow stopped and nobody noticed.

This family fills that hole with two kinds of tool: detectors that alert on data or timing anomalies, and trackers that tell you what depends on what, so that when something is wrong you know who to talk to.

Data detectors

Flow Interruption Detector

Use it when a job could succeed while doing nothing, and you would not find out.

Our streaming job syncs scans to object storage, and many batch pipelines across several teams read the result. It worked for seven months. Then one day it consumed its input, wrote nothing, and did not fail — so no alert fired, and we learned about it from a consumer's complaint. That is a reputational failure as much as a technical one.

For streaming, first decide which arrival model you have. Continuous delivery: you expect at least one record per unit of time, so an alert fires when the count for that unit is zero. Irregular delivery: gaps are normal, so measuring per-minute counts would fire constantly and train everyone to ignore the alert. Instead measure the length of the no-data window, and alert when it exceeds what you have agreed is plausible.

For batch and at-rest data, you are measuring freshness, at one of three layers. The metadata layer is cheapest — read the table's last-modification time and alert if it has not moved within the expected interval. The data layer is the fallback when metadata is unavailable: either add a modification-time column, or count rows per evaluation period and compare — if the count is unchanged across two consecutive hours on an hourly pipeline, something stopped. The storage layer works for any file format: watch when the last file appeared in the location.

Watch out. Thresholds are the hard part. "At least one record a minute" is easy to write and probably wrong for a source doing thousands a second, where a 99% drop still passes. Deriving the threshold from historical volume is the usual approach and it generates false positives whenever the business does something unusual, which brings alarm fatigue — the failure mode where the alert works perfectly and nobody reads it. Metadata can lie in both directions. It may not exist for your store; and where it does, "last modified" can be moved by a schema change, which adds no rows at all. Storage-layer false positives. Compaction writes new files while producing no new data. From the storage layer that looks like a healthy flow, and it is not.

Skew Detector

Use it when a pipeline can run correctly over an input that is incomplete.

Consumers complained again, this time about an aggregate computed over half the expected data. Our job was fine; the input was half-empty because of a problem upstream. Detecting that means comparing sizes rather than validating rows.

Three steps. Pick the comparison window — for a daily job, today against yesterday. Set a tolerance threshold: how different may they be? Fifty per cent up or down is a common starting point, and the right number comes either from the variation you actually observe or from asking the business what a normal day looks like. Then implement the comparison, in one of two ways.

Window to window: the percentage difference between two values. Works for batch runs and for streaming windows. Standard-deviation ratio: how far each data point sits from the dataset's mean, expressed as stddev(x) / avg(x). This one is the right tool for detecting storage skew across the partitions of a topic or a table, and most stores and monitoring systems already have both functions.

Note where this belongs: it is an excellent first audit in an AWAP pipeline, acting as the guard that stops a partial input from being processed at all.

Watch out. Seasonality is the biggest problem and it is not really technical. A promotional campaign, a public holiday, a peak season — all produce legitimate 50% swings. Encoding that into a formula needs business knowledge and, realistically, exceptions for the most volatile periods. Which means communication. Knowing next week's campaign is happening turns a false positive into an expected variance, and that information lives in another department. The fatality loop. A subtle one: today's dataset is a third of yesterday's, so the check fails. Tomorrow's dataset is normal — and therefore three times "yesterday", so the check fails again, for the opposite reason. Compare against the last successful run's dataset rather than literally the previous day, and the loop cannot form.

Time detectors

Lag Detector

Use it when you need to know how far behind the producer your consumer is running.

A streaming job started receiving 30% more data. The announcement email was missed, and a week later a consumer complained about slow delivery. Before you can build a scaling policy you need the metric the policy would react to.

Choose a lag unit appropriate to the store: a record position or append time for a log, a commit version for a table format, a partition timestamp for a partitioned store. Then lag is simply last available unit minus last processed unit, which means both sides must be published — the consumer emits what it has processed, and the producer (or a probe) emits what is available.

On a partitioned source you get one lag per partition, and how you combine them changes what you learn. MAX gives the worst case, so a single bad partition is visible immediately. A percentile — P90 or P95 — tells you how the bulk is doing. Publishing both is the honest answer: the percentile for trend, the max for incidents.

Watch out. Never use the average. Take per-partition lags of 10, 5, 30, 2, 3, 5 and 3 seconds. The average is 8 seconds and reads as "healthy". The P90 is 18 seconds, and one partition is at 30. The average is not wrong arithmetically; it is wrong as a summary, because it hides exactly the tail you are monitoring for. A bad MAX may not be a consumer problem. If one partition receives far more data than the others, of course its lag is worst — and no amount of scaling the consumer fixes a distribution problem. Check the Skew Detector before you resize anything.

SLA Misses Detector

Use it when you have promised someone a completion time.

Our batch job is scheduled at 06:00 and must finish within 40 minutes, because downstream pipelines have to publish business statistics before 08:00. We have optimised it; we have not made it immune to surprises. So we need to be told when it overruns — and so do the consumers.

For a batch job it is a subtraction: end time minus start time, compared to the threshold. For a streaming job in a windowed or micro-batch model, the same subtraction per iteration. For a record-at-a-time engine with no windows, measure per record from read to write and aggregate with MAX or a percentile, using the observer patterns from §09 to collect the numbers.

Lag and SLA look interchangeable and are not. A consumer with a throughput limit processes a fixed volume per iteration and therefore always meets its SLA — while its lag grows without bound, because a skewed partition keeps arriving faster than the limit allows. And the reverse: a batch job reading a daily dataset has no lag at the moment it starts, and can still blow its SLA by taking too long. Two metrics, two failure modes.

Watch out. Event-time SLAs include other people's problems. Processing time measures your pipeline — read to write. Event time measures the whole journey — generation to write — and that includes a producer whose network dropped and who flushed a buffer forty minutes later. That is a genuine end-to-end miss and it is not a processing failure, so mixing the two into one number produces an alert nobody can act on. Track both, separately, and label them.

Data lineage: who depends on whom

Dataset Tracker

Use it when you need to know where a dataset came from and who is consuming yours.

A dataset we consume keeps changing a column's type, and our job keeps failing. Our direct provider is not the culprit — they process data from a third team. Without a dependency graph, finding the actual source is a sequence of guesses and messages.

Dataset lineage builds that graph: a family tree over data containers — tables, folders, topics, queues — annotated with who owns each one. Two ways to get it. Managed, where the platform derives dependencies from the jobs, tables and dashboards it already sees. Nothing to build; and the coverage stops at the platform's own boundary, which is often narrower than the marketing suggests — even within one cloud, some services' loading jobs are tracked and others are not.

Or custom, which starts with identifying inputs and outputs per query, task or pipeline, at one of two layers. At the orchestration layer, each pipeline reports its inputs and outputs — sometimes automatically, since an orchestrator often knows a task's dataset from its configuration. At the database layer, a job parses executed queries into trees and extracts the referenced tables. Either way you then need something to store and visualise it; the open standard in this space is OpenLineage, with integrations for the mainstream orchestrators and processing engines.

Watch out. Managed solutions see their own world. Mix in an open-source store, a second cloud, or a bespoke service, and you have a partial lineage graph — which is arguably more dangerous than none, because it looks authoritative. Custom task types need custom resolution. Automatic input/output detection works for built-in task types; anything you wrote yourself is opaque and needs its own reporting.

Fine-Grained Tracker

Use it when the question is about a column or a row, not a table.

Our denormalised parcel table has grown past thirty columns over three years, and the team turns over regularly. Dataset lineage answers most of the new joiners' questions, but not the one they ask most: which upstream columns actually feed this column? It is also the exact question the Schema Migrator needs answered before anyone can remove a field.

Column level is available natively on some platforms, usually via a catalog. Built yourself, it means analysing the query execution plan and tracing each output column back to its inputs. Given SELECT concat(c.code, '-', h.region) AS route_key FROM couriers c JOIN hubs h ..., the plan tells you route_key depends on couriers.code and hubs.region. Popular engines have this wired into the lineage frameworks already.

Row level answers a different question — which job produced this specific row — and the implementation is not lineage extraction at all. You attach the producing job's name, version and batch to the record, using the decoration patterns from §05: a metadata header on a message, a column or companion table on a table. Chain it, and each hop can carry its parent's lineage, so a single suspect row tells you the whole path it took.

Watch out. Custom code is a black box. Plan analysis works beautifully for native SQL functions and not at all for a user-defined function or a programmatic mapping — the framework sees an output and cannot interpret the logic. Expect gaps precisely where your most complicated transformations are. Row-level lineage has no visualisation ecosystem. Dataset and column lineage are well served by tools; row-level is not, so you will be building your own query layer over it. That is fine — it is a debugging aid, not a dashboard. Transformations evolve. The logic that built a column last year is not the logic that builds it today, so lineage without versioning will confidently tell you something that used to be true.

11.Streaming Data — seven patterns for unbounded input

API Gateway · Zero-ETL Synchronizer · Hybrid Source · Sidecar · Partial State Writer · Streaming Reprocessor · Batch Reprocessor

Batch pipelines measure their latency in tens of minutes at best, which rules them out of anything genuinely real-time. But latency is the smaller of the two differences. The bigger one is the dataset: a batch job gets a complete dataset — bounded — while a streaming job gets small increments forever and never learns when the data ends — unbounded. Most of the awkwardness in this section traces back to that.

Everything here reuses the earlier families. Streaming ingestion is §02 with different timing; reprocessing is §04 under harder conditions; the Sidecar is literally §06 applied to sources and sinks. The patterns that follow are the streaming-specific parts on top.

Streaming ingestion

API Gateway

Use it when application developers are writing directly to your broker, and both sides are suffering for it.

We needed a near-real-time parcel-journey view urgently, so we created a topic and asked the backend team to publish scan events straight to it. Month one was fine. Then broker maintenance made the topic unreachable and events were simply dropped, because nobody had designed a failure path. Then a backend release started emitting malformed payloads and the data quality problem became ours. Both incidents have the same root cause: the producers are coupled to our broker.

The pattern inserts a service between them, and to be worth its existence it needs five properties:

Connectivity — producers must be able to reach it with no special client, which in practice means ordinary HTTP or gRPC rather than a broker-specific protocol.

Fault tolerance — when the broker is unavailable, the gateway must handle it invisibly. Two options: a dead-letter store with a replay pipeline, or a local buffer the gateway itself drains once the connection returns. Same external behaviour; the difference is who does the re-delivery.

Scalability — a traffic spike must scale the gateway, not throttle it, or the gateway becomes a user-facing bottleneck.

Availability — deploys must not disturb producers. A stable name in front of a load balancer buys that, and lets both layers evolve independently.

Validation — the gateway is the boundary of your data network, so it is where you enforce both the schema (the shape) and the semantics (the business rules). Rejecting a bad record at the door beats discovering it in Gold.

Watch out. The gateway is not the place for all business logic. Semantic validation belongs there; transformation mostly does not. Concrete example: downsampling events from every ten seconds to every minute at the gateway looks like a helpful efficiency, and it permanently removes the option for any consumer that needed the raw resolution. New security surface. This is a public-facing service, so it inherits application-security concerns the rest of your platform does not have — denial-of-service protection being the obvious one, and largely a solved problem on managed offerings. Producers must still be resilient. The gateway has fault tolerance; it does not have 100% uptime. Producers should buffer locally too, or you have moved the single point of failure rather than removed it.

Semantic validation and shifting left

Technical validation checks the shape: types, required fields, nesting. Semantic validation checks the meaning: a weight cannot be negative, an event time cannot be in the future, a scan type must be one of a known set.

Shifting left is the practice of moving both as close to the source as possible. Validate once at the gateway and every downstream consumer inherits the guarantee; validate in each consumer and you get the same rule implemented five times, drifting apart.

Zero-ETL Synchronizer

Use it when a job's entire purpose is moving data from a broker to the lakehouse, unchanged.

After a reorg the platform team is a quarter of its previous size and has been asked to reduce what it maintains. The most obvious candidates are the streaming ingestion jobs whose only function is taking events out of a topic and writing them into the lakehouse. They contain no business logic and they have to be deployed, monitored and upgraded like everything else.

The best change to a codebase is subtraction, and this pattern is subtraction: no pipeline at all. Vendors offer managed versions today, with support for the major table formats varying by vendor, and open-source work is underway to convert closed broker segments into columnar files directly. Whichever route, the shape is the same — you write no code, and configure four things: the output location, the permissions the service needs, the catalog to register schema and table changes in, and throughput or sizing controls.

Watch out. Zero ETL is not zero effort. The result is still a lakehouse table, and it still needs compaction, partition tuning, monitoring and housekeeping. You have removed pipeline code, not operational responsibility. Zero transform means exactly that. If anything needs changing — a field masked, a timestamp normalised — it can only happen after the records land, which is the Transformation Replicator from §02. Treat the synchronised table as raw. Transforming it in place is a one-way door with no easy restore; write the transformed version to a new table instead. Do not lean on time travel as your immutability guarantee — retention and compaction policies both bound how far back it reaches.

Data value in a stream

Hybrid Source

Use it when a streaming job must be primed with history before it starts running live.

The parcel-journey streaming job is written, tested and ready. One thing blocks release: it needs to be initialised with historical scans from the lakehouse, and only then switch to the real-time topic. It has to do both, in order, without processing anything twice.

Start by listing every historical source and — the part that actually matters — the boundaries of each: first and last position to read. Two failures come from getting this wrong. Getting stuck in history, if a "historical" source is still receiving data, so its end never arrives. And interleaving, where the same records are read from both sides: if the lakehouse table is fed by a Zero-ETL Synchronizer from the very topic you are about to stream, then without explicit boundaries you will process everything twice.

Any source type works as history so long as you can close its boundaries — including other topics, using the readers' explicit start and end positions. Implement it as a single job that chains its readers (some engines provide a hybrid-source abstraction that does exactly this), or as multiple jobs where a historical job runs to completion before the real-time job starts, coordinated by the orchestrator. Either way, make retries cheap: checkpoints, or an orchestrator that restarts only the failed step.

Watch out. Schema consistency. History and live data often differ slightly. Convert both to one internal representation in a dedicated step, rather than sprinkling conditionals through your transformation logic. Overlap means duplicates, which means you need §04. Do not load history in one gulp. Reading years of data in one pass invites out-of-memory failures and hammers whatever you are writing to. Treat the historical source as a stream too — an "available now" style trigger reads everything currently present while still respecting your throughput limits, so it behaves like a streaming job and checkpoints like one. Data filters make bad boundaries. "History is parcels 1 to 1000" does not terminate if that source is still live, because new records for those parcels keep arriving. Bound on technical positions — offsets, ingestion time — not on business predicates. Being stuck in the past. If the live stream is 10,000 records a minute and you have 100,000 historical records per minute to get through, the job never catches up on steady-state hardware. Split it: a well-resourced initialisation phase, then a normal-sized steady state.

Sidecar

Use it when one streaming job must consume several sources, or feed several sinks.

The parcel-journey job needs to combine two streams and publish its results to two different stores. The name is borrowed from the container-orchestration pattern where two complementary services share a lifecycle while living in separate places.

For inputs, do not process the sources separately — combine them into one logical stream with a union, and transform once. That requires harmonising schemas first. Skip that step and your transformation fills up with source-dependent conditionals, one per column, and becomes unreadable and untestable.

For outputs, apply the shared transformation once and write the result to each destination, possibly in different shapes — the detailed rows to an object store, an aggregate to a warehouse.

The pattern is a streaming specialisation of §06's fan-in and fan-out, and which variant you are using is decided by your consistency requirements: all inputs must have data → Aligned Fan-In; not necessarily → Unaligned Fan-In; all writes must succeed → Parallel Split; some may fail → Exclusive Choice on the write outcome. The difference from the generic patterns is scope: the Sidecar only ever concerns sources and sinks, whereas fan-in and fan-out apply to any task.

Watch out. Schema consistency is the first cost, and it is code you would not otherwise write. Consistency versus performance on the write side. If throughput matters more than simplicity, write to the fastest store from the streaming job and let separate jobs propagate to the slower ones — the object store that wants large files, the key-value store with lower throughput. That keeps the streaming job fast and gives you two more jobs to deploy and monitor. Data consistency across heterogeneous sinks is genuinely unsolved. Two different stores means no shared transaction, so partial success is possible and you cannot roll it back. Options today: use a change feed to propagate from one store to another, so there is one authoritative write; or watch the multi-table transaction work progressing in the major table formats, which will eventually cover the multi-table case within one format.

A useful side-trick: the heartbeat source

Some micro-batch engines skip an iteration entirely when the input has no new data — which is efficient, and means any per-batch work you attached (emitting metrics, expiring state, running an audit) also does not happen during a quiet period.

The Sidecar's input side gives you a fix. Union your real source with a tiny synthetic source that produces one row per interval, filtered down to nothing, purely so a batch always exists. The job then ticks along regardless of traffic, and your per-batch logic keeps running.

Partial State Writer

Use it when consumers cannot wait for a long-running aggregation to finish.

Parcel journeys run two to four hours. The exceptions team wants to use them to spot problem parcels, and waiting four hours defeats the purpose — they want the journey as it stands, updated on every new scan. Modern stateful engines support incremental emission out of the box; what you have to decide is when.

There is no default worth having. Emitting an updated journey on every scan is reasonable, because each scan is meaningful. Emitting an updated counter on every increment is usually not, and every tenth or twentieth is fine. Then pick how consumers distinguish in-flight from finished — conventionally a boolean such as is_final, and it needs to be part of the published contract rather than a column you happen to have.

Watch out. Partial is not final, and consumers will forget. The fraud-and-exceptions case makes the stakes clear: act on a partial journey and you flag a parcel that was about to be scanned as delivered. Publishing the state is not enough — publishing its completeness is the actual requirement. Throughput multiplies. If the target keeps one row per entity, every partial emission becomes a merge, and merges are the expensive write. As a consumer of a frequently-updated state you can choose to sample it rather than absorb every version. Writing semantics change. Final-only output can be an append; partial output usually cannot, because each version replaces the last — so MERGE, with everything that implies. Latency and ownership. Emission crosses a network and a storage layer, so there is irreducible delay. If your use case cannot tolerate it, the answer is not a faster writer — it is computing the state inside the latency-sensitive application, where you also control the logic instead of negotiating for changes to somebody else's job.

Reprocessing: the part nobody plans for

Streaming Reprocessor

Use it when you have to redo work and the source data is still there.

Yesterday's release of the journey job accidentally removed the check that closes inactive journeys, and today's numbers are visibly wrong. The bug is fixed; now everything since the release must be reprocessed. The topic still holds the data, so this is the easy case — you rewind.

First find where to start from, either from the source (offsets or timestamps per partition for a log; a timestamp or version for a table) or from the framework's checkpoint metadata, which already records exactly this. Then start the job from that position, in one of three ways: pass the position in, alongside a fresh checkpoint location so the old progress files are not picked up; promote the desired checkpoint by removing the invalid newer ones; or restore explicitly from a chosen checkpoint, which invalidates the later ones for you. The third is by far the safest and requires framework support.

Watch out. Finding the start is easy or awful, depending on the source. An append-only log gives you a numeric position or an ingestion time. An object store gives you last-modified times and file names — workable if names are ordered, guesswork if not. Prefer the checkpoint layer wherever it exists. Duplicates. Reprocessing means processing again, which means writing again; idempotency is not optional here. All or nothing. Rewinding replays everything from that point, so fixing one tenant's data means either custom filters or the Batch Reprocessor below. A fresh checkpoint has no memory. Starting clean discards accumulated state, so a stateful job must rebuild it — possibly by reading the old state and seeding the new job with it. Deleting checkpoint files is a loaded gun. One mistake corrupts the job's state; back them up first, automate the procedure, and expect that many production environments forbid file deletion outright, which rules the approach out. Retention caps how far back you can go — a two-week retention means a three-week-old bug cannot be fixed this way.

Make reprocessing part of your release process

If your engine supports explicit checkpoints, the cheapest insurance available is a two-step deploy: checkpoint the running job and stop it, then start the new version, resuming from that checkpoint. Cost: one extra step. Benefit: every release has a named, known-good restore point, so recovering from a code regression is "resume from the pre-release checkpoint" rather than an archaeological dig through checkpoint directories at 2am.

Why retention limits are becoming less of a wall

Tiered storage lets a broker keep recent data on fast local disks and older data in cheap remote storage with a much longer retention. That decouples "how far back can I replay" from "how much local disk can I afford", which used to be the same question.

Diskless topics, under active discussion in the streaming community and already shipping in several vendor platforms, go further: producers write directly to object storage, which removes replication and cross-zone transfer costs from the equation entirely.

Neither changes the patterns. Both change how often the Streaming Reprocessor is available to you rather than the Batch one.

Batch Reprocessor

Use it when the source data has aged out of the stream, or you only need to fix a subset.

A different bug took three weeks to surface. The topic's retention is fourteen days, so rewinding is impossible. Fortunately the raw events were also copied to the lakehouse with six months' retention — which is exactly why copying raw streaming data to at-rest storage is worth doing even when nothing needs it today.

The prerequisite is code organisation. If your transformation logic is interleaved with reading from a topic and writing to a topic, none of it is reusable against a table. Extract the transformations — filters, groupings, mappings — into their own module with no knowledge of I/O, and both jobs can share them. That single refactor is most of the work.

Then mark the output so consumers can tell reprocessed rows from live ones, using the Wrapper (an origin attribute in the payload) or the Metadata Decorator (the same thing, in metadata, invisible unless asked for). Finally decide how it runs: provisioned by a CI/CD pipeline that creates the stack, runs it and tears it down; from the orchestrator, like any other batch workload; or both, with CI/CD building the infrastructure and triggering a pipeline that lives in the orchestrator. There is no best answer — pick whichever tooling your team already operates well.

Watch out. Shared code is not identical code. The job was designed for streaming and now has to serve two masters. Software design patterns earn their keep here: a factory to build the right reader and writer per environment, an adapter or decorator to reshape the historical schema into what the transformation expects, a template to share all the logic while letting each mode annotate its output differently. State recovery is the hard part, and it splits cleanly in two.

If your state is event-time windows, you are fine. To reprocess 10:00–11:00 you read 10:00–11:00: the windows are self-contained. If your state is arbitrary — sessions — you are not. A journey overlapping 10:00–11:00 may have started at 09:50, or 08:50, or the day before, and reprocessing without it produces a truncated journey that looks like a real one. Three ways out, in increasing cost and correctness: increase state retention in the streaming job or back the state up periodically (easy, and you never know how far back you will be asked to go); accept approximate results, restoring only the requested range or a fixed number of adjacent partitions (cheap, honest, and it must be disclosed); or reconstruct the state fully from adjacent partitions, walking outwards until the session rule says a boundary has been reached.

Two things worth internalising beyond the trade-offs

Exactly-once is a composite, not a feature. Notice how often it came up and how often the answer was "not on its own". A Checkpointer gives you at-least-once. A Windowed Deduplicator cleans your input, not your output. A Transactional Writer is idempotent inside one transaction and not across a backfill. A FIFO Orderer guarantees order and says nothing about duplicates. Exactly-once is what you get when you compose a delivery guarantee with an idempotency pattern — and any product page claiming otherwise is describing one of the halves.

Snowball backfilling is the tax on every correctness fix. It appeared in the Dead-Letter replay, in both Late Data Integrators, in the Merger's backfill inconsistency, in the Sessionizer's forward dependency, and in the Batch Reprocessor's state reconstruction. The pattern is always the same: you fix data in a partition your consumers already read, so they must reprocess, and so must theirs. There is no technical solution to it. There is only knowing who your consumers are — which is why the lineage patterns in §10 are not optional infrastructure hygiene, they are what makes the rest of this catalogue safe to use.

Almost every pattern here is a way of deciding what you are willing to be wrong about, and for how long.

∎The takeaway

You will not use all seventy-seven of these. Nobody does. What a catalogue like this actually changes is the speed of recognition: the moment a colleague says "the re-run doubled the table" you are no longer designing from scratch, you are choosing between five idempotency patterns whose costs you already know. That is the whole return on reading it.

Two things are worth carrying away even if you forget every name. First, the hard problems in data engineering are not about code, they are about time — retries, replays, late arrivals, schema drift, and who found out when. Every family in this catalogue is a different answer to a question about time. Second, almost every pattern is an explicit decision about what you will be wrong about. A deduplication window says which duplicates you accept. A lookback window says which late data you abandon. A denormalised table says which updates you will pay for. Making those choices deliberately, and writing them down where consumers can see them, is most of what separates a platform people trust from one they work around.

Come back to this page when something breaks. Find the shape, take the name, then go and read the two paragraphs under Watch out — that is where the version of this material that would have saved you a week is hiding. ∎
