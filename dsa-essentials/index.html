Not a computer-science course wearing a data-engineering label. 01.Where the DSA bar really sits

Three role tiers, two ways to fail, and the one comparison that beats fifty LeetCode Hards

There is a persistent claim that you need 200–500 solved problems and software-engineering-level algorithmic fluency to get a data engineering job at Apple, Amazon, Meta or Netflix. It is not true, and believing it costs people months. But the correction is not "DSA doesn't matter" — skip it entirely and you fail the technical screen. So the useful question is not how much, it is which, and the answer depends almost entirely on the role, not the company logo.

How to tell which tier a job posting is in

Read the posting for what the team builds, not what it uses. A team that uses Spark, Airflow, Snowflake and dbt to move and model data is tier one — easy-to-medium DSA, medium-to-hard SQL. A team that builds a query engine, a table format, a stream processor or the internal platform those tools run on is tier three, and the coding round will look like a software-engineering coding round because it is one.

Concrete tier-three signals: the words query engine, Trino, table format, Delta/Iceberg internals, Kafka/Kinesis internals, data infrastructure, SWE–Data, or a JD that lists a systems language alongside Python. Tier-one signals: pipelines, ETL/ELT, data modelling, warehouse, orchestration, data quality, stakeholder.

The two failures, and they are mirror images

Interview enough data engineering candidates and the same two profiles keep arriving. Neither is a weak engineer. Both prepared hard — for one half of the interview.

The diagnostic, in one question each

Ask yourself both. One: given a 2 GB log file, can you write a single-pass Python function that returns the top three users by total latency, and state its time and memory cost without hesitating? Two: can you explain, out loud, why a nested loop join and a hash join produce the same rows at wildly different cost, and which one your engine picked last time you looked at a query plan? If either answer is shaky, that is your next week of prep — and it is not on LeetCode.

Why the same structures mean different things to us

Here is the part that reframes everything. Software engineers and data engineers use the same dozen data structures, but they occupy a completely different position in the work. For a backend engineer, the algorithm is the product: navigation needs a real shortest-path implementation, rate limiting needs a real sliding window, search ranking needs a real sort. Those are shipped features.

For a data engineer, the algorithm is almost never the product — it is the explanation of the engine you are operating. You do not implement hashing; you choose a partition key, and hashing is why that choice determines your skew. You do not implement a queue; you configure a stream, and queueing is why back-pressure behaves as it does. You do not implement a DAG traversal; you write an Airflow DAG, and topological ordering is why your task started when it did.

When you are processing tens of terabytes, nobody cares whether you can code Dijkstra's algorithm. They care whether you know that a nested loop join will flatten the cluster while a hash join finishes in ten minutes.

The one comparison worth more than fifty Hards

If you internalise a single thing from this page, make it this diagram. It is the complexity question a data engineer is actually asked, in the form it is actually asked in — not "what is the Big-O of this loop" but "why did this job get slow, and what would you change."

What this section buys you

A defensible answer to "how much DSA should I do?" — enough to cover tiers 1 and 2 of this page, at easy-to-medium difficulty, and no further unless the role is platform or infra. Everything after this is that syllabus, one topic at a time, with the pipeline reason attached so it stays in memory as engineering rather than trivia.

02.Big-O intuition, the only version you need

You will never be asked to derive a complexity. You will constantly be asked to recognise one

Big-O has a reputation as the most academic corner of interview preparation, and for a data engineer that reputation is exactly backwards. You do not need limits, you do not need the master theorem, and you will not be asked to prove anything. You need one skill: look at code you just wrote and name its class. That is it. Because the moment you can do that, you can also predict which of your pipelines is going to fall over when the input triples — which is the actual job.

There are five classes worth recognising, and each one has a concrete data-engineering counterpart. Learn them as pairs — the curve and the operation — and the abstraction disappears.

ClassWhat it meansIn your pipelineRecognise it by
O(1)cost does not grow with inputa dict/set lookup, a broadcast-side probe, reading a partition's metadatano loop over the data

O(n²)every row against every rowa nested loop join, a cross join, a lookup inside a row loopa loop inside a loop over the same data

The two rules that cover every case you will meet

Drop the constants. Three passes over a file is still O(n), because when the input grows a thousandfold, three passes and one pass both grow a thousandfold. Keep the dominant term. A sort followed by a scan is O(n log n) + O(n), which is O(n log n) — the scan is noise next to the sort.

Both rules describe growth, not speed. A well-written O(n²) routine can beat a sloppy O(n) one on a thousand rows. Big-O tells you who wins as the data grows, and in data engineering the data always grows. Say the memory cost too — "O(n) time and O(distinct keys) memory" is a complete answer; naming only time invites the follow-up you did not prepare.

The code, walked line by line

Two versions of one question — does any id repeat in this batch? — plus a function that counts what the slow one really does. This is the smallest possible demonstration of the single most valuable trade in applied algorithms: spending memory to buy time.

 2

 4  formulas, but looking at code you just wrote and naming its class.
 5  """

 8  def dupes_nested(ids):
 9      """Does any id repeat?  n(n-1)/2 comparisons -> O(n^2) time, O(1) space."""
10      for i in range(len(ids)):
11          for j in range(i + 1, len(ids)):
12              if ids[i] == ids[j]:

14      return False

19      seen = set()
20      for x in ids:
21          if x in seen:

23          seen.add(x)
24      return False

Three ways Big-O bites a data engineer specifically

Hidden linear operations. x in some_list, list.pop(0), list.insert(0, x), del list[0] and string concatenation in a loop are all O(n). Put any of them inside a loop over your rows and you have written O(n²) without typing a nested loop.

Per-row work that is not per-row. Compiling a regex, opening a connection, or reading a small config inside the row loop. The complexity class is unchanged but the constant is enormous, and constants are what actually show up in your runtime graph.

Memory is the cost that kills distributed jobs. Time degrades gracefully; memory does not. An O(n)-memory routine that fits on your laptop's sample will spill to disk, then OOM, on the real partition. Always quote both numbers, and always ask which one is bounded by the data and which by the number of distinct keys — that second one is usually much smaller, and saying so is a strong signal.

03.Arrays, strings and parsing a log file

Contiguous memory, then the two pointer techniques and the two binary searches that live on top of it

A Python list is a contiguous run of memory. That one physical fact explains every cost it has, and it is worth holding in your head literally rather than as a table of complexities to recite. Because the slots sit next to each other at a known width, the machine can compute the address of any element instead of hunting for it — and because they sit next to each other, making room in the middle means physically moving everything after it.

Why append is "amortised" O(1) rather than plain O(1)

A list keeps spare capacity past its last element. Most appends drop straight into a spare slot — genuinely constant. Occasionally the spare space runs out, so Python allocates a bigger block and copies everything across, which is O(n) for that one call. Because the block grows proportionally rather than by a fixed amount, those copies get rarer as the list grows, and the cost averaged over all appends is constant.

Practical consequence: building a list of ten million rows with append in a loop is fine. Building it with insert(0, row) is not, and the difference is about five orders of magnitude, not a few percent.

Parsing a log file — the most common DE coding question there is

If you get one live-coding question in a data engineering interview, there is a good chance it is some variant of "here is a log file, parse it, aggregate it, and give me a metric." It looks unglamorous next to a graph problem, which is exactly why candidates who only practised LeetCode fumble it. It tests whether you can work with real, messy, string-shaped input, keep the per-line work constant, and state your costs.

The trap in this question

The failure mode is almost never the parsing. It is doing something O(n) inside the per-line loop — appending to a list and then searching it, re-compiling a regex, or sorting the accumulated results on every iteration. Keep the per-line work O(1), do any sorting once at the end, and the whole routine stays linear. If you are asked for the top three users, that is a heapq.nlargest(3, …) at the end (§11), not a sort inside the loop.

Two pointers, shape one: the squeeze

Two pointers comes in exactly two shapes. The first starts at opposite ends and walks inward. It works because on a sorted input, each comparison eliminates an entire candidate permanently — so the pointers can only move toward each other, and the whole thing is one linear pass with no extra memory.

 1  """Two pointers, the opposite-ends shape: find a pair that hits a target.
 2  
 3  Precondition: the input is SORTED. Say that out loud before you write a line.
 4  """
 5  
 6  
 7  def pair_summing_to(sizes, target):
 8      """Two file sizes that fill a batch exactly -> O(n) time, O(1) space."""
 9      left, right = 0, len(sizes) - 1
10      while left < right:
11          total = sizes[left] + sizes[right]
12          if total == target:
13              return sizes[left], sizes[right]
14          if total > target:
15              right -= 1          # too big: the largest candidate cannot be used
16          else:
17              left += 1           # too small: the smallest candidate cannot be
18      return None
19  
20  
21  def reverse_in_place(chars):
22      """The same squeeze, with a swap -> n/2 swaps, no second array."""
23      left, right = 0, len(chars) - 1
24      while left < right:
25          chars[left], chars[right] = chars[right], chars[left]
26          left += 1
27          right -= 1
28      return chars

33      print(pair_summing_to(parts, 105))
34      print(pair_summing_to(parts, 1000))
35      print(''.join(reverse_in_place(list('partition'))))

Shape two: same direction

The other shape has both pointers moving forward, one reading ahead and one lagging behind to commit results — a read pointer and a write pointer. It is how you compact a list in place, de-duplicate a sorted run without allocating, or filter rows without a second array. You have already seen its cousin: the sliding window in §12 is the same two-forward-pointers idea with a running aggregate attached, and the merge in §06 is it across two inputs at once.

Binary search, and why there are two of them

Binary search deserves emphasis out of proportion to its length, because a surprising number of problems reduce to it once you see the monotone structure. The version everyone is taught looks for an exact value. The version you will actually use looks for a boundary — the first element satisfying some condition — and that one shows up constantly in data work: the first offset at or after a watermark, the first partition containing data newer than a checkpoint, the first row past a cutoff.

 1  """Binary search, both forms.
 2  
 3  The equality form is the one taught; the predicate form is the one you use.
 4  Both need the input sorted, and both turn a billion rows into thirty probes.
 5  """
 6  
 7  
 8  def find(offsets, target):
 9      """Classic: return the index of target, or -1 -> O(log n)."""
10      lo, hi = 0, len(offsets) - 1
11      while lo <= hi:
12          mid = (lo + hi) // 2
13          if offsets[mid] == target:
14              return mid
15          if offsets[mid] < target:
16              lo = mid + 1        # target is right of mid
17          else:
18              hi = mid - 1        # target is left of mid
19      return -1
20  
21  
22  def first_at_or_after(offsets, watermark):
23      """Predicate form: the first index whose value is >= watermark.
24  
25      Works when the test is MONOTONE — once it turns True it stays True.
26      Returns len(offsets) when nothing qualifies.
27      """
28      lo, hi = 0, len(offsets) - 1
29      best = len(offsets)
30      while lo <= hi:
31          mid = (lo + hi) // 2
32          if offsets[mid] >= watermark:
33              best = mid          # a candidate: remember it, then look left
34              hi = mid - 1
35          else:
36              lo = mid + 1
37      return best

40  if __name__ == '__main__':

42      print('find(28)         ->', find(offsets, 28))

44      print('first >= 25      ->', first_at_or_after(offsets, 25))

In real code this is bisect.bisect_left(offsets, watermark), which returns exactly what first_at_or_after returns. 04.Linked lists — the structure behind the structures

Not something you will write in production, but the model behind call stacks, tree nodes, and pagination tokens

You will almost never hand-roll a linked list in production data engineering Python — a list covers contiguous storage and collections.deque covers cheap-at-both-ends, and §03 and §07 already made the case for each. So why give it a section at all? Because a linked list is not really a data structure you use so much as a shape you will meet again and again wearing other names: a tree node with a left and right instead of one next is a linked list with two pointers (§09). A call stack frame pointing at the frame that called it is a linked list you did not write (§08). A paginated API response with a next_token field is a linked list where the pointer is a network call instead of a memory address. Learn the shape once, here, where it is stripped down to one pointer, and it stops being a surprise everywhere else.

The other reason it earns a slot: reversing one is the single most common "warm-up" question attached to a bigger interview, precisely because it is small enough to finish in five minutes and still exposes whether you can hold three moving pointers in your head without tangling them.

The classic: reversing the chain

Three pointers, one pass, no extra memory. prev trails behind, curr is the node being processed, and a third temporary — call it nxt — exists for exactly one reason: the moment you overwrite curr.next, you lose the only reference to the rest of the chain unless you saved it first. Every bug in this function is really the same bug: doing those four lines in the wrong order.

 1  """Linked lists: pointer-chasing instead of index arithmetic."""
 2
 3
 4  class Node:
 5      __slots__ = ('value', 'next')
 6
 7      def __init__(self, value, next=None):
 8          self.value = value
 9          self.next = next

11
12  def build(values):
13      """Build a singly linked list from an iterable -> O(n)."""
14      head = None
15      tail = None
16      for v in values:
17          node = Node(v)
18          if head is None:
19              head = node
20          else:
21              tail.next = node
22          tail = node
23      return head
24
25
26  def to_list(head):
27      """Walk the chain into a Python list, for printing -> O(n)."""
28      out = []
29      node = head
30      while node is not None:
31          out.append(node.value)
32          node = node.next
33      return out
34
35
36  def reverse(head):
37      """Reverse the chain in place, three pointers, one pass -> O(n) time, O(1) memory."""
38      prev = None
39      curr = head
40      while curr is not None:
41          nxt = curr.next       # save before we overwrite it
42          curr.next = prev      # flip this node's pointer backwards
43          prev = curr           # advance prev
44          curr = nxt            # advance curr
45      return prev                # prev is the new head
46
47
48  if __name__ == '__main__':
49      head = build([7, 3, 9, 4])
50      print('built       :', to_list(head))
51      new_head = reverse(head)
52      print('reversed    :', to_list(new_head))
53      print('old head now:', to_list(head))

The other kind: doubly linked

Give every node a second pointer, prev, and you get exactly one new capability: given a reference to any node — not the head, not an index, a node you are already holding because you just visited it — you can remove it in O(1), with no walk at all. A singly linked list cannot do this, because curr.next = prev requires already knowing prev, and the only way to know it in a singly linked list is to have walked from the head counting as you go, which is O(n). That single capability is the entire reason a second pointer ever earns its keep.

 1  """Doubly linked lists: the second pointer buys O(1) removal, given a reference."""
 2
 3
 4  class DoublyNode:
 5      __slots__ = ('value', 'prev', 'next')
 6
 7      def __init__(self, value, prev=None, next=None):
 8          self.value = value
 9          self.prev = prev
10          self.next = next
11
12
13  def build_doubly(values):
14      """Build a doubly linked list from an iterable -> O(n)."""
15      head = None
16      tail = None
17      for v in values:
18          node = DoublyNode(v, prev=tail)
19          if head is None:
20              head = node
21          else:
22              tail.next = node
23          tail = node
24      return head, tail
25
26
27  def to_list_doubly(head):
28      """Walk forward into a Python list, for printing -> O(n)."""
29      out = []
30      node = head
31      while node is not None:
32          out.append(node.value)
33          node = node.next
34      return out
35
36
37  def remove(node):
38      """Splice a node out given only a reference to it -> O(1), no walk."""
39      if node.prev is not None:
40          node.prev.next = node.next
41      if node.next is not None:
42          node.next.prev = node.prev
43      node.prev = None
44      node.next = None
45
46
47  if __name__ == '__main__':
48      head, tail = build_doubly([7, 3, 9, 4])
49      print('built   :', to_list_doubly(head))
50      target = head.next.next          # the node holding 9, found however you like
51      remove(target)
52      print('removed :', target.value, '-> now', to_list_doubly(head))

Array, deque, or linked list — in one line each

Need index k fast? Array (§03) — O(1). Need both ends fast, order preserved? deque (§07) — already a doubly-linked list under the hood, with the bookkeeping done for you. Need to hold a reference to one node and splice around it in O(1), never touching the rest? That is the doubly linked list above — the one job a real linked list still wins, and rare enough in Python that you should recognise it rather than expect to meet it.

Where this shape shows up without the name

A collision chain in a hash bucket — §05's orders and events sharing bucket b3 — is a tiny linked list. A paginated API (next_token, next_page_url) is a linked list where dereferencing a pointer costs a network round trip instead of a memory fetch, and "keep paging until the token is empty" is to_list() above with curr.next replaced by an HTTP call. And every stack frame in §08 points at the frame that called it — you have been walking a linked list backwards every time you have read a traceback.

05.Hashmaps and sets — the data engineer's workhorse

Counting, grouping, de-duplicating, and the hash join that all three explain

If you only had time to prepare one topic on this page, prepare this one. Interviewers reach for hashmap problems constantly for data engineering roles, and the reason is not laziness — it is that a hashmap is the mechanism behind the operations we spend our days running. Every GROUP BY, every COUNT DISTINCT, every hash join, and every partitioning decision is a hash table wearing different clothes.

The idea is one step away from §03. An array is fast because it computes an address from an index. A hash table asks: what if we could compute an address from the value? Then "is this key present?" stops being a search and becomes arithmetic — compute where the key would live, and look only there.

What "O(1) average" is actually promising

A good hash function is deterministic (the same key always gives the same bucket, or you could never find anything again), fast (it runs on every single operation), and well spread (clustering is what turns constant time back into linear time). With a decent function and a load factor kept under control — CPython resizes at about two-thirds full — the number of keys per bucket stays a small constant, so lookup is O(1) on average.

The worst case is genuinely O(n): if every key collided you would be scanning one long chain, which is just a list again. You will not see it by accident in everyday code — but adversarial or heavily skewed keys are the distributed version of this problem, and it has a name you already know: data skew. One hot key means one bucket, means one partition, means one task doing all the work while 199 sit idle.

Routine one: counting

Any question phrased as "how many times", "which is most common", or "do these two things agree" is a counting problem, and counting is one pass with one constant-time update per row. No sorting required, which is the part people get wrong.

Four ways hash structures bite

Unhashable keys. d[[1, 2]] = 'x' raises TypeError: unhashable type: 'list'. Only immutable objects can be keys, because a mutated key's hash would change and the value would be lost in a bucket nobody looks in. Use a tuple — tuple(sorted(parts)), not sorted(parts). This bites whenever a composite key or a sorted signature becomes a dict key.

No ordered queries, at all. A hashmap cannot tell you its smallest key, its keys in a range, or what comes next — the hash function deliberately destroys ordering. Those questions need a heap (§11) or a tree (§09). Asking a dict for a range is a design error, not a slow query.

Memory is larger than you think. A set of a million integers is far bigger than a list of a million integers, because the table keeps empty slots on purpose to stay fast. On a cluster this is the difference between an in-memory hash join and one that spills to disk.

Skew is the distributed worst case. A single hot key — NULL, 'unknown', a default tenant id — sends a disproportionate share of rows to one bucket and therefore one task. Your O(1) average is intact and your job still hangs. Naming this when asked "what could go wrong with your hash join?" is a strong answer.

06.Sorting, merging, and the other join

Why every sort is O(n log n), what a merge really does, the join that falls out of it for free — and the sorting algorithms themselves, traced and coded

You will not be asked to implement quicksort. You will be asked, in some form, why sorting costs what it costs — because sorting is the hidden price on a large share of what a data platform does. ORDER BY, DISTINCT, window functions, a sort-merge join, and most shuffles all pay it. So the first half of this section is one picture that makes O(n log n) obvious and then the merge routine, which is the piece genuinely worth being able to write. The second half is the reference: the rest of the shelf — seven algorithms, each with a trace, the code, and the property that decides when it is the right one.

Why merge sort, specifically, and not the rest of the shelf

Quicksort (average case), heapsort and merge sort all reach the O(n log n) lower bound that any comparison sort is provably stuck with; insertion sort, selection sort and bubble sort are O(n²) and exist mainly to show what quadratic looks like in practice. So no interviewer is testing whether you have all six implementations memorised. Merge sort is the one worth being able to write for a concrete reason: it is the only one of the O(n log n) family that is also how sorting works once data no longer fits in memory on one machine. A big ORDER BY that spills to disk, and a Spark sort shuffle merging partitions from many executors, are both doing exactly the "merge two sorted runs" routine below, at a larger scale. Quicksort and heapsort optimise for a single machine with everything in RAM — a real concern for a systems engineer, and not the shape of a data platform's sorting problem.

If you are targeting a Data Platform or SWE–Data role (§01's tier-three signals), the in-memory family is back in scope, and quicksort's average-case O(n log n) with worst-case O(n²) — the input-order dependence that a random pivot exists to defeat — is worth knowing cold. The rest of the shelf is written out at the end of this section, one family at a time, for exactly that reading.

Why O(n log n) is a floor, not an accident

Any sort that works by comparing pairs cannot beat O(n log n) — there are n! possible orderings and each comparison splits the possibilities roughly in half, so you need about log₂(n!) ≈ n log n comparisons to pin down one. Quicksort, merge sort, heapsort and Timsort all land on the same bound because they are all comparison sorts.

Sorts that beat it do not compare — they exploit the shape of the keys. Counting sort and radix sort are O(n + k) and O(n × digits). This is not trivia: a bucketed or range-partitioned table is a radix sort you already paid for, which is why a join or an aggregation on the clustering key can skip the sort entirely. That connection is a genuinely strong interview answer, and the counting sort at the end of this section takes the mechanism apart.

What Python actually runs

sorted() and list.sort() use Timsort — a merge sort that first detects runs already in order and merges those. On data that is partly sorted already, which describes most real data, it approaches O(n). It is also stable, and the next section is about why that one word saves you a daily chore. Memory: sorted() allocates a new list, list.sort() sorts in place, and Timsort needs O(n) scratch space in the worst case — which is why a large sort is the thing that spills to disk.

Four words that do all the distinguishing

Stable — rows with equal keys keep their input order. It is the only one of the four with a daily use: it is what makes "latest row per key" two lines above, and what lets two sorts compose (ORDER BY on one key, then another) instead of the second destroying the first. "Is quicksort stable?" is not a memory test — it follows from the fact that a partition swap moves values across the array, which can jump one equal key past another.

What to actually memorize

Of the seven, three are worth being able to write from memory: insertion sort (the one you're actually handed on a whiteboard), merge sort (the one that explains ORDER BY, external sorts, and the sort-merge join), and counting sort (the one that explains why a partitioned table skips the sort). For the other four — bubble, selection, quicksort, heapsort — a data engineering interview only needs the diagram, the complexity, and the one property that names it. Being able to say "quicksort partitions around a pivot, average O(n log n), worst O(n²) on already-sorted input with a bad pivot" is worth more than code you are unlikely to be asked to produce for this role. The exception is a Data Platform or SWE–Data track (§01's tier-three signals) — there, quicksort's partition is worth writing too.

The quadratic three: bubble, selection, insertion

These are what people mean when they say "the sorting algorithms", and they are the three you will never ship. They are still worth twenty minutes. They are the clearest available picture of what O(n²) costs; one of them runs inside sorted() on every call; and when an interviewer does ask for a sort on a whiteboard, it is almost always one of these — what is being checked is whether you handle loop boundaries and the swap cleanly, not whether you know something clever.

Bubble and selection sort — concept only, no code to memorize

Bubble sort repeatedly swaps adjacent out-of-order neighbours, which drags the largest remaining value to the right end each pass; stable, O(n²), and its one trick is an early exit — a pass with no swaps proves the array is sorted, the only reason it is ever O(n). Selection sort scans the unsorted tail for the smallest value and swaps it into place; not stable, always O(n²) with no early exit to add, but it does exactly n−1 swaps, its one virtue when a write costs far more than a read. Neither ships in real code — they exist to make quadratic cost visible, and the diagram above is the whole of what is worth carrying from them.

Where insertion sort actually runs

As the callout at the top of this section says, sorted() and list.sort() begin by scanning for runs that are already in order. When a run is shorter than a threshold computed from n — between 32 and 64 items — they do not recurse: they extend that run with insertion sort, because at that size its tiny constant factor beats a merge. So the answer to "is insertion sort ever useful?" is that it runs inside every call to sorted() you have ever made, and it is a large part of why sorted() is near-linear on the partly-ordered data most pipelines produce.

Divide and conquer: merge sort and quicksort

Two algorithms, one idea: split the problem roughly in half, solve both halves the same way, combine. What separates them is when the work happens. Merge sort splits blindly down the middle and does everything on the way back up, in the merge routine from earlier in this section. Quicksort does the work on the way down, in the partition, and its combine step is nothing at all — once a value is on the correct side of the pivot, and the pivot is in its final slot, there is nothing left to do.

That single difference explains every other difference between them. Merge sort needs somewhere to merge into, so it allocates O(n) and can stream from disk; quicksort rearranges the array it was given, so it allocates nothing and cannot. Merge sort's split is data-independent, so its cost is O(n log n) on every input; quicksort's split depends on the pivot, so a bad pivot costs it everything.

Quicksort — concept only, no code to memorize for a DE interview

The diagram above is the whole mechanism: swap a random pivot to the end, walk a pointer across the rest, and swap anything smaller below a boundary that advances with it — one pass, and the pivot lands in its final position with nothing left to do. Recurse on each side and there is no combine step at all, which is what makes it in place.

The bad case is not exotic: take the last element as a fixed pivot, hand it an already sorted array, and every partition splits off one item instead of half — n levels of O(n) work, so O(n²) on the most likely input in the world. Three defences, in increasing order of how much they impress: a random pivot (makes a bad split unlikely rather than impossible), median-of-three (sample first, middle and last, pivot on the middle value — what most C libraries do), and recurse into the smaller side, loop on the larger, which does not fix the time but caps the recursion depth at O(log n). The production answer is that nobody ships plain quicksort: std::sort and numpy.sort's default are introsort — quicksort that switches to heapsort once its recursion gets too deep, and to insertion sort on small partitions, so the worst case becomes O(n log n) by construction. Naming introsort is a strong signal precisely because it shows you know what each of the three algorithms was brought in to supply. Targeting a Data Platform or SWE–Data track instead (§01's tier-three signals)? Write the partition — it is also the mechanism behind quickselect and "top K", below.

The partition also answers "top K"

Run one partition and you know where the pivot belongs. If that index is past the k you want, recurse into the left side only; if it is before, recurse into the right. That is quickselect, and because each step discards a fraction of the array it is O(n) on average rather than O(n log n) — the k-th smallest value without sorting anything. It needs the whole array in memory and rearranges it, so for a stream, or for "top 10 by latency" over a file you are reading once, the min-heap in §11 is the better answer. Both are worth having: the heap for streams, quickselect for an array you already hold.

Heapsort: no bad case, no extra memory

Heapsort is the algorithm that gives up nothing on paper. O(n log n) on every input, like merge sort. O(1) extra memory and in place, like quicksort. No recursion, so no stack to blow. It is also the one you are least likely to find inside a standard library, and the reason why is a genuinely useful story about what a complexity class leaves out.

The structure it runs on is the heap from §11 — a binary tree stored in a flat array, where the children of index i sit at 2i+1 and 2i+2. The sort is two phases: arrange the array into a max-heap, then repeatedly take the root (the largest value left), swap it to the back, and repair the heap that remains. The sorted region grows from the right, and it never needs a second array.

Heapsort — concept only, no code to memorize

Two phases, both in place. First, arrange the array into a max-heap: starting from the last node with a child and working backwards, sift each node down until it is larger than both children — cheap at the bottom where there are many nodes, expensive at the top where there is one, and the sum comes out to O(n), not O(n log n). Second, repeatedly swap the root (the largest value left) to the back of the unsorted region and sift the new root down to repair the heap — n iterations of O(log n), so the sorted region grows from the right while the live heap shrinks from the left, sharing one array. Not stable: a swap can carry the root past an equal value anywhere in the heap. heapq.heapify plus repeated heappop is the same idea in three lines, at the cost of O(n) for a second list.

Then why does nothing ship it?

Because O(n log n) counts comparisons and hardware charges for memory access. Heapsort jumps from index i to 2i+1, so on a large array almost every step is a cache miss, while merge sort and quicksort walk memory in order and get the cache line they are about to need for free. That constant factor is large enough that Timsort and introsort beat heapsort in practice on nearly every real input — and both are stable or in place in ways heapsort is not. It survives as introsort's safety net, and in code that must not allocate or must hold a guaranteed bound: real-time systems, kernels, embedded. This is the cleanest example on the page of a case where the complexity class does not decide the winner.

Counting sort: beating O(n log n) by not comparing

The O(n log n) floor applies to algorithms that decide by comparing pairs of values. Nothing forces a sort to do that. If the key itself can be used as an address — a small integer — you can sort with no comparisons at all, in O(n + k), where k is the size of the key space. Counting sort is that idea in its clearest form, and it is the one member of this family worth being able to write.

It is also the family a data engineer should be quickest to recognise, because you have probably paid for one already without writing any code. A table bucketed or partitioned on a key was sorted into ordered ranges at write time, which is why an engine can skip the shuffle and the sort for a join or a window function on that key. Naming that connection is worth more in an interview than any implementation.

 1  """Sorting without comparing — and the pipeline idea hiding inside it.
 2  
 3  Every sort above compares pairs of values, which is why none of them can beat
 4  O(n log n). This one never compares: it uses the key itself as an address. The
 5  price is that the key has to be a small integer — which is exactly what a
 6  status code, an hour, or a day-of-week column already is.
 7  """
 8  
 9  EVENTS = [(14, 'a'), (9, 'b'), (14, 'c'), (11, 'd'), (9, 'e'), (14, 'f')]
10  
11  
12  def counting_sort_by(rows, key, k):
13      """Rows whose key is an int in [0, k). O(n + k) time and memory, stable —
14      and no comparison between two rows happens anywhere in here."""
15      counts = [0] * k
16      for row in rows:
17          counts[key(row)] += 1                 # how many rows carry each key
18      starts = [0] * k
19      total = 0
20      for v in range(k):
21          starts[v] = total                     # where key v's block begins
22          total += counts[v]                    # -> a prefix sum over counts
23      out = [None] * len(rows)
24      for row in rows:                          # left to right, so the first
25          v = key(row)                          # row holding a key stays first
26          out[starts[v]] = row                  # -> the sort is stable
27          starts[v] += 1
28      return out

33      print('by hour  :', by_hour)
34      print('hour 14  :', [e for h, e in by_hour if h == 14],
35            '<- a, c, f: arrival order kept, so it is stable')
36      print('matches  :', by_hour == sorted(EVENTS, key=lambda r: r[0]),
37            '- same answer as a stable comparison sort, without comparing')

The answer that lands, when they ask how you would sort it

"A comparison sort cannot beat O(n log n), so if we need better we have to stop comparing. When the key is a small integer — a status code, an hour, a day of week — counting sort is O(n + k) and stable, because the key can be used as an index instead. But the version I meet most often is not code I write at all: a table bucketed or partitioned on the sort key was already put into ordered ranges at write time, so the engine can skip the shuffle and the sort entirely. Radix and bucket sort are named in the O(n log n) floor box above and left there, because the half of them that matters to a data engineer is the data-modelling point — a partitioned table is a sort you already paid for — rather than the code. 07.Stacks and queues

Last-in-first-out, first-in-first-out, and why collections.deque exists

Stacks and queues are the easiest topic on this page and they still earn their place, for two reasons. First, they are the containers that make DFS and BFS in §09 differ by a single line — understand them here and that section is nearly free. Second, the queue hides one of the most common silent performance bugs in Python data code, and it is the kind of thing an interviewer will spot in your solution without saying anything.

Neither is a new structure. A stack is a list where you only touch one end. A queue is a list where you touch both — and that is the whole problem.

The classic: matching brackets

The standard stack problem, and it has an honest data-engineering face: validating nested structure. Checking that a JSON payload's brackets balance, or that a generated SQL string's parentheses close, is this routine. The insight is that a stack is the right shape because the most recently opened bracket is always the one that must close next — which is the definition of last-in-first-out.

 1  """Stacks, queues, and the one-line mistake that makes a loop quadratic."""
 2  from collections import deque
 3  
 4  PAIRS = {')': '(', ']': '[', '}': '{'}
 5  
 6  
 7  def is_balanced(text):
 8      """Valid nesting? -> O(n) time, O(depth) memory."""
 9      stack = []
10      for ch in text:
11          if ch in '([{':
12              stack.append(ch)
13          elif ch in PAIRS:
14              if not stack or stack.pop() != PAIRS[ch]:
15                  return False    # a closer with nothing, or the wrong opener
16      return not stack            # anything still open means invalid
17  
18  
19  def drain_as_queue(items):
20      """FIFO done right: deque.popleft() is O(1)."""
21      q = deque(items)
22      order = []
23      while q:
24          order.append(q.popleft())
25      return order
26  
27  
28  def drain_as_list(items):
29      """FIFO done wrong: list.pop(0) shifts everything, so this is O(n^2)."""
30      q = list(items)
31      order = []
32      while q:
33          order.append(q.pop(0))
34      return order
35  
36  
37  if __name__ == '__main__':

39      for value in (7, 3, 9):
40          stack.append(value)
41      before = list(stack)            # snapshot: pop() mutates in place
42      top = stack.pop()
43      print('stack      :', before, '-> pop() returned', top, '-> now', stack)
44      for text in ('{[()]}', '{"a":[1,{"b":2}]}', '{[)]}', '{['):
45          print(f'{text:<20} balanced: {is_balanced(text)}')
46      print('queue order:', drain_as_queue('ABCDE'))
47      print('same answer:', drain_as_list('ABCDE') == drain_as_queue('ABCDE'))

Which container, in one line each

Stack → a plain list with append and pop. Nothing else needed; do not import anything. Queue → collections.deque with append and popleft. Both ends → also deque, which is O(1) at either end and is the general answer. Priority order rather than arrival order → that is a heap, and it is §11.

A deque is a doubly-linked list of fixed-size blocks, which is why both ends are cheap and why d[i] in the middle is O(n) — the opposite trade to a list. Use a deque for ends, a list for random access. And note queue.Queue is a different thing entirely: a thread-safe queue with locking overhead, for concurrency rather than algorithms.

Where these two show up in the platform you already run

A queue is the shape of every streaming system you touch: a Kafka partition is an append-at-the-back, read-from-the-front log, and consumer lag is literally the distance between the two pointers. Back-pressure is what a bounded queue does when the producer outruns the consumer. A stack is the shape of recursion itself — §08 makes that explicit, and §09's DFS keeps one on the call stack for you. RecursionError is Python telling you the interpreter's own stack overflowed.

08.Recursion — the call stack, made explicit

A function calling itself is just §07's stack, with the interpreter doing the pushing and popping for you

§07 ended with a promise: a stack is the shape of recursion itself. Here is the payoff. Every time a function calls itself, Python pushes a new frame onto its own call stack — arguments, local variables, and the address to resume at — exactly like append did to your list in §07. When that call hits a base case and returns, Python pops the frame and resumes the caller with the result — exactly like pop. You do not write the push or the pop. You write the base case and the recursive case, and the interpreter operates the stack for you.

This matters for a data engineer for a narrower reason than it matters for a software engineer: you will rarely write a recursive algorithm from scratch, but you will constantly read and debug code that walks nested structure recursively — a nested JSON payload, a directory tree, an org hierarchy, a Spark logical plan. §09's tree and graph traversals are exactly this recursion with a specific data structure attached. Understanding recursion here, on the plainest possible example, is what makes that section nearly free.

 1  """Recursion: the interpreter's call stack, made visible."""
 2
 3  import sys
 4
 5
 6  def total(nums):
 7      """Sum a list recursively -> O(n) time, O(n) stack frames."""
 8      if not nums:                          # base case: stop recursing
 9          return 0
10      return nums[0] + total(nums[1:])      # do less work, then trust the answer
11
12
13  def total_iterative(nums):
14      """Same answer, no call stack growth -> O(n) time, O(1) extra memory."""
15      running = 0
16      for n in nums:
17          running += n
18      return running
19
20
21  if __name__ == '__main__':
22      values = [7, 3, 9, 4]
23      print('recursive  :', total(values))
24      print('iterative  :', total_iterative(values))
25      print('match      :', total(values) == total_iterative(values))
26      print('stack limit:', sys.getrecursionlimit())

Recursive, or iterative — in one line each

The structure is naturally nested (a tree, a JSON document, a directory) → recursion usually reads cleaner, because the code's shape matches the data's shape. The structure is a flat sequence and depth could be large (a log file, a big list) → iterate, or recurse with an explicit stack, so a pathological input cannot exhaust the call stack. When in doubt, write the recursive version to think it through, then decide whether the input can ever be deep enough to matter.

Where this shows up in the platform you already run

§09's DFS is this pattern with a tree node instead of a list slice, and its three traversals are three ways of choosing when to look at nums[0]. §10's dynamic programming is this same recursion with one change: before recursing, check whether this exact call has been answered already — the diagram there is the naive version of this section's left panel, except the calls overlap instead of forming a clean line, which is precisely the waste memoisation removes. And RecursionError, whenever you meet it, is Python reporting that the left panel above grew past a thousand rows.

09.Trees, graphs and what makes a DAG a DAG

The three traversals, DFS versus BFS, and the acyclic property your scheduler depends on

This is the section with the most direct payoff, because a data engineer already works with a graph every single day and calls it something else. Your Airflow DAG is a graph. Your dbt lineage is a graph. Your table dependencies are a graph. Everything in this section is the vocabulary for things you already operate — you just may not have connected the two.

The bar here is modest and specific: know what a tree and a graph are, understand DFS and BFS, solve simple problems with both, know the three traversal orders, and know what a DAG is. No balancing, no red-black trees, no advanced graph algorithms.

Tree, graph, DAG — the three definitions, in order

A graph is nodes plus edges, and that is all. A tree is a graph with no cycles where every node has exactly one parent. A DAG — directed acyclic graph — relaxes the one-parent rule but keeps the no-cycles one: edges have a direction, and you can never follow them back to where you started.

That middle relaxation is why your pipeline is a DAG and not a tree: aggregate legitimately depends on both clean and build_dim, so it has two parents. And "acyclic" is not jargon — it is precisely the property that lets a valid running order exist at all, which §13 makes concrete.

The three traversals

There are three ways to walk a tree depth-first, and they differ only in when you visit the node relative to its children. That is genuinely the whole distinction — the same recursion with one line moved.

The BST caveat worth knowing

A BST's O(log n) lookup assumes it is balanced. Insert already-sorted data into a naive BST and every node becomes a right child — you have built a linked list with extra steps, and lookup degrades to O(n). Real databases solve this with self-balancing structures (a B-tree is the disk-friendly version, which is what your index actually is). You will not be asked to implement one; you may well be asked why an index on a monotonically increasing key behaves differently, and this is the answer.

DFS and BFS are one algorithm

Here is the most economical fact in this section. Depth-first and breadth-first search are the same six lines. The only difference is which end of the container you take from — and if you have read §07, you already know both containers.

 1  """DFS and BFS — the same six lines with a different container."""
 2  from collections import deque
 3  
 4  GRAPH = {'A': ['B', 'C'], 'B': ['D'], 'C': ['D'], 'D': ['E'], 'E': []}
 5  
 6  
 7  def dfs(graph, start):
 8      """A STACK, so the most recent node is taken first -> dives deep."""
 9      seen = {start}
10      box = [start]
11      order = []
12      while box:
13          node = box.pop()            # the only line that differs
14          order.append(node)
15          for nxt in graph[node]:
16              if nxt not in seen:
17                  seen.add(nxt)
18                  box.append(nxt)
19      return order
20  
21  
22  def bfs(graph, start):
23      """A QUEUE, so the oldest node is taken first -> sweeps level by level."""
24      seen = {start}
25      box = deque([start])
26      order = []
27      while box:
28          node = box.popleft()        # the only line that differs
29          order.append(node)
30          for nxt in graph[node]:
31              if nxt not in seen:
32                  seen.add(nxt)
33                  box.append(nxt)
34      return order
35  
36  
37  def shortest_hops(graph, start):
38      """BFS also gives the fewest-hops distance, for free, in an UNWEIGHTED graph."""
39      dist = {start: 0}
40      box = deque([start])
41      while box:
42          node = box.popleft()
43          for nxt in graph[node]:
44              if nxt not in dist:
45                  dist[nxt] = dist[node] + 1
46                  box.append(nxt)
47      return dist

50  if __name__ == '__main__':

52      print('bfs  :', bfs(GRAPH, 'A'))
53      print('hops :', shortest_hops(GRAPH, 'A'))

What breaks a workflow

Cycle detection is DFS with one extra colour

If you are asked to detect a cycle directly, the trick is that seen is not enough — revisiting a finished node is fine, revisiting a node that is still on the current path is a cycle. So you track three states instead of two: unvisited, in progress (on the stack right now), and done. Hitting an in-progress node means you have closed a loop. That is the "white/grey/black" colouring, and it is the most advanced graph idea this page asks of you. §13 gets the same answer a second way, by counting what a topological sort fails to emit.

10.Basic 1-D dynamic programming

Two problems, one idea: stop recomputing what you already worked out

Dynamic programming is the topic that causes the most disproportionate anxiety, so let me be exact about the requirement. For a data engineering interview you need very basic one-dimensional DP — enough that if a simple DP problem appears you recognise it and are not thrown. Two canonical examples cover it: the staircase and coin change. DP on trees, DP on grids and bitmask DP are firmly in §14's overkill list.

And the idea itself is small. DP is §08's recursion plus a note-taking habit. That is all. If a recursive solution keeps solving the same subproblem, write the answer down the first time and look it up afterwards.

The two signals that a problem is DP

Overlapping subproblems — a naive recursion solves the same smaller case more than once. Optimal substructure — the best answer for n can be built from the best answers to smaller inputs. If both hold, cache the subproblems and you are done. If only the second holds, you have a plain recursion; if neither, DP is the wrong tool.

Two names for the same thing, and interviewers use both. Memoisation is top-down: keep the recursion, add a cache. Tabulation is bottom-up: throw away the recursion and fill an array in an order that guarantees every cell you read is already finished. Same recurrence, same answer, same complexity — tabulation just avoids Python's recursion limit and is usually easier to reason about memory for.

Coin change, filled cell by cell

The second canonical problem, and the one that makes tabulation's discipline visible: a cell may only read cells that are already finished. Get the fill order right and the whole thing is two nested loops with no recursion at all.

 1  """The 1-D dynamic programming you need: enough not to panic, and no more."""
 2  import math
 3  
 4  
 5  def stairs_naive(n):
 6      """Ways to climb n stairs in 1 or 2 steps -> O(2^n). Correct, unusable."""
 7      if n <= 1:
 8          return 1
 9      return stairs_naive(n - 1) + stairs_naive(n - 2)
10  
11  
12  def stairs_memo(n, cache=None):
13      """The same recursion with a cache -> O(n) time, O(n) memory."""
14      cache = {} if cache is None else cache
15      if n <= 1:
16          return 1
17      if n not in cache:
18          cache[n] = stairs_memo(n - 1, cache) + stairs_memo(n - 2, cache)
19      return cache[n]
20  
21  
22  def stairs_table(n):
23      """Bottom-up, two variables -> O(n) time, O(1) memory."""
24      prev, cur = 1, 1
25      for _ in range(n - 1):
26          prev, cur = cur, prev + cur
27      return cur
28  
29  
30  def coin_change(coins, amount):
31      """Fewest coins making `amount`, or -1 -> O(amount * len(coins))."""
32      dp = [0] + [math.inf] * amount
33      for a in range(1, amount + 1):
34          for coin in coins:
35              if coin <= a:               # only read a cell already finished
36                  dp[a] = min(dp[a], 1 + dp[a - coin])
37      return -1 if dp[amount] is math.inf else dp[amount]

45      print('naive  :', naive)
46      print('memo   :', memo)

Where a greedy guess goes wrong

The tempting shortcut on coin change is to take the largest coin that fits, repeatedly. It works for real currency and it is wrong in general. With coins of 1, 3 and 4 making amount 6: greedy takes 4, then needs 2, so 4+1+1 — three coins. The DP finds 3+3 — two coins. The reason DP is needed at all is that a locally optimal choice can foreclose the globally optimal one. If you offer greedy, say which assumption it needs.

Honestly: how much does this matter for a DE?

Less than any other section on this page, and that is the point of including it at exactly this depth. DP almost never appears in a data engineering pipeline — running totals, sessionisation and gaps-and-islands are window functions, not DP. The value of this section is insurance: if a simple DP question shows up in a screen, you recognise the overlap, write the recurrence, and fill a row. Practise the staircase and coin change until they are boring, then stop and go back to §05.

11.Heaps and priority queues

Top-K without sorting, latest-N events, and merging more streams than fit in memory

Tier two starts here. Everything from §02 to §10 is the floor; this and the next two sections are what you add once that floor is solid, and what a product-company or platform-leaning screen is more likely to reach for.

A heap answers one question very cheaply: what is the smallest thing here? It cannot tell you the second smallest without work, it cannot give you a sorted list, and it cannot do range queries. That narrowness is the trade — in exchange you get the minimum in O(1) and insert-or-remove in O(log n), which is exactly what "top K of a stream" needs.

The mistake this diagram exists to prevent

A heap is not sorted. Read the array left to right and you get 2, 5, 7, 9, 6, 8 — note the 6 after the 9. Only the root is guaranteed to be the minimum. Printing a heap and treating the output as ordered is the classic error, and the file prints exactly this array after heapify to make the point concrete. If you want it sorted, that is sorted(heap), and it costs O(n log n) like any other sort.

Top-K, and the inversion that trips everyone

The pattern: you have a stream of n values and you want the K largest, where K is small and n may not fit in memory. The obvious answer is to sort and slice — O(n log n) time and O(n) memory. The heap answer is O(n log K) time and O(K) memory, and when n is a billion and K is ten, that difference is the difference between a job and no job.

The counter-intuitive part is which heap to use. To keep the K largest, you use a MIN-heap. Read that twice. The reasoning: the heap holds your current best K, and the only value you ever need to evict is the worst one you are keeping — so you want cheap access to the smallest of your survivors, which is what a min-heap gives you.

 1  """Top-K with a heap — the pattern behind every "worst N queries" dashboard."""
 2  import heapq
 3  
 4  LATENCIES = [7, 2, 9, 4, 11, 1, 8]
 5  
 6  
 7  def top_k(values, k):
 8      """The k LARGEST, using a MIN-heap of size k.
 9  
10      Min-heap, not max: the smallest survivor sits at the root, which is exactly
11      the one you want to evict cheaply. O(n log k) time, O(k) memory.
12      """
13      heap = []
14      for v in values:
15          heapq.heappush(heap, v)
16          if len(heap) > k:
17              heapq.heappop(heap)     # drop the smallest kept so far
18      return sorted(heap, reverse=True)
19  
20  
21  def top_k_by_sort(values, k):
22      """The same answer for O(n log n) time and O(n) memory."""
23      return sorted(values, reverse=True)[:k]
24  
25  
26  def stream_merge(*runs):
27      """Merge any number of sorted runs with a k-way heap — O(N log k)."""
28      return list(heapq.merge(*runs))
29  
30  
31  if __name__ == '__main__':

33      print('sort    :', top_k_by_sort(LATENCIES, 3))
34      print('agree   :', top_k(LATENCIES, 3) == top_k_by_sort(LATENCIES, 3))
35      print('nlargest:', heapq.nlargest(3, LATENCIES))
36      print('merged  :', stream_merge([2, 9, 27], [4, 10, 55], [1, 88]))
37      heap = [2, 5, 7, 9, 6, 8]

"Worst 10 queries by runtime" on any observability dashboard — top-K over a stream. External merge sort — the k-way merge above, and the reason a large ORDER BY can complete at all. Event-time processing — a heap keyed on timestamp is how a stream processor holds slightly-out-of-order events until a watermark passes. Task scheduling — a priority queue is what picks the next job when tasks have priorities rather than just dependencies (dependencies alone are §13).

12.Sliding window and prefix sums

The two techniques that turn a quadratic subrange scan into a single pass

Both techniques in this section solve the same class of problem — something about a contiguous run of rows — and both work by refusing to recompute what they already know. They are the natural next step after §03's two pointers, and if you write SQL you have used both without naming them: a sliding window is a ROWS BETWEEN frame, and a prefix sum is a running-total column.

Two window shapes, and how to tell which you have

Variable width — the question asks for the longest or shortest run satisfying a condition. The width is the answer, so it has to flex: grow on the right, shrink from the left when the condition breaks. Fixed width — the question gives you k ("the best 3-day total"). The width never changes, so each step adds one value and drops one, and there is no inner loop at all.

The fixed-width version is the more useful one for data work, because it is exactly a rolling metric: a 7-day moving average, a rolling error rate, an hourly count over a 5-minute slide. Adding one and dropping one is O(1) per step regardless of how wide the window is — which is why a rolling 30-day sum costs the same as a rolling 3-day sum.

Prefix sums: pay once, then query for free

A sliding window handles a window that moves. A prefix sum handles the harder case: arbitrary ranges, asked in any order, many times. You pay one linear pass up front to build a table of running totals, and afterwards every range sum is a single subtraction — no matter how wide the range.

 4  def longest_within_budget(sizes, budget):
 5      """Longest run of batches whose total stays within budget -> O(n).
 6  
 7      The left edge only ever moves forward, so although there are two loops
 8      every index enters the window once and leaves once.

15              lo += 1

17      return best

Which of the two to reach for

One window that moves in order → sliding window. O(1) memory, works on a stream you cannot rewind, and it is the only option if the data does not fit. Many arbitrary ranges, asked out of order → prefix sums. O(n) memory and one pass up front, then unlimited O(1) queries. If a question asks about a single moving window, a prefix table is over-engineering; if it asks a thousand random range queries, a sliding window cannot answer them at all.

Three caveats worth naming

The window technique needs a monotone condition. Shrinking from the left must make the condition more satisfiable. It does for sums of non-negative numbers, counts, and distinct-element limits. Introduce negative values and "sum ≤ budget" stops being monotone — dropping a negative from the left makes the total bigger, the shrink loop no longer makes progress, and the technique is simply invalid. That is when you need a prefix sum with a sorted structure instead.

Prefix sums only invert operations that have an inverse. Sum works because subtraction undoes addition. Count and XOR work. Max and min do not — you cannot "un-max" a value, so there is no prefix-max trick, and range maximum needs a different structure entirely (which is in §14's overkill list for good reason).

Floats drift. Building a prefix table over a few million floats accumulates rounding error, so a range near the end of the array can differ from the direct sum in the low digits. For money, use integers (minor units) or Decimal — this is a real reconciliation bug, not a theoretical one.

13.Topological sort and shortest paths

Kahn's algorithm, cycle detection for free, and exactly where BFS stops being enough

Of everything in tier two, this is the section with the most direct claim on your attention, because topological sort is not an interview algorithm you might need — it is the algorithm your orchestrator executes on your behalf every night. Airflow deciding which task can start, dbt working out model build order, a package manager resolving dependencies, a query planner ordering operators: all the same routine. Understanding it converts a black box you operate into a mechanism you can reason about, and that is worth more than the interview points.

The question it answers: given tasks with dependencies, produce an order in which every task runs only after everything it depends on has finished. Kahn's method is almost embarrassingly direct — repeatedly take a task with nothing left upstream, run it, and release its downstream tasks.

In-degree is the only idea here

The in-degree of a task is the number of edges pointing at it — how many upstream tasks it is still waiting on. A task is runnable exactly when its in-degree hits zero. Running it decrements the in-degree of everything downstream, which may release more tasks. That is the whole algorithm, and it is why the ready set is a queue rather than a search: you never need to look for work, work is handed to you as dependencies clear.

There is usually more than one valid order — clean and build_dim could run in either sequence, or in parallel. A topological sort produces a valid order, not the valid order, and the fact that several exist is precisely what lets your scheduler run independent tasks concurrently. Whether the ready set is a queue, a stack, or a priority queue is what decides which valid order you get.

 2  
 3  Kahn's method: repeatedly take a task with nothing left upstream, emit it, and
 4  release its downstream tasks. If the ready queue empties before every task has
 5  been emitted, the leftovers can never start.
 6  """

 8  
 9  DEPS = {'extract': ['clean', 'build_dim'],
10          'clean': ['aggregate'],
11          'build_dim': ['aggregate'],
12          'aggregate': ['publish', 'quality'],
13          'publish': [],
14          'quality': []}

17  def topo_order(deps):
18      """Emit as many tasks as dependency order allows -> O(V + E)."""
19      indeg = {task: 0 for task in deps}
20      for task in deps:
21          for downstream in deps[task]:
22              indeg[downstream] += 1
23  
24      ready = deque(t for t in deps if indeg[t] == 0)
25      order = []
26      while ready:
27          task = ready.popleft()
28          order.append(task)
29          for downstream in deps[task]:
30              indeg[downstream] -= 1
31              if indeg[downstream] == 0:
32                  ready.append(downstream)
33      return order

37      """A valid run order for the whole graph, or None if one cannot exist."""
38      order = topo_order(deps)

44  
45      Note this is a superset of the cycle itself — 'quality' below is not in the
46      loop, but it can never run because everything upstream of it is stuck.
47      """
48      return set(deps) - set(topo_order(deps))

Shortest paths: where BFS runs out

§09 showed that BFS gives the shortest path in an unweighted graph, free, because it expands strictly in order of distance. That covers what a data engineering interview is likely to ask. The thing genuinely worth knowing is the boundary — the point at which BFS silently stops being correct.

The answer to give, and where to stop

"If the edges are unweighted, BFS gives the shortest path and I'd use that — it's O(V + E). Once edges carry a cost, BFS is wrong rather than slow, and you need Dijkstra, which is BFS with a priority queue instead of a plain queue. And Dijkstra assumes non-negative weights; with negative edges you're into Bellman-Ford." That is the complete answer, it takes fifteen seconds, and it demonstrates the thing that actually matters — knowing which tool is valid when. Implementing Dijkstra is not required for a classic DE role, and negative-weight algorithms are in §14's skip list.

Two things a topological sort gets you at work

Impact analysis. "If this source table is late, what breaks?" is a traversal forward from that node; "what must succeed before this dashboard is trustworthy?" is a traversal backward. Both are §09's DFS over the lineage graph you already have. Parallelism. Because a topological sort produces levels of mutually independent tasks — everything whose in-degree hits zero at the same step — the size of each level is the maximum useful parallelism at that point in the run. That is a concrete answer to "why does our DAG not go faster when we add workers": the graph's shape, not the cluster, is the constraint.

14.The overkill list — and why skipping it is a decision, not a gap

Five topics that look impressive on a study plan and repay almost nothing in a DE interview

A syllabus is defined as much by what it excludes as by what it contains, and this is the section most likely to actually change how you spend next month. Everything below is real computer science, genuinely useful in the right role, and a poor investment for 80–90% of data engineering interviews. Skipping it should feel like a decision you made, not a hole you are anxious about.

The reasoning is the same in every case: these topics are hard enough to need weeks, appear rarely enough to be a lottery ticket, and displace preparation on SQL, modelling and system design, where the return is close to certain.

The five, with the reason for each

Hardcore dynamic programming — on trees, on grids, with bitmasks

Skip it · §10 already covers the DP you need

Multi-dimensional DP is the single biggest time sink in interview preparation. Grid DP needs you to reason about two moving indices at once, tree DP needs DP and recursion held simultaneously, and bitmask DP needs you to think of an integer as a set. Each is weeks of practice to become reliable at.

Meanwhile the DP that shows up in a classic DE screen is one-dimensional and shallow — the staircase, coin change, maybe a maximum-subarray. And the DP-shaped problems in the actual job are not DP at all: running totals, sessionisation and gaps-and-islands are window functions in SQL, solved declaratively.

When it does matter: a SWE–Data or platform role at a company with a shared coding bar. If that is your target, §01's tier-three signals apply and this becomes in scope.

Advanced graph algorithms — max flow, min cut, Bellman-Ford, MST

Skip it · §09 and §13 cover the graph work data engineering does

The graph algorithms a data engineer genuinely uses are traversal (DFS, BFS) and ordering (topological sort). Those cover lineage, impact analysis, dependency resolution and scheduling — the entire realistic surface. Max flow and min cut solve network-capacity problems that essentially never arise in a pipeline; minimum spanning trees solve a problem you do not have.

Bellman-Ford is the one with a plausible-sounding case, and it still is not worth it: it exists to handle negative edge weights, and your DAG's edges are dependencies, which have no weight at all. Knowing the boundary — that Dijkstra needs non-negative weights and Bellman-Ford is what lies past it — is the complete requirement, and §13 gives you that in one sentence.

What to say instead: name the boundary. "Unweighted — BFS. Weighted non-negative — Dijkstra. Negative — Bellman-Ford, and I haven't needed it." That reads as judgement, not as a gap.

Contest-style problems that turn on a mathematical insight

Skip it · these test a different skill entirely

The problems where the solution depends on spotting a number-theory identity, a clever bit trick, or a combinatorial reformulation — the kind where you either see it in the first two minutes or you do not. Competitive programmers train specifically for this and they are genuinely good at it.

It is close to uncorrelated with data engineering ability. A DE coding round is testing whether you can take a messy real-world requirement, pick a sane structure, write clear code, and reason about its cost at scale. Those are the opposite skill: practical reasoning under ambiguity, not insight under time pressure.

The tell: if a problem's solution is one line and the explanation starts "notice that…", it is this category. Pattern-matching more of them does not generalise.

Specialised structures — segment trees, Fenwick trees, tries, union-find

Mostly skip · union-find is the one with a real use

Segment trees and Fenwick (binary indexed) trees answer range queries with updates — the case §12's prefix sums cannot handle, since a prefix table must be rebuilt when a value changes. That is a real gap and it is also a real non-problem for us: our data is append-mostly and our range aggregations are a warehouse's job.

Tries are for prefix search, which is a search-engine concern rather than a pipeline one. Union-find (disjoint set union) is the closest to earning a place — it is how you group connected records, which is exactly entity resolution and record deduplication across sources. If you do identity-resolution work, learn it. Otherwise it is tier-three.

Proportionality: these four are each a day to understand and a week to become fluent in. Spend the week on window functions.

Implementing the structures from scratch

Skip it · know the costs and the trade-offs, use the library

Writing your own hash table, balanced BST or heap from scratch is a teaching exercise, not an interview requirement — §04 covers a linked list because it is the mental model behind the call stack and behind tree nodes, not because you will build one to spec. Nobody will ask a data engineer to implement a red-black tree, and if they do, that interview is a mismatch worth noticing.

What you are asked is one level up: which structure, why, and what it costs. Use dict, set, collections.deque, heapq and bisect, and be able to explain what each is doing underneath — which is what §05, §07, §11 and §03 respectively gave you.

The exception: the small routines are fair game and worth being fluent in — the hash join in §05, the merge and one sort of your choosing in §06, BFS in §09, Kahn's sort in §13. Fifteen lines each, and each one explains a piece of your platform.

How to handle one of these if it appears anyway

Occasionally you will draw a question from this list. Do not pretend. Say what you can see — the brute-force solution and its cost, the structure of the problem, what you would look up — then write the brute force and reason about it properly. A clean O(n²) solution with an accurate complexity analysis and a stated idea of what the better approach would need is a pass in most DE loops. Silence, or a half-remembered optimal algorithm you cannot defend, is not. The interview is testing reasoning, and reasoning is exactly what you still have access to.
